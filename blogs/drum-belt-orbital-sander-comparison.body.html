The Three Machines, in Plain English

Drum sanders are the blunt instrument: abrasive wrapped on a rigid, fast-spinning drum, cutting hard exactly where the drum touches. Enormous material removal, zero forgiveness — hesitate for one second and the drum digs a hollow you’ll see forever; a wave in your walking rhythm becomes a wave in the floor, called chatter. This is the machine rental shops carry. Belt sanders are the professional standard for the main cut: a continuous abrasive belt over a drive system, usually with better drive control and dust pickup. The action is smoother and more even than a drum’s, tracking is self-correcting, and stop marks are far harder to make. It still removes wood fast — it’s just governable. Orbital and planetary sanders cut with rotating pads instead of a spinning cylinder: slow removal, almost impossible to gouge. A rental orbital can’t realistically strip a worn floor — people run out of patience two hours into the first room — but as a finishing action, multi-head planetary machines are superb, which brings us to the TRIO.

Why Rental Drum Sanders Wreck Floors

The physics is unforgiving arithmetic: a drum spinning at full speed removes wood continuously, so the only way to sand flat is to keep the machine moving at a perfectly steady rate — starting the drum already in motion, never pausing, feathering every pass into the last. Professionals build that rhythm over hundreds of floors. A first-timer meets it on their own living room, and the tuition is permanent: a moment’s pause at the wall while checking the cord, and there’s a hollow; an uneven walking pace, and the floor ripples with chatter that only appears once finish and low light arrive. We wrote the longer case in why refinishing isn’t a DIY project, and it’s not gatekeeping — it’s that the rental counter hands out the least forgiving machine in the trade to the least practiced operators, every weekend. When a rental-sander floor comes to us afterward, the fix is sanding past the damage, which spends wear thickness the floor can never get back.

Where the TRIO Fits: the Pass That Makes Floors Flat

After the belt sander has done the heavy lifting and the edger has handled the perimeter, the floor is stripped — but it isn’t yet flat in the way finish will reveal. Belt and edger leave slightly different scratch patterns, and their boundary can telegraph as a subtle halo around the room once sheen goes down. That’s the job of the Lägler TRIO, the planetary machine we run as a final pass: three counter-rotating discs under a heavy machine that ride the floor’s true plane, erasing directional scratches and unifying field and edge into one surface. It’s slow, it does nothing dramatic, and it’s the difference between a floor that looks refinished and a floor that looks new. It’s also a genuine tell when comparing contractors — a crew that owns a planetary finishing machine has invested in the boring last ten percent, which is where refinishing quality actually lives.

The Sequence We Actually Run — and Where the Dust Goes

A typical floor gets a grit ladder, coarse to fine — 36 to start if the floor carries old finish or deep wear, stepping through the grits to 100 — with each pass existing to erase the scratches of the pass before. Skipping a grit saves an hour and leaves scratches the finish will magnify forever; it’s one of the invisible corners a low bid can cut. Floor Sanders: What Everett Homeowners Ask Us

What's the actual difference between a drum sander and a belt sander?
The abrasive mounting, which changes everything. A drum sander wraps a sheet of sandpaper around a rigid spinning drum — huge cutting force, zero forgiveness. A belt sander runs a continuous abrasive loop over rollers, which cuts more evenly, tracks straighter, and is far harder to gouge a floor with. Pros run belts; rental shops stock drums.
Can I refinish my own floor with a rented orbital sander?
You're unlikely to gouge anything — orbitals are nearly foolproof — but you're also unlikely to finish. Orbital removal is so slow that stripping a genuinely worn floor takes many times longer than renters expect, and old finish clogs pad after pad. They're fine for light scuff-sanding; as a full-refinish tool they mostly produce abandoned projects.
What is a Lägler TRIO and why do you keep mentioning it?
It's a German planetary sander — three counter-rotating discs under a heavy machine — that we run as the final pass on our refinishes. It erases the directional scratch patterns the belt sander and edger leave and blends them into one flat, uniform surface. It's slow and undramatic, and it's a large part of why a finished floor looks even in hard light.
What are chatter marks and can they be fixed?
Chatter is a repeating wave pattern cut into the floor by a machine vibrating or moving unevenly — drum sanders are the classic source. You often can't see it on raw wood; it appears once finish and low-angle light arrive. It's fixable by sanding past it, ideally with a planetary machine, but that spends wear thickness — prevention is much cheaper.
Why does the grit sequence matter so much?
Because each grit's only job is to erase the scratches of the grit before it. Jump from 36 to 80 and the 36-grit scratches never get erased — they get polished, and finish magnifies them into permanent haze. A proper ladder takes more passes and more hours, which is exactly why it's one of the first corners a too-cheap bid cuts.
Do you sand the edges with the same machine?
No — the big machines can't reach the last few inches at walls, so a separate edger handles the perimeter, run through matching grits. Blending edger work into the field is genuine craft; the halo of swirls around the room's edge on some floors is what it looks like when that blending got skipped. The TRIO pass helps unify the two zones.
Is sanding fir different from sanding oak?
Meaningfully, yes. Old-growth fir runs about 660 on the Janka scale versus red oak's 1290 — roughly half the hardness — so every abrasive bites deeper and every equipment flaw shows larger. Fir wants sharper paper, finer entry grits, and a lighter machine touch. Crews that treat it like oak leave marks that crews that respect it don't.
How much dust does professional sanding actually make?
Far less than the horror stories — modern machines capture the overwhelming share of dust at the cut through vacuum systems. There's still fine dust in the work areas, which is why we offer full plastic containment with sealed doorways and protected HVAC returns at a flat $250. Between capture and containment, the rest of the house stays livable.
