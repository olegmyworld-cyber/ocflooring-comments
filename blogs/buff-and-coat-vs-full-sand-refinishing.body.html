“Buff and coat” and “screen and recoat” are the same service wearing different names — we abrade the existing finish with a fine screen or buffer pad and lay a fresh coat of finish over it, never touching wood. A full sand is the other thing entirely: machines take the floor down to bare wood, erasing finish, damage, and color in one pass, and we rebuild from zero. One is maintenance; the other is a reset.

The comparison matters because the two get sold interchangeably, and they shouldn’t be. What Each One Actually Does

A buff and coat treats the finish as what it is — a renewable wear layer. The screening removes the scratched, dulled top skin of the old polyurethane and gives the new coat something to grip; the fresh coat then restores thickness, sheen, and protection. Everything below the finish is untouched, which is precisely its power and its limit: the floor keeps its color, its patina, its flatness — and also its deep scratches, its gray spots, and every other flaw that lives in the wood.

A full sand removes the entire finish and a thin slice of wood with it, which is why it can erase what a recoat can’t: wear-through, deep scratches, sun fade, water marks, an outdated stain color. The floor that emerges is effectively new material — we flatten it with a Lägler TRIO final pass and build a fresh 2–3 coat waterborne system on it. The costs are the obvious one (price and 3–5 days of disruption) and a quieter one: each full sand spends a portion of the floor’s finite wood, and a solid floor has maybe four to six sandings in its lifetime. Sanding a floor that needed a recoat isn’t just triple the price — it’s withdrawing from a account you can’t refill.

The Candidacy Test: Five Questions, Two Minutes

Whether a buff and coat is even on the menu comes down to five checks. One: is the finish intact everywhere? Drop water on the worst traffic areas — if it soaks in and darkens the wood anywhere, the film is breached and screening is off the table. Two: is the damage finish-deep? Scratches your fingernail glides over will screen out; grooves that catch it won’t — the full triage is in our deep vs surface scratches guide. Three: do you like the color? A recoat locks in the current look; any color change means sanding. Four: what’s been put on the floor? Wax, polish, oil soaps, and certain “rejuvenator” products leave residues that stop new finish from bonding — the honest risk in this trade, and the reason we ask about your cleaning products before quoting. Five: has it been recoated recently with unknown products? Adhesion over a mystery finish gets tested, not assumed.

Fail any of the five and the answer is a full sand — not because we prefer the bigger ticket, but because a recoat that peels or seals in damage is the most expensive cheap job in flooring. Pass all five and a full sand would be over-treatment; we’ll say that just as plainly.

The Ten-Year Math

Here’s the comparison that actually matters, because nobody buys one floor service in isolation — you buy a decade of floor condition. Take 1,000 sq ft. Strategy A, maintenance: buff and coat every four years — three services in a decade at $1,990 each, roughly $6,000, and the floor never looks worse than “lightly used” while its sandable wood sits untouched. Strategy B, neglect and reset: do nothing until year eight or nine, when the traffic lanes have worn through and gone gray, then pay $3,990 for the full sand the neglect made mandatory. Cheaper on paper — until you price the years of tired floors, the week of disruption, and the sanding you just spent from the floor’s lifetime budget.

The honest wrinkle: Strategy A only works if you start while the floor still qualifies. The prefinished floors need one honest asterisk: their beveled board edges and factory aluminum-oxide coatings make recoating a more careful job — the screen has to abrade a very hard finish without burning through on the bevels — and adhesion gets verified, not guessed. Buff and Coat vs Full Sand: What Homeowners Ask Us

What's the difference between buff and coat and screen and recoat?
Nothing — they're two names for the same service. The existing finish is abraded with a fine screen or buffing pad, then a fresh coat of finish goes over it. Some companies also call it a maintenance coat. If a quote uses different words at a very different price, ask what's actually included.
How do I know if my floor qualifies for a buff and coat?
The finish must be intact everywhere — drop water on the worst traffic spots, and if it beads rather than soaking in, you're likely a candidate. Damage should be limited to dullness and fine scratches, you must be keeping the current color, and the floor can't have been treated with wax, polish, or oil soaps that block adhesion.
Why can't you buff out deep scratches or gray spots?
Because they're in the wood, not the finish. Buffing only removes and replaces the finish layer; anything below it — grooves, gray oxidized patches, stains, fade — stays and gets sealed under the new coat, often looking shinier and therefore worse. Damage in the wood only comes out with sanding.
How long does each option take?
Buff and coat: one day, walking in socks the same evening, furniture back in 48–72 hours. Full sand: typically 3–5 days for sanding plus 2–3 coats of finish, same-evening sock traffic after the final coat, furniture at 48–72 hours, and rugs after 7 days while the finish cures.
How often should floors get a buff and coat?
Every 3–5 years for a typical household, every 2–4 with active dogs — the point is renewing the wear layer before any spot wears through. On that rhythm a floor can go decades without needing a full sand, preserving its finite sandable wood for genuine emergencies or a future color change.
Can a buff and coat change my floor's sheen?
Yes — that's one of its underrated uses. The new topcoat sets the sheen, so a glossy floor can go satin or matte in a single day without sanding. What it can't change is color: the wood and any stain underneath stay exactly as they are.
Why did a contractor quote me a full sand when the floor looks fine?
Possibly over-selling — or possibly a residue problem. Floors maintained with polishes, oil soaps, or wax can't be reliably recoated because new finish won't bond, and an honest contractor who finds residue has to quote the sand. Ask which reason applies; the water-bead test and your cleaning-product history usually settle it quickly.
Is a buff and coat worth it on a floor we'll refinish eventually anyway?
Usually, yes. At $1.99 per square foot it buys years of good-looking, protected floor and defers the $3.99 sand — and since each full sand consumes part of the floor's sandable lifetime, deferring is genuinely valuable, not just cash-flow management. The exception is a floor you'll restain within a year or two; then just wait and sand once.
