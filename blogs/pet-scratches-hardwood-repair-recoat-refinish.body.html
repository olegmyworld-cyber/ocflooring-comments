First, Sort the Damage: Finish, Wood, or Stain

Pet damage comes in exactly three flavors, and the repair follows the flavor. Finish scratching — the fine white webs and dull lanes where the dog corners by the back door — sits entirely in the polyurethane. Your fingernail glides over it. Wood damage — deeper gouges from big dogs, dents in soft boards — catches your fingernail and often shows pale raw wood. Urine staining — from spots that yellowed to boards gone gray-black — is chemical, not mechanical, and behaves differently from both.

The fingernail-and-water-drop routine from our scratch triage guide applies unchanged: nail glides and water beads means the finish is intact and a recoat handles it; nail catches or water soaks in means the film is breached. The only pet-specific addition is your nose and your eyes on color — any spot that’s darker than the surrounding wood has had liquid in it, and dark spots are the one category where waiting actively destroys boards.

The Recoat: Where Most Pet Floors Land

If the damage inventory is scratched finish — and for households with trimmed-claw dogs on oak, it almost always is — the fix is a screen & recoat: abrade off the scratched topcoat, lay down a fresh coat of commercial waterborne. $1.99/sq ft, one day, socks that evening, and the scratch field is gone. For pet households specifically, waterborne chemistry earns its keep twice over — the roughly 65 g/L VOC finishes we use mean nobody has to board the dog at a kennel for a week, and the details are in our guide to low-VOC refinishing with kids and pets.

Here’s the part that matters more than any single repair: for a home with active dogs, the recoat isn’t a one-time fix, it’s the maintenance rhythm. Scratching is going to continue — claws don’t negotiate — so the winning strategy is treating the topcoat as a sacrificial layer and renewing it every 2–4 years, before wear ever reaches wood. Households on that rhythm essentially never face a forced full sand; households that skip it are the ones calling us about gray traffic lanes five years later.

Urine Stains: The Honest Bad News

Urine is alkaline, and it doesn’t scratch wood — it reacts with it. Caught the same day, it wipes up consequence-free. Left to soak in repeatedly, it works down into the wood fiber and reacts with the tannins, and the wood itself changes color: first a darkened halo, eventually gray-black. That color change is the whole problem. A light, recent mark often sands out with the rest of the floor during a $3.99/sq ft refinish. A black stain usually doesn’t — the discoloration runs deeper than any sane sanding depth, and bleaching is a lottery we don’t recommend on floors people have to live with. Black boards get cut out and replaced with matching stock, woven in during the refinish and quoted per repair. Our urine-stain refinishing guide goes board-by-board on this.

Two things follow from that chemistry. First, speed matters more than product choice — enzymatic cleaners are fine, but the variable that decides a board’s fate is hours-to-cleanup, not brand. Second, if you’re refinishing a floor with a history of accidents, budget for the possibility of a few board replacements: we can predict a lot from the surface, but some stains only show their true depth once sanding starts, and an honest contractor tells you that before the machines are running rather than after.

Species, Sheen, and the Floors That Fight Back

How fast a dog damages a floor is mostly decided before the dog arrives, by what the floor is made of and how it’s finished. On the Janka scale, the red oak in most of Renton’s postwar housing sits at 1,290 — hard enough that claws scratch finish rather than denting wood. White oak (~1,360), maple (1,450), and hickory (1,820) do even better; hickory in particular shrugs off big dogs. Douglas fir, at 660, is the regional exception: in older fir-floored homes, claws leave genuine dents and grooves, and pet owners with fir should set expectations — and recoat cadence — accordingly.

Finish choices move the visibility needle almost as much as species moves the damage needle. Lower sheens hide scratching dramatically — the light scatter that makes a scratch flash white on a semi-gloss floor disappears into a matte or satin surface — and clear waterborne over natural wood hides wear better than any dark stain, which shows every pale scratch line like chalk on a blackboard. Pet Scratches on Hardwood: What Owners Ask Us

Can dog scratches be buffed out of hardwood floors?
Usually, yes. Most claw scratching sits in the finish layer, and a screen and recoat — abrading off the scratched topcoat and applying a fresh one — removes it completely in a day at $1.99 per square foot. Scratches that catch your fingernail have reached the wood and need sanding instead.
Do we need to refinish the whole floor for pet scratches?
Only if scratches have cut through the finish into wood across the floor, or urine has stained boards. Finish-level scratching — the common case — needs a recoat, not a refinish. Spot-sanding individual scratches doesn't blend invisibly, so the choice is generally recoat everything or sand everything in the affected rooms.
Can pet urine stains be sanded out?
Light, recent marks often sand out with a full refinish. Black or gray-black stains usually can't — the discoloration is a chemical reaction that runs deeper than sanding can reach, and those boards get replaced with matching stock during the refinish. The darker the stain, the worse the odds, which is why speed of cleanup matters more than any product.
What's the most scratch-resistant finish for dogs?
A commercial two-component waterborne like the Bona and Pallmann systems we use — the same grade specified for gyms and restaurants — in a matte or satin sheen. The hard finish resists claw wear, and the low sheen hides the scratching that happens anyway. No residential finish is scratch-proof under an 80-pound dog; the goal is slow accumulation plus easy renewal.
Will a recoat get rid of the pet smell?
No. Odor lives in urine that has penetrated wood and subfloor, and sealing over it doesn't neutralize it. If a floor smells, the source boards need to be found and replaced — sometimes with subfloor treatment below them — before finishing. A recoat is a surface renewal, not a remediation.
Does the dog have to leave the house during the work?
During sanding and coating days, pets should be out of the work area — a day or two for a recoat, and with the low-VOC waterborne finishes we use, the household including pets can typically sleep home the same night. Keep paws off new finish until it's ready for foot traffic, and remember dogs don't wear socks: plan on closed doors or a gate for the first evening.
How often should a pet household recoat their floors?
Every 2–4 years, ahead of visible wear-through, versus 3–5 for households without pets. The recoat renews the sacrificial finish layer before claws reach wood, which is what lets a floor survive decades of dogs without ever needing a full sand. Waiting until bare spots appear forfeits the cheap option.
Are trimmed nails really enough to protect the floor?
Trimming helps more than any product you can buy — blunt claws scratch finish far more slowly than sharp ones — but it reduces, not eliminates. Runner rugs on the main dog highways and a mat at the water bowl cover most of the rest. The floors we see age best combine trimmed nails, low sheen, and a recoat rhythm.
