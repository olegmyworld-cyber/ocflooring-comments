Is It Pine, or Is It Fir? (And Does It Matter?)

Around Puget Sound, most floors that homeowners call “pine” are actually old-growth Douglas fir — it was the local timber, and it went into nearly every pre-war house in the city. True pine shows up too, especially in upstairs bedrooms and in homes whose builders ordered flooring from further afield. Telling them apart: fir has tight, straight, densely spaced grain lines in oranges and ambers, while pine tends toward broader, swirlier grain with prominent round knots and a yellower cast. We’ve written a full guide to refinishing old Seattle fir if your floor turns out to be the local species.

For refinishing purposes, the answer to “does it matter” is: less than you’d think. Both are softwoods that sand fast, dent easily, and blotch under stain. The technique that protects one protects the other. Where it does matter is color — fir goes richly amber-orange under finish, pine stays lighter and yellower — and in hardness, where old-growth fir’s tight grain gives it a bit more resistance than most pine. Either way, you own a floor that a modern builder could not buy at any lumberyard: old-growth softwood with growth rings packed tight from slow, ancient trees. That tight grain is precisely why these floors have survived a century of feet.

What Sanding Softwood Is Actually Like

Softwood comes off fast. A big machine that needs several deliberate passes to cut through oak’s old finish will dig into pine before the operator has finished the first wall-to-wall run if the grit and pace are wrong. That speed sounds like an advantage, and in scheduling terms it is — but it means every mistake happens faster and cuts deeper. Chatter marks, drum stops, and edger swirls that oak would partially resist get carved straight into pine. This is genuinely a floor where machine discipline separates outcomes, which is why our final passes run through the Lägler TRIO — a three-disc flattening machine we’ve written about before — that levels softwood without gouging it.

Century-old softwood also comes with century-old hardware. These floors were face-nailed, and a hundred years of seasonal movement backs those nails out a whisker at a time. Every one of them has to be found and set below the surface before sanding, because a nail head meeting a sanding belt ruins the belt and scars the floor. Add board patches where walls moved and radiators came out, and the prep day on a century home is often as long as the sanding itself. It’s also why softwood quotes deserve an in-person look rather than a phone estimate — the repair list is the variable, and it hides under the old finish.

What a Softwood Floor Won’t Give You — and What It Will

Here’s the conversation we have at every softwood estimate: this floor will dent. At 660 Janka for fir and less for pine, heels, chair legs, dropped keys, and dog claws will all leave their signatures, starting the week you move back in. No finish prevents it — finish resists abrasion, not compression. If a floor that stays showroom-flat matters to you, softwood will frustrate you, and we’d rather say so before sanding than after. The species comparison in our fir-versus-oak Craftsman guide goes deeper on that trade.

What you get in exchange is character that hardwood can’t match: boards wider than anything modern, color that glows instead of sitting flat, and a patina argument in your favor — on a softwood floor, the dents read as history rather than damage, and they accumulate into a surface that looks better at year ten than year one. Most of our century-home clients sand out the worst damage, keep the deepest marks, and choose finish that lets the wood look its age. The homes wear it well.

Stain, Color, and the Finish Choice

Softwood and stain have a rocky relationship. Pine and fir absorb pigment unevenly — thirsty earlywood gulps it, dense latewood refuses it — and the result is the blotchy, striped look that fills flooring-forum horror threads. It can be managed with conditioners and gel stains, but our standing advice is simpler: don’t fight the wood. A clear finish over sanded softwood delivers the color these floors are loved for, at our natural-refinish rate of $3.99/sq ft, with zero blotch risk.

The finish chemistry matters more here than on oak. Oil-modified polyurethane ambers as it ages, and on already-warm softwood that ambering shoves the floor toward orange — pumpkin, honestly — within a few years. The waterborne systems we run (Bona and Pallmann, two to three coats) hold the wood at the color you see the day the job ends, and their thin, clear build lets the grain show instead of sitting under a plastic-looking layer. On fir especially, waterborne versus oil is the difference between glowing amber and a Halloween palette.

The Thickness Question Every Century Floor Faces

A hundred-year-old floor has usually met a sander before — often several. Each full sanding removes a little wood, and softwood’s fast cut means careless past jobs may have removed more than their share. Before we quote, we check what’s left: at floor vents and in closets you can see board thickness directly, and tongue-and-groove flooring can only be sanded until the wear surface above the tongue runs out. Most century softwood floors still have a refinish or two in them; a few don’t, and it’s far better to know that before the machines arrive. Our guides to how many times a floor can be sanded and when floors can’t be refinished cover the full checklist.

When a softwood floor is genuinely too thin for another full sand, the options aren’t only replacement: a screen and recoat can renew the finish without removing wood, and gentle hand-scraping can rescue localized spots. Losing an original 1915 floor to one avoidable over-aggressive sanding is the outcome we plan hardest against — it’s wood nobody can reorder.

Refinishing Pine and Softwood Floors: What Seattle Homeowners Ask

Can hundred-year-old pine floors really be refinished?
Usually, yes. The limiting factor is remaining wood thickness after past sandings, which we check at vents and closets before quoting. Most century softwood floors have at least one more full refinish in them, and floors too thin for sanding can often still be recoated.
How do I know if my floor is pine or Douglas fir?
Fir shows tight, straight, closely spaced grain lines in amber and orange tones; pine has broader, swirlier grain, round knots, and a yellower color. In Seattle’s pre-war homes fir is the more common answer. Refinishing technique is nearly identical either way.
Will refinishing get all the dents and marks out?
Not all, and on a century floor you may not want it to. Sanding removes surface damage, but chasing every deep dent means removing extra irreplaceable wood. Most clients sand out the worst, keep the deepest character marks, and end up glad they did.
Can you stain pine or fir a darker color?
It’s possible but risky — softwood absorbs stain unevenly and tends to blotch, even with conditioners. We recommend a natural finish on nearly every softwood floor. If you want a dramatically darker floor, it’s worth reading about stain behavior before committing.
What finish is best on softwood?
A clear commercial waterborne — we use Bona and Pallmann systems, two to three coats. Waterborne keeps softwood’s warm color from tipping into orange, which is what ambering oil-based polyurethane does to fir and pine within a few years.
Will the new finish stop the floor from denting?
No. Finish protects against abrasion and moisture, not compression — dents come from the wood itself, and fir at 660 Janka will always dent more easily than oak at 1290. Felt pads, rugs in work zones, and realistic expectations are the actual protection plan.
How long does a century-home softwood refinish take?
Typically 3 to 5 days: a day of prep and repairs — setting old face nails, patching boards, addressing gaps — then sanding, then two to three coats of finish. You can walk on waterborne finish in socks the same evening it goes down, with furniture back at 48 to 72 hours.
What does it cost?
Our full sand with natural finish runs $3.99 per square foot, with a 500 sq ft job minimum. Century homes sometimes add repair time for patches and nail work, which is why we quote softwood floors in person rather than over the phone.
The boards have gaps — is that a problem?
Seasonal gaps are normal in old softwood and mostly a cosmetic call. Filling wide gaps in a floor that still moves with the seasons tends to fail, so we usually leave honest gaps alone and fill only where boards are stable. We’ll walk you through the trade-offs on site.
