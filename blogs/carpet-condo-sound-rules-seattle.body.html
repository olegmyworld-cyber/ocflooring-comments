Why Your Building Cares What Is on Your Floor

Concrete and steel are wonderful at holding buildings up and terrible at keeping impact noise out. Airborne sound — voices, music — is what most people imagine, but the complaint that actually lands on condo boards is impact noise: heels on a hard floor, a chair dragged back, a dog's toenails at 6 a.m., all of it transmitted straight through the slab into the ceiling below. A hard floor couples every one of those impacts directly to the structure. Carpet and pad decouple them — the impact lands on fiber and foam before it ever reaches concrete.

That is the entire reason acoustic rules exist in CC&Rs, and why they got stricter as hard-surface flooring got fashionable. Boards spent two decades refereeing disputes between upstairs owners who installed handsome new floors and downstairs owners who suddenly heard every footstep. The rules you have to comply with today are the scar tissue from those fights.

IIC, Translated Into Plain English

Most acoustic clauses hang on one number: IIC, Impact Insulation Class. It measures how well a floor assembly stops impact noise from reaching the room below — higher is quieter. Building codes generally set IIC 50 as the minimum for multifamily construction, and that is where most CC&Rs start; plenty of Seattle buildings write a higher bar into their rules, and some specify how it must be demonstrated.

Three things worth knowing about the number. First, it rates the whole assembly — slab, underlayment, floor covering together — not the flooring product alone, which is why a marketing sheet's lab number and your building's reality can differ. Second, lab tests flatter: a rating measured in a laboratory floor rig usually comes out several points better than the same assembly measured in a real building, and some boards ask for field numbers for exactly that reason. Third — the practical one — carpet over pad is the easiest pass there is. Soft floor coverings absorb impact at the surface, which is precisely what IIC measures, and a normal carpet-and-pad assembly lands far above the thresholds boards write down. Hard surface over a bare slab, for comparison, can fall short of code entirely without an engineered sound mat underneath.

What the CC&Rs Actually Say

Read your building's flooring clause before you fall in love with any floor — it is usually two paragraphs, and it decides the project. The common patterns: a minimum IIC rating for any new floor covering, sometimes with proof required; a carpet requirement in bedrooms and living areas, with hard surface allowed only in kitchens, baths and entries; a coverage percentage in older buildings — the classic rule that a certain share of the floor must be covered by carpet or rugs; and an approval process, where the board or manager signs off on the spec before work starts. Some buildings add logistics rules on top: work hours, elevator reservations, protected corridors, and where a torn-out floor is allowed to leave the building.

If your unit is going carpet, that clause stops being an obstacle and becomes your argument. You are installing the assembly the rule was written to encourage. We have walked this road with boards before — the same dynamic we described on the refinishing side in condo floor work under an HOA — and a clean submission almost always comes back approved without discussion.

Condo Sound Rules and Carpet: What Owners Ask Us

What IIC rating do I need for my Seattle condo?
Read your CC&Rs — that is the only number that binds you. Building codes generally set IIC 50 as the multifamily minimum, and most buildings start there; some Seattle boards require more, and some specify how compliance is demonstrated. Carpet over pad clears any of these thresholds comfortably, which is why it is the low-drama answer.
Does carpet really satisfy condo acoustic requirements?
Yes — it is the assembly the rules were effectively written around. IIC measures impact absorption, and soft floor coverings absorb impact at the surface better than anything else you can install. A normal carpet-and-pad build lands far above typical CC&R thresholds, without the engineered underlayment a hard floor needs to get close.
My building requires board approval for new flooring. What do you provide?
The full package: spec sheets for the carpet and pad with acoustic data, our license and insurance certificate, a schedule inside the building’s work hours, and the elevator and disposal plan. You forward it, the board signs off, we install. We would rather spend a week on paperwork than have a job stopped in the lobby.
Can I put hardwood in my condo instead?
Often yes, with an approved acoustic underlayment and the board’s sign-off — and if that is what you want, get the assembly approved in writing before ordering anything. But go in with open eyes: you are paying for a sound mat, doing more paperwork, and still ending up closer to the acoustic minimum than a basic carpet install starts.
What is the 80% carpet rule I keep hearing about?
Older buildings often wrote coverage rules instead of IIC numbers: some percentage of each room’s floor — commonly cited as 80% — must be covered by carpet or rugs. If your CC&Rs read that way, wall-to-wall carpet satisfies it by definition. Check whether your building has since amended to a modern IIC clause; many have.
Does thicker pad mean a quieter floor for my downstairs neighbor?
Any reasonable pad passes the acoustic requirement, so choose pad for comfort and carpet life first — density matters more than sheer thickness there. That said, a quality dense pad does absorb impact better than a collapsed builder-grade one, and in a building, every bit of absorption is neighbor goodwill.
How does a condo install differ from a house install?
The cutting is the same; the logistics are not. Elevator reservation, corridor protection, permitted work hours, and the building’s disposal rules all have to be arranged in advance, which is why we start with the building manager at estimate time. Most units are still installed in a single day once the date is booked.
