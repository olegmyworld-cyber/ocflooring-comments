The Three Bamboos (and Why Construction Decides Everything)

Flip a spare board or peer at a floor vent cutout and you’ll find one of three constructions. Classic solid bamboo — horizontal or vertical — is made of bamboo strips laminated together; horizontal shows the plant’s characteristic knuckles, vertical shows tight, thin lines. Strand-woven bamboo shreds the stalk into fibers and compresses them with resin into dense blocks that get milled like lumber; the surface looks more like exotic hardwood grain than like bamboo. Engineered bamboo glues a thin bamboo veneer over a plywood or composite core — the same idea as engineered hardwood, with the same refinishing catch.

Construction determines refinishability because it determines what a sander finds beneath the surface. Solid and strand-woven boards are their material all the way through; sanding just exposes more of the same. Engineered boards have only that top veneer to offer, and once it’s gone, sanding reveals glue and plywood — unrecoverably. If your floor is engineered, everything in our guide to refinishing engineered hardwood and its wear layer applies to bamboo verbatim: the wear layer thickness is the whole conversation, and on much of the 2000s engineered bamboo it’s thin enough that one careful refinish is the lifetime maximum — or zero.

Classic Solid Bamboo: Refinishable, With Quirks

Horizontal and vertical solid bamboo is the good news category — it sands and refinishes much like hardwood, and it’s where most of our successful bamboo projects live. The quirks are real but manageable. Bamboo’s fibers run dead straight, and sanding can raise a fine fuzz that hardwood doesn’t produce; it takes disciplined grit progression and screening between coats to bury it smooth. The knuckles on horizontal bamboo are slightly denser than the material around them, so aggressive sanding can create subtle wave; our Lägler TRIO final pass exists for exactly this kind of flatness problem. None of this changes the price — it changes the technique.

The one solid-bamboo subtype that needs an expectation talk is carbonized bamboo — the caramel-brown version. That color comes from heat-treating the sugars in the stalk, and the treatment softens the material noticeably; carbonized floors dent the way softwoods do, and no refinish changes that. Worth knowing too: the color goes all the way through, so sanding doesn’t lighten it — but sanding also can’t remove the dents economically, so a carbonized floor covered in compression marks will emerge refinished, flatter, cleaner, and still dented. We’ve had that talk often enough to put it in writing here.

Strand-Woven: the Floor That Fights the Sander

Strand-woven bamboo genuinely earns its hardness reputation — dense with compressed fiber and resin, it tests past jatoba’s 2350 Janka, which is to say past every hardwood on our reference chart, hickory’s 1820 included. Owners love what that does for daily wear. Sanders feel it differently: strand bamboo cuts slowly, loads abrasive fast, and takes visibly more passes per pound of progress than oak. We budget more abrasive and more machine time for strand jobs, the way we do for the hard exotic species — the same fight we described in our piece on refinishing hickory, turned up a notch. It’s work, but it’s doable work, and a well-sanded strand floor under fresh waterborne finish looks terrific.

Two strand-specific cautions. First, some strand-woven floors — especially later, thinner, click-together product — are engineered strand: veneer over core, which puts them back in the wear-layer conversation above. Second, stain is even less of an option here than on other bamboo; the resin-dense surface takes pigment unevenly at best. Our recommendation on every bamboo construction is the same: sand clean, finish natural with a clear waterborne system, and let the material be its own color. That’s also the honest reason the $6.50/sq ft stain tier effectively doesn’t exist for bamboo — we’d be selling you a blotch.

When Refinishing Bamboo Doesn’t Work

Four situations move us from “yes” to “spend the money differently.” Thin or exhausted veneer — engineered bamboo whose wear layer won’t survive a full sand; we measure at a vent or threshold before quoting, every time. Delamination — the 2000s boom included some bargain product whose glue lines are now letting go; strips separating at the edges or hollow-sounding boards mean sanding pressure will make things worse, not better. Moisture damage — bamboo cups and swells like wood, and boards that have drunk a dishwasher leak generally need replacement, not sanding. Widespread deep denting on carbonized floors — refinishable in theory, but you’ll pay for a full refinish and keep most of the dents; owners who hate the dents are usually happier replacing.

The Verdict, Compressed

Refinishing bamboo makes sense when…

  • It’s solid horizontal, vertical, or strand-woven construction
  • An engineered veneer measures thick enough for a full sand — verified, not assumed
  • Boards are tight, flat, and well-adhered — no delamination or hollow spots
  • The damage is surface wear and finish dullness, not deep dents or water swelling
  • You’re happy with the floor’s existing color under a clear natural finish

Replacement wins when…

  • Edge-on inspection shows plywood layers under a thin, tired veneer
  • Strips are separating or boards sound hollow — the glue is going
  • Moisture has cupped or swelled boards at kitchens, baths, or entries
  • It’s carbonized bamboo and the dents are the thing you hate
  • You want a stained or dramatically different color — bamboo won’t take it well

Bamboo Floor Refinishing: What Sammamish Homeowners Ask

Can bamboo floors be refinished at all?
Often, yes. Solid horizontal and vertical bamboo refinish much like hardwood, and strand-woven bamboo sands slowly but successfully. The exception is engineered bamboo, where a thin veneer over plywood may not survive a sanding — that has to be measured before anyone quotes you.
How do I know which kind of bamboo I have?
Look at a board edge at a floor vent, threshold, or spare plank. Plywood layers mean engineered; solid material throughout means solid. On the surface, knuckles mean horizontal, thin parallel lines mean vertical, and a dense hardwood-like swirl means strand-woven. Caramel-brown color throughout is carbonized.
Is strand-woven bamboo really harder than hardwood?
Yes — quality strand-woven tests beyond jatoba’s 2350 Janka, which tops every species on our hardwood chart, including hickory at 1820. That hardness is why it wears well and also why it sands slowly and consumes more abrasive, which shows up as machine time in a quote.
Why does carbonized bamboo dent so easily if bamboo is supposed to be hard?
The caramel color comes from heat-treating the stalk’s sugars, and that treatment softens the material substantially. Carbonized bamboo behaves more like a softwood underfoot. Refinishing restores the surface but not the hardness — the denting continues, and we say so before every carbonized job.
Can you stain bamboo a different color?
We advise against it. Bamboo’s dense, pore-free surface takes stain unevenly, and strand-woven barely accepts it at all — the realistic outcome is blotch. If you want a genuinely different color, replacement is the honest route; if you like the current color, a clear waterborne finish will keep it.
What finish do you use on bamboo?
The same commercial waterborne systems we use on hardwood — Bona and Pallmann, two to three coats. Non-ambering finish matters on bamboo: natural bamboo stays blonde instead of yellowing, and carbonized keeps its intended caramel rather than drifting orange.
How much does bamboo refinishing cost?
Refinishable bamboo prices like hardwood: $3.99 per square foot for a full sand with natural finish, with a 500 sq ft minimum. Strand-woven can run above that once we’ve seen it, because it takes meaningfully more abrasive and machine time — we’ll tell you at the estimate, in writing.
Can a bamboo floor be screen-and-recoated instead of fully sanded?
Yes — if the finish is dull but intact, with no wear-through, a screen and recoat at $1.99 per square foot renews the surface in a day. It’s an especially good fit for engineered bamboo, since it spends none of the precious veneer. Buildup or wear-through pushes you back to the full-sand conversation.
My bamboo is separating at the strips — can sanding fix that?
No, and it can make it worse. Strips letting go means the laminating adhesive is failing, and machine sanding puts pressure across exactly those joints. Delaminating bamboo is a replacement conversation, and we’d rather tell you that at the estimate than discover it mid-job.
