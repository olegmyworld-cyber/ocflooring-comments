What “Sandless Refinishing” Actually Is

Every no-sand service on the market — the franchises, the one-day wonder ads, our own screen and recoat — is a variation on the same three steps: deep-clean the floor, abrade or chemically etch the existing finish so it will bond, and lay down a fresh topcoat. The differences are in step two. Franchise “sandless” outfits typically use a liquid etching solution; we run a buffer with a fine abrasive screen, which is where the name screen and recoat comes from. Either way, the new finish is only as good as its grip on the old one, and the old finish stays part of your floor forever — along with everything trapped in and under it.

Done on the right floor, this is a legitimately great service. It’s the flooring equivalent of an oil change: cheap, fast, and the single best way to make hardwood last decades. Our screen-and-recoat vs full-refinish guide covers when we recommend it. The problem isn’t the service — it’s the word “refinishing” in the marketing, which quietly promises results only sanding can deliver.

What It Genuinely Fixes — Credit Where It’s Due

A recoat, chemical or mechanical, solves surface-level problems well: overall dullness from years of foot traffic, fine scratches that live entirely in the finish layer, a scuffed sheen in hallways and kitchens, and the general tired look that makes a ten-year-old floor read as thirty. It also adds a fresh wear layer, which is the part with long-term value — you’re spending down the new coat instead of the finish that protects your wood. One honest day of work, walkable in socks that evening with the waterborne products we use, furniture back in 48–72 hours. For a busy Renton household that just wants the floor to look cared-for again, that’s a lot of result for $1.99 a foot.

And to be fair to the franchise operators: fast, low-odor, low-dust, and inexpensive are real virtues. On a floor that qualifies, we have no quarrel with the category. The quarrel starts with what happens at floors that don’t qualify.

What It Can’t Fix, and Why

The dividing line is simple: finish damage recoats; wood damage sands. If a scratch catches your fingernail, it’s through the finish and into wood — a new topcoat will seal it under clear plastic, permanently, like a leaf in resin. Gray or black patches in traffic paths mean the finish wore through and the exposed wood oxidized; coating over gray wood gets you shiny gray wood. The same goes for sun fade, water marks, pet stains, and any color change you’re hoping for — a recoat cannot lighten, darken, or re-stain a floor, because color work happens on bare wood. Cupped or damaged boards? Still cupped, now glossier.

There’s also a failure mode nobody’s ad mentions: adhesion. New finish over a floor that was ever waxed, polished, or cleaned with the wrong products can peel in sheets — and residues like that are exactly what a quick chemical etch can miss. It’s one reason we test and screen mechanically rather than trusting a solvent wipe. When a floor is too far gone even for sanding, that’s a different conversation — our guide to when floors can’t be refinished covers those edge cases.

How to Tell Which One Your Floor Needs

A recoat-level fix works when…

  • Scratches disappear when you wet the floor with a damp cloth — they’re in the finish
  • No gray or dark patches in the traffic paths
  • You’re happy with the current color and just want the life back
  • The floor has never been waxed or oil-soap cleaned
  • You’re maintaining a floor we (or anyone) refinished in the last decade

You need the full sand when…

  • Any scratch catches a fingernail, or boards show bare or gray wood
  • There are water marks, pet stains, or sun-fade lines under the rugs
  • You want a different color — stain work needs bare wood
  • The finish is peeling or flaking anywhere
  • It’s a 50s–60s rambler floor that’s never been refinished — it’s earned it

Sandless Refinishing: What Renton Homeowners Ask Us

Does sandless refinishing actually work?
Yes, on the right floor — one with an intact, never-waxed finish and no damage into the wood itself. It's a recoat service: it restores sheen and adds a fresh wear layer. It does not work as a substitute for refinishing a scratched, worn, or stained floor, because it removes no wood.
Is a screen and recoat the same thing as sandless refinishing?
Nearly. Both clean the existing finish, abrade it, and apply a new topcoat. The difference is how the old finish is dulled — franchise services typically use a chemical etch, while we use a buffer with fine abrasive screens, which we find more reliable for adhesion. Neither touches bare wood.
How much does each option cost?
Our screen and recoat is $1.99 per square foot and takes a day. A full sand with natural finish is $3.99 per square foot, typically 3 to 5 days; adding stain brings it to $6.50. We carry a 500 square foot minimum on refinishing work.
Will a recoat remove scratches from my floor?
Only scratches that live entirely in the finish — the faint white ones that vanish when the floor is damp. A scratch you can feel with a fingernail is in the wood, and a recoat will seal it in place under the new finish rather than remove it.
Can sandless refinishing change my floor's color?
No. Color work — staining, lightening, evening out sun fade — requires bare wood, which means sanding. Any no-sand service returns your floor in the same color it started, just cleaner and with fresh sheen.
Why did my neighbor's no-sand finish peel?
Almost always adhesion failure: the new coat couldn't bond because of wax, polish, or cleaning-product residue on the old finish. This is the biggest risk of quick-turnaround recoat services, and it's why we test suspect floors first — and decline the recoat when residue is present.
How long does a recoat last compared to a refinish?
A recoat on a healthy floor typically buys several years of protection, and repeating it every 3 to 5 years can postpone full refinishing almost indefinitely. A full refinish resets the clock entirely — with the waterborne systems we use, expect roughly a decade before the next one on a family floor.
Is the dust from a real refinish as bad as people say?
Not anymore. Our machines capture the great majority of dust at the source, and for sensitive households we offer full dust containment for a flat $250. The dust argument for sandless services is twenty years out of date.
My floor was refinished before I bought the house. Recoat or refinish?
If the finish is intact — no fingernail scratches, no gray wear-through — a recoat is usually the smart money regardless of who did the original work. We'll tell you which side of the line your floor is on at a free estimate, and it costs nothing to ask.
