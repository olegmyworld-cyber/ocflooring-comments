Why Color Can’t Be Fixed From the Top

A finished floor is three layers of information: the wood’s own color, any stain that soaked into the top fraction of a millimeter of that wood, and a clear film sitting over both. When you look at a floor and see uneven color, the unevenness is in one of the first two layers. The clear film on top is, by design, clear — adding another coat of it magnifies what’s underneath rather than hiding it, the way a second coat of varnish on a watercolor makes the watercolor more visible, not less.

That’s why a screen and recoat — the $1.99/sq ft service that solves so many other complaints — is useless here. It abrades the existing finish and lays a fresh coat on top. Sheen evens out. Scratches disappear. Color does not move at all. Same for every product marketed as a floor restorer or color-refresher: they’re either acrylic polishes that add a haze you’ll eventually have to strip, or tinted films that look passable for a season and then wear off the traffic lanes in patches, leaving a worse problem than the one they covered.

To change color, you have to reach the wood. That means sanding through the finish and past the stain-penetrated layer — a full sand, the same job as a first-time refinish, with all the same steps. The good news is that most floors have plenty of wood left for it; a solid ¾-inch floor can generally take several sandings across its life, and one extra correction cycle rarely puts a floor near that limit.

The Five Ways a Refinish Comes Out Uneven

Picture framing. A dark or light border, one to three feet wide, following the walls. The drum sander does the field and an edger does the perimeter, and if those two machines don’t finish on the same grit, the wood ends up with two different surface textures. Bare, you can’t see it. Stained, the coarser area drinks more pigment and goes darker. It is the single most common defect we get called about, and it is entirely a process failure — matching grit sequences between machines is basic, and the crews who skip it are usually the ones being paid too little to care.

Drum marks and swirl showing through. Straight lines across the boards, or cloudy circular scratches, that only appeared once the color went on. Same mechanism: sanding scratches that were invisible on raw wood become visible where stain pools in them. Our notes on drum, belt, and orbital sanders cover why the machine matters less than the sequence — but a floor that never got a proper final cut will telegraph every earlier mistake once it’s colored.

Lap lines in the stain. Stain applied in sections that were allowed to start setting before the next section met them leaves visible seams — usually running across the room in the direction the applicator was working. Two people staining a large room, one wiping too slowly, an August afternoon with the heat on: all it takes. Fixable, but only by starting over.

Blotch that belongs to the wood. Douglas fir, pine, birch, and maple have uneven-density grain that absorbs stain wildly unevenly. On these species a dark stain will look mottled no matter how skilled the crew, unless the floor is conditioned, sealed first and toned, or simply finished natural. If your floor is fir — and in this part of Snohomish County plenty are — the blotch may not be anyone’s fault. Our guide to choosing a stain color gets into which species accept which colors gracefully.

Sheen unevenness mistaken for color. Sometimes the color is fine and the shine isn’t — a dull patch where a coat went thin, a shinier lane where someone hit it twice. In raking window light this reads exactly like uneven color, and it is the one item on this list a $1.99/sq ft recoat genuinely does fix. Worth checking before you commit to a sand: look at the suspect area from several angles, and from directly above with the light behind you. If the difference disappears when the reflection does, it’s sheen, not color.

When Uneven Color Isn’t a Mistake at All

Three situations come up often enough to name. First, species variation: red oak boards from different trees vary from pink-tan to nearly brown, and a floor sanded to bare wood shows that variation honestly. A previous finish may have ambered enough to blend it; a fresh clear waterborne will not. That’s not a defect, that’s the floor.

Second, sun fade in progress. If the floor was refinished recently and the furniture went straight back, you may be looking at a shadow map from the last twenty years that’s still equalizing — or a new one forming. Light-driven color change keeps happening, and we cover what does and doesn’t reverse in our guide to restoring faded, sun-damaged floors.

Third, replacement boards. Wood laced in to patch a doorway or a plumbing repair is younger, differently cut, and sometimes a different species entirely. It will never match perfectly — good lacing makes it unremarkable, not invisible. And when a floor reads differently from one room to the next for reasons that have nothing to do with the sanding, our piece on why a refinished floor looks different room to room is the more useful read.

A re-sand will fix it

  • Picture framing — the dark perimeter border
  • Drum marks, edger swirl, or scratch patterns showing under stain
  • Stain lap lines and seams
  • A color you chose and simply don’t like
  • Blotchy stain on an oak floor that should have taken color evenly

A re-sand is the wrong tool

  • Sheen differences — a $1.99/sq ft recoat handles those
  • Natural board-to-board variation in the species
  • Grain blotch that’s inherent to fir, pine, or maple (change the plan, not the sanding)
  • Fresh sun-shadow patterns that will keep changing anyway
  • Laced-in repair boards that will never be a perfect match

How We Keep It From Happening the Second Time

Correction work is unforgiving in one specific way: the homeowner has already been burned once and will be inspecting the second job under a flashlight, which is fair. So the process matters more than usual.

Matched grit sequences between the big machine and the edger, every step, no exceptions — that alone eliminates picture framing. A final pass with a multi-disc planetary sander to erase directional scratch: the Lägler TRIO exists for exactly this, and on a floor about to take stain it isn’t optional. Water-popping when a dark stain is going down, so the grain opens uniformly and the color lands even. A stain sample on the actual floor, in the actual room, in the actual light — never off a manufacturer’s board — before we commit. Then two to three coats of Bona or Pallmann waterborne, which stays clear instead of ambering over the color you just paid to get right.

None of that is exotic. It’s just the difference between a crew doing the steps and a crew skipping the ones nobody sees, which is most of what why refinishing isn’t a DIY project comes down to.

Uneven Color After a Refinish: What Lynnwood Homeowners Ask Us

Can uneven color be fixed without sanding the whole floor again?
Almost never, if the unevenness is really color. Stain and wood color sit under the finish, so anything applied on top makes them more visible rather than less. The exception is uneven sheen, which people often mistake for color and which a screen and recoat does fix. That's the first thing we check on a visit.
What causes the dark border around the edges of my room?
It's called picture framing, and it comes from the edger finishing on a different grit than the drum sander. The two surfaces have different textures, so they absorb stain differently. It's invisible before color goes on, which is why an inexperienced crew doesn't catch it until it's too late. A re-sand with matched grit sequences fixes it permanently.
Will another sanding damage my floor?
A typical solid ¾-inch hardwood floor can take several full sandings over its life, and one correction cycle rarely pushes a floor near the end of that. What we check is the depth to the tongue and the height of the nail heads, plus how many times it's been done already. Engineered floors are the real constraint, since the wear layer is thin.
Can you just re-stain over the finish that's there?
No. Stain has to soak into wood, and there's a clear film in the way. Tinted coatings do exist, but on a floor they wear off the traffic lanes in patches within a couple of years and leave you with a worse-looking problem plus a stripping job. If the color is wrong, the wood has to be exposed again.
My fir floor came out blotchy. Was that the contractor's fault?
Possibly not. Douglas fir, pine, and maple have uneven grain density and absorb stain unevenly by nature — a dark stain on fir is mottled unless the floor is conditioned, sealed and toned, or finished natural. If your contractor promised even dark color on fir without discussing that, the failure was in the conversation. Finishing natural is often the better answer.
How much does correcting uneven color cost?
The same as the refinish it replaces: $3.99 per square foot for a full sand with a natural waterborne finish, $6.50 per square foot with stain, and $55 to $75 per tread on stairs. Our minimum is 500 square feet. That's frustrating to hear when you already paid once, but there's no cheaper mechanism that reaches the wood.
Do I have to redo rooms that look fine?
Usually the connected ones, yes. Color has to change at a doorway or a threshold to look deliberate — if it changes in the middle of a continuous floor, the eye reads it as damage. So the scope is the connected surface, not the problem area. Rooms behind a real transition can genuinely be left alone.
How do I avoid this on the second try?
Ask three concrete questions: what grit does the edger finish at compared to the drum, do you do a planetary or multi-disc final pass before stain, and will you put the actual stain on my actual floor as a sample first. Contractors who do this work correctly answer all three instantly. The answers, not the price, are what predict the outcome.
