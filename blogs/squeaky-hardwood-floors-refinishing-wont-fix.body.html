Where Squeaks Actually Come From

A nail-down hardwood floor is a sandwich: finish boards nailed to a plywood or board subfloor, which is nailed or screwed to the joists. Squeaks are born at the joints of that sandwich. The most common is board-on-nail: a floorboard works slightly loose over the years, and each footstep slides the board a hair up and down its nail shank — that’s the classic sharp chirp. Second is board-on-board: two neighboring boards rubbing edges as they flex. Third, and deepest, is subfloor-on-joist: the whole subfloor panel lifting off a joist where its fasteners lost grip, which reads as a lower, woodier thunk-creak.

Why do tight floors loosen? Mostly seasonal movement. Wood swells and shrinks with humidity, and every cycle works fasteners a little looser — the same physics behind the gaps that open in winter. Add normal settling of the framing, the odd undersized joist span, and a few decades, and some noise is close to inevitable. Squeaks are almost never structural danger, for what it’s worth — a squeaky floor is annoying, not failing. But they don’t sand away, either, and here’s precisely why.

Why Sanding Doesn’t Touch a Squeak

Follow the depth. A full refinish removes roughly 1/32 of an inch from the top of the boards and replaces the finish film — everything happens in the top millimeter of a floor system that’s three inches deep. The loose nail shank sits half an inch down. The subfloor joint sits an inch and a half down. The joist connection deeper still. The refinish never visits any of the places the squeak lives. If anything, homeowners occasionally report squeaks are more audible after refinishing, for a mundane reason: the house is emptier and quieter during floor week than it’s been in years, rugs are up for the 7-day cure, and everyone is suddenly listening to their floor.

This is worth knowing defensively, too. “The refinish will tighten everything up” is a line that gets used to close estimates, and it isn’t true — new finish stiffens the surface film, not the fastening. If a squeak matters to you, it needs its own line on the scope of work, with an actual mechanical fix attached. What that fix looks like depends on which layer is talking, and on what access exists — which is the next section, and where the good news lives.

What Actually Silences a Squeak

Every real squeak fix does the same thing: it stops the movement, either by re-fastening the loose layer or by taking the friction out of the joint. From above — the usual approach in a finished house — loose boards get screwed or ring-shank nailed down through to the subfloor or joist, with the holes filled. On a floor that’s staying as-is, those fills are visible if you know where to look; done during a refinish, they vanish completely under the sanding and new finish. That’s the whole argument for timing. There are also specialty above-floor systems using breakaway screws, and the old talcum-or-graphite-in-the-seam trick for board-on-board rubbing — honest but temporary, a lubricant rather than a repair.

From below is better when it’s available: a basement or crawlspace ceiling that exposes the joists lets squeaks be fixed invisibly with shims, construction adhesive, and screws driven up through the subfloor — no holes in the finish floor at all. The layer diagnosis matters here, and it’s the kind of thing covered in our primer on subfloors and what sits beneath your hardwood. Our practice on refinishing jobs is simple: walk the floor with us at the start, point out every squeak that bothers you, and we fasten what’s accessible from above before the sander runs — the repairs disappear into the refinish, and the floor comes back both new-looking and quieter. What we won’t promise is total silence: a 30-year-old floor system has more joints than any repair list, and “dramatically better” is the honest target.

The Squeak That Comes Every January

A Pacific Northwest specialty: floors that squeak all winter and go quiet in June. That’s humidity doing exactly what humidity does — winter heating drives indoor air dry, boards shrink, joints and fasteners loosen their grip, and the floor gets talkative; summer moisture swells everything snug again. If your squeak keeps a calendar, that’s useful diagnostic information (it’s movement-driven, and mild), and the cheapest mitigation isn’t a contractor at all: it’s keeping indoor humidity steadier through the heating season. We’ve written more about how humidity moves hardwood floors — the same swings behind seasonal gaps, cupping, and most of the mystery noises houses make in their first cold snap.

Seasonal squeaks are also the ones most worth triaging honestly: if a squeak only exists six weeks a year, drilling and fastening may be more cure than the disease deserves. Year-round squeaks in high-traffic paths are the ones that earn a real repair — ideally timed, again, to refinishing week.

Squeaky Floors: What Redmond Homeowners Ask Us

Will refinishing my hardwood floors fix the squeaks?
No. Squeaks are caused by movement between boards, nails, subfloor, and joists — all below the surface a refinish touches. Sanding removes about 1/32 of an inch from the top and new finish is just a film. The repair that works is mechanical fastening, which we can do during refinishing so the holes disappear.
Why does my floor squeak more in winter?
Winter heating dries indoor air, boards shrink, and joints and fasteners lose their grip — the floor literally gets looser for a season. Summer humidity swells it quiet again. A squeak that keeps a calendar is movement-driven and mild; steadier indoor humidity through the heating season is the cheapest fix.
Are squeaky floors a sign of structural problems?
Almost never. A squeak means two components are rubbing, not that anything is failing — old floors talk. The exceptions worth a look are squeaks paired with visible sagging, sloping, or bounce, which point at framing rather than flooring. On their own, squeaks are an annoyance with a mechanical fix.
How do you fix squeaks during a refinish?
We walk the floor with you first and mark every squeak that bothers you. Accessible loose boards get screwed or ring-shank nailed down to the subfloor or joists, holes are filled, and then sanding erases every trace of the repair before finish goes down. It's the one week in a floor's life when squeak repair is invisible.
Can squeaks be fixed without refinishing?
Yes — from below, invisibly, if a basement or crawlspace exposes the joists: shims, adhesive, and screws driven up into the subfloor. From above in a finished floor, fastening works but leaves filled holes that a careful color match minimizes, not eliminates. That trade-off is why we suggest bundling repairs with refinishing when one is on the horizon anyway.
Will every squeak be gone after you fasten the floor?
Honestly, no — 'dramatically quieter' is the real target. A decades-old floor system has hundreds of joints, and fastening addresses the ones that are loose and reachable today. Seasonal movement can eventually wake a new spot. What you shouldn't expect is the main hallway announcing every trip to the kitchen.
Do squeaky floors hurt resale?
They don't help. Inspectors note them, buyers step on them during showings, and a squeak in a freshly refinished floor reads as a question mark. If you're refinishing before listing, that's exactly the moment to have the main-path squeaks fastened — same project, no visible evidence, quieter walk-through.
Why did squeaks seem louder after my floors were refinished?
The floor didn't get worse — the house got quieter. During and after floor week the rooms are empty, the rugs are up for the cure period, and hard surfaces carry sound. The squeaks were always there; the soft furnishings that muffled them are what changed. It's also why refinishing week is the best time to fix them.
