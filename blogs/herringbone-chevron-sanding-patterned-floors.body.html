Herringbone vs Chevron (and Why the Sander Cares)

Quick vocabulary, because the two get swapped constantly. Herringbone is rectangular boards laid in a staggered zigzag — each board meets the next at 90°, and the ends overlap in that classic broken-weave look. Chevron boards are cut at an angle on the ends (commonly 45°) so each pair meets in a clean point, making continuous arrows down the room. Visually, chevron reads sleeker; herringbone reads more traditional. To a sanding machine they present the same problem with different geometry: alternating grain direction, every few inches, across the entire floor.

Why that matters: wood sands differently along the grain than across it. Sanding with the grain shears fibers cleanly; sanding across it tears them. On a straight-lay floor you simply run the machine with the boards. On herringbone there is no “with the boards” — a pass that flatters one set of boards is cross-cutting its neighbors at 90°. Cut too aggressively and you leave scratch patterns in alternating boards that are nearly invisible on raw wood… and glaring once finish, stain, or low afternoon sun arrives. Every botched-herringbone rescue we’ve done tells the same story: someone sanded it like a normal floor.

How We Actually Sand a Patterned Floor

Sequence and machinery. Heavy stock removal happens on the diagonal — at roughly 45° to the pattern, splitting the difference so no board takes a pure cross-grain cut. Where the old finish is thick or the floor uneven, we may work two opposing diagonals before the floor is flat and clean. Then the machine that makes pattern work civilized takes over: the Lägler TRIO, a three-disc planetary sander whose heads spin and orbit simultaneously. Its scratch pattern is effectively directionless — it treats both halves of the zigzag identically, which is exactly what a two-direction floor needs. The TRIO handles the intermediate and fine passes, erasing the diagonal scratches and leaving a uniformly abraded surface with no directional bias. Edges, borders, and the picture-frame strips that often surround herringbone fields get edger and hand work, following each border piece’s own grain.

The finish system on top is our usual: Bona or Pallmann waterborne, 2–3 coats with abrasion between. Nothing about the pattern changes the chemistry — it changes how much care the surface underneath demands.

The Stain Question: Pattern Floors Play by Different Rules

Here’s the conversation we have at every patterned-floor estimate. Because alternating boards present different grain angles to the light, they absorb stain and reflect sheen slightly differently — that’s not a defect, it’s the entire reason the pattern shimmers. A natural finish or a light stain celebrates this: the floor changes character as you walk around it, which is what you paid for. A dark, heavy stain mutes it: pigment evens out the light-play, and from across the room a dark espresso herringbone can read almost like straight flooring. Neither is wrong, but you should choose on purpose. Our general advice from the stain-color guide applies double here: sample on your floor, in your light, across at least one full zigzag repeat — a sample on a single board tells you nothing about how the pattern will behave. On white oak herringbone, natural and lightly toned finishes are what most Mercer Island clients land on, and we think they’re right.

Repairs, Borders, and the Other Things Patterns Hide

Two practical notes before the local section. First, repairs: replacing a damaged board in a herringbone field is genuinely fiddlier than in straight flooring — the piece is locked into the weave and often glued, and its replacement must match species, thickness, and the exact angle cut. It’s absolutely doable and we do it, but it argues for fixing water leaks fast and using felt pads religiously, because pattern repairs cost more per board than plank repairs. Second, wide borders and inlay strips around the field are sanded as their own small floors, each with its own grain direction — they’re also where previous refinishers’ shortcuts show first, usually as dish-out or cross-grain scratching at the frame. If your floor has a decorative border, ask whoever quotes it how they’ll handle the transition; a shrug is disqualifying. The same attention-to-geometry logic from our wide-plank refinishing guide applies: the less standard the floor, the more the outcome depends on who’s holding the machine.

Herringbone & Chevron Refinishing: What Homeowners Ask Us

Can herringbone and chevron floors be refinished at all?
Yes, and they respond as well as any hardwood — the wood is ordinary oak or similar; only the geometry is special. The requirement is a contractor who sands to the pattern: diagonal roughing passes and a multi-directional machine for the finishing cuts, instead of straight drum passes that cross-cut half the boards.
What goes wrong when patterned floors are sanded incorrectly?
Cross-grain scratches in alternating boards. A straight sanding pass runs with one set of boards and across the other, tearing fibers in the cross-cut set. The scratches hide on raw wood and appear under finish, stain, or low-angle light — a checkerboard of scratched and clean boards that can only be fixed by resanding correctly.
What is the difference between herringbone and chevron?
Herringbone uses rectangular boards meeting at 90 degrees in a staggered zigzag. Chevron boards have angle-cut ends that meet in a point, forming continuous arrows. Chevron reads more modern, herringbone more classic. Sanding-wise they pose the same challenge: grain running in two directions across the whole floor.
Does refinishing a herringbone floor cost more?
Our per-foot pricing is the same: $3.99 for a natural refinish, $6.50 with stain. The pattern demands more skill and more careful machine time, but it doesn't change what you pay per square foot. Repairs inside the pattern are the exception — replacing woven-in boards runs more than plank repairs.
Can you stain a herringbone floor dark?
You can, and it looks handsome — but understand the trade: dark pigment evens out the alternating light reflection that makes the pattern shimmer, so from a distance the floor reads flatter and more uniform. Lighter and natural finishes preserve the light-play. We always sample across a full pattern repeat before committing.
Our chevron floor was just installed unfinished. Is finishing it different from refinishing?
The sanding is gentler — new floors need flattening and fine sanding rather than heavy stock removal — but the direction problem is identical, so the same diagonal-plus-TRIO approach applies. Site-finishing a new pattern floor is also the chance to get the color exactly right before furniture arrives.
How many times can a herringbone floor be sanded?
The same as any solid floor of its thickness — the pattern doesn't reduce the sanding budget. Most solid herringbone boards carry the same wear layer as plank flooring. If it's engineered chevron, the wear-layer thickness decides, exactly as with any engineered floor.
Do you handle the borders and inlay strips too?
Yes — borders are sanded as their own small floors, following each piece's grain with edgers and hand work, then finished with the field so the sheen matches. Border transitions are where rushed jobs show first, so we treat them as a named line item in the plan, not an afterthought.
How long does refinishing a patterned floor take?
The same 3 to 5 days as a comparable straight-lay refinish — the extra care is in technique, not calendar time. Waterborne finish means you're walking on it in socks the evening of the final coat and moving furniture back at 48 to 72 hours.
