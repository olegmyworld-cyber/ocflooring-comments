Quick answer

Finish peels for one reason: it never bonded to what’s underneath. The usual culprits, in the order we find them in Seattle homes, are contamination (wax, oil soap, acrylic polish, or silicone furniture spray under the new coat), a recoat applied without abrading the old finish, incompatible products layered on each other, and moisture pushing up from below. Peeling is not normal wear — a properly bonded finish wears thin gradually; it doesn’t come off in flakes and sheets. The fix depends on the cause, which is why we diagnose before we quote: some floors need a $1.99/sq ft screen & recoat done right, and some need the full $3.99/sq ft resand because nothing will ever stick to what’s on there now.

Adhesion 101: Why Finish Lets Go

A floor finish holds on two ways: mechanically, by gripping microscopic scratches in the surface below, and chemically, by being compatible with that surface. Break either one and you get a coat of plastic resting on the floor rather than attached to it. It can rest there convincingly for a while — until seasonal wood movement, a dragged chair, or plain foot traffic flexes the film past what its weak grip can hold. That’s why peeling so often shows up months after the work, and why it concentrates at board edges and traffic lanes: those are where the film flexes most.

You can usually read the failure from the flake itself. Turn a chip over. If the underside is glossy and smooth, the new finish went over old finish that was never abraded — a classic rushed recoat. If the underside is cloudy, waxy, or slick to the touch, there was contamination under the coat. If the flake pulled up wood fibers with it, the problem is in the wood — usually moisture. Ten seconds with a flake narrows the diagnosis more than any amount of theorizing.

The Usual Suspects, Ranked

1. Contamination. The big one. Acrylic “rejuvenator” polishes, oil soaps, silicone-based furniture sprays that drifted onto the floor, and — in older Seattle homes — decades-old paste wax all leave films that polyurethane cannot grip. The cruel part: the floor looks clean. The contamination is invisible until the new coat starts crawling, beading, or later flaking. This is why we’re fussy about what goes on a floor between refinishes — the cheap bottle of shine product can quietly cost a full resand.

2. No abrasion before recoating. A recoat only works if the old finish is scuffed to give the new coat tooth. “We just mopped it and rolled a new coat on” is a sentence we hear from homeowners describing a bargain crew’s work, and it reliably produces peeling within the year. The mechanics of doing it right — and when chemical bonding systems substitute for screening — are in our guide to screen & recoat vs chemical abrasion.

3. Incompatible layers. Waterborne over fresh oil poly that hasn’t fully cured, finish over a wax-based cleaner’s residue, or big-box polyurethane over an unknown factory coating. Products that are individually fine can still refuse to bond to each other.

4. Moisture. Finish applied to wood that was too damp, or vapor pushing up from a wet crawlspace, will lift finish off from below — often with a whitish haze first, which is its own subject we cover in white and cloudy spots on hardwood. Moisture failures look different: bigger sheets, lifted grain, and flakes with fibers attached.

Why Seattle Floors Peel More Than Most

Two local factors stack the deck. First, age: Seattle’s Craftsman and pre-war housing stock has floors that have been maintained for eighty to a hundred years — which means eighty to a hundred years of products. Paste wax was the standard floor care for decades, and wax never fully leaves a floor without aggressive sanding; a coat of poly applied over a “cleaned” 1928 fir floor in Wallingford is a peeling call waiting to happen. Old Douglas fir adds its own wrinkle — it’s resinous, soft, and less forgiving of shortcuts, which is part of why we treat old Seattle fir as its own discipline. Second, moisture: a large share of the city’s houses sit over vented crawlspaces or century-old basements, and in our climate those stay damp from October to May. A floor that reads dry in August can be pushing vapor through its finish by November. When we do hardwood floor refinishing in Seattle, checking what’s under the floor is part of the estimate, not an afterthought — a $10 vapor test now beats a peeled floor later.

The Fix: Recoat, Resand, or Solve the Building First

Here’s how the repair decision actually gets made. If the peeling is isolated — a few spots near a door, one room of a larger floor — and the flake says “no abrasion,” the existing finish elsewhere may still be sound. We test it: scuff a patch, apply finish, let it cure, and try to peel it. If it holds, a proper screen & recoat at $1.99/sq ft can save the floor, with the failed areas cut back and blended. If the flake says contamination — wax, polish, silicone — spot fixes are off the table, because the contaminant is everywhere the mop went, not just where the finish has let go so far. That floor needs a full sand to bare wood at $3.99/sq ft, taking every trace of the old surface with it. It’s the expensive answer, but it’s the only one that doesn’t fail twice.

And if the diagnosis is moisture, stop — fixing the floor before the building wastes the refinish. Crawlspace vapor barriers, gutter and drainage problems, and plumbing leaks come first; then we refinish. We’d rather push a job back a month than sand a floor that’s going to peel again from below. The difference between the finish failing and the wood failing is the same line we draw in deep vs surface damage: we can rebuild anything that’s finish-deep, but the substrate has to be right first.

Making the Next Finish the Last One That Peels

Prevention is boring and absolute. On our jobs: the floor is sanded or abraded immediately before coating, tack-cleaned, and coated with a compatible system — Bona or Pallmann waterborne, 2–3 coats, each one keyed to the last. No coat ever goes over an unknown surface without a bond test. On your side, the list is even shorter: nothing goes on the floor but a cleaner made for polyurethane-finished hardwood — no polish, no oil soap, no “restorer” — and any moisture problem in the house gets treated as a floor problem too. Do that, and finish failure drops out of your vocabulary; the finish just wears slowly and honestly until a scheduled recoat, the way it’s supposed to.

Peeling & Flaking Finish: What Seattle Homeowners Ask Us

Is peeling finish normal wear and tear?
No. A bonded finish wears thin gradually in traffic lanes — it dulls and scratches, but it doesn't detach. Flaking, chipping, or peeling in strips means the finish never bonded to the layer below it. That's an application or contamination problem, not age.
Why did our finish start peeling months after the job?
The bond was bad from day one; it just took time for stress to expose it. Seasonal wood movement, furniture, and foot traffic flex the finish film, and a poorly bonded film eventually gets levered off — usually starting at board edges and traffic areas. The failure date is misleading; the cause is in the original application.
Can you fix peeling finish without sanding the whole floor?
Sometimes. If the peeling is isolated and the surrounding finish passes a bond test, we cut back the failed areas and do a proper screen and recoat at $1.99 per square foot. But if the cause is contamination — wax, polish, oil soap — the whole surface is compromised and only a full resand at $3.99 per square foot fixes it. The flake itself usually tells us which case you have.
What products cause finish to peel?
The recurring offenders: acrylic floor polishes and restorers, oil soaps, wax pastes, and silicone-based furniture sprays that drift onto the floor. All of them leave residues that new finish cannot grip. If a product promises shine, assume it is in this category until proven otherwise.
We just paid another company to refinish and it's peeling. What now?
Document it — photos, dates, invoice — and ask them to honor their workmanship warranty; peeling within months is an application failure, not wear. If they won't stand behind it, we can diagnose the cause and quote the correct fix. We're happy to put what we find in writing.
Does Seattle's damp climate cause peeling?
It contributes. Damp crawlspaces and basements push moisture vapor up through floors from roughly October to May, and finish applied over damp wood — or wood that gets damp later — can lift from below. That's why moisture readings and a look underneath the floor are part of a competent estimate here.
Can old wax really still be on a floor after decades?
Yes. Paste wax soaks into finish and wood and survives ordinary cleaning indefinitely. Floors waxed in the 1950s can still fail a bond test today. The only reliable removal is sanding to bare wood, which is why old Seattle floors with a wax history should be resanded, not recoated.
How do you make sure your own finish doesn't peel?
Fresh abrasion immediately before every coat, a clean tack, moisture checks before we start, and a compatible system — Bona or Pallmann waterborne, two to three coats — applied on its schedule. On recoats of floors we didn't finish, we bond-test first and tell you honestly if the floor fails. Our work carries a one-year workmanship warranty.
Is peeling different from the finish wearing through?
Completely. Worn-through finish is gradual: dull lanes, then gray wood showing. That's normal aging, fixed cheaply if caught early. Peeling is detachment — pieces of finish coming off intact. One is a maintenance schedule; the other is a defect that needs diagnosis.
