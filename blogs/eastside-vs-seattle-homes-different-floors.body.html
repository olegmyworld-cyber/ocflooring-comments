Quick answer

Seattle floors are usually old, soft, and thin; Eastside floors are usually newer, harder, and sometimes not solid wood at all — and those two facts drive almost every difference in how we quote, schedule, and finish a job on each side of the lake. In a Wallingford bungalow the first question is whether there’s enough Douglas fir left above the nails to sand. In a Bellevue or Sammamish house built after 1985, the first question is whether the “hardwood” is engineered and how thick its wear layer is. Same crew, same rates, very different risks.

What’s Under a Seattle House

Cross into the older Seattle neighborhoods — Ballard, Wallingford, Queen Anne, Green Lake, the Central District, much of West Seattle — and you’re mostly in housing built between about 1900 and 1940. The floor is usually vertical-grain old-growth Douglas fir, sometimes oak in the formal rooms of a nicer house, laid over a diagonal board subfloor. It’s beautiful material that nobody manufactures anymore, and it has three characteristics that shape every job.

First, it’s soft: 660 on the Janka scale, half of red oak. It dents from a dropped can and dishes out under a chair, and it has to be sanded with a lighter hand and finer sequence than oak wants. Second, it’s thin. A hundred-year-old floor has often been sanded two or three times already, and the honest first move on any pre-war house is to measure what’s left above the fasteners rather than promise a full sand — the question we walk through in how many times a floor can be refinished. Third, it has a history: shellac, wax, paint, a coat of oil poly in 1978, black pet stains that soaked into open grain decades ago, and a patch where a wall came out. Every one of those is a variable that a fresh finish has to live with.

Then there’s the building around it. Old foundations and unheated crawlspaces mean seasonal moisture swings, which show up as winter gaps you can drop a dime into and summer boards that tighten right back — normal behavior on old fir, explained in why hardwood floors have gaps. Radiators, narrow doorways, tight stair runs, plaster that doesn’t love vibration, and street parking that makes unloading a machine an adventure. None of it is a dealbreaker. All of it is why an estimate in Ballard takes longer than an estimate in Redmond, and why we go into detail about the fir-specific version in old-growth fir in Ballard and Queen Anne.

What’s Under an Eastside House

Bellevue, Kirkland, Redmond, Sammamish, Issaquah — outside a few older pockets, the dominant stock is 1970s through 2000s, with a heavy 1985–2005 concentration and a newer wave on top of it. The floor is usually red oak, sometimes white oak, sometimes maple in a 1990s kitchen, and increasingly — the further you get past 2005 — engineered plank rather than solid.

That last point is the single biggest divergence between the two sides of the lake, because engineered flooring changes what’s possible. An engineered floor can almost always be recoated; it can only be sanded if the wear layer above the tongue is thick enough, and plenty of premium-looking products aren’t. We measure at a vent or a threshold before quoting a sand, every time, and sometimes the answer is that this floor gets exactly one refinish in its life or none. The full explanation is in can engineered hardwood be refinished.

The second Eastside pattern is factory-prefinished flooring, which arrived in force in the 1990s. Those floors came with hard aluminum-oxide coatings and micro-beveled edges, and both matter: the coating is stubborn to abrade for a recoat, and the bevels mean a full sand flattens the plank profile and changes the look permanently. That’s not a reason to avoid refinishing — it’s a reason to know what you’re choosing, which we cover in site-finished vs. factory-finished over the long run.

Structurally, Eastside houses are easier: plywood subfloors, level framing, wide doorways, driveways to park in, and open plans that let a machine actually run. They also bring their own noises — engineered truss floors that squeak from movement no amount of sanding addresses, which is its own topic in squeaks refinishing won’t fix — and a lot of glass. Big south- and west-facing windows in newer construction fade floors unevenly, and that’s a much more common call for us in Bellevue and Sammamish than it is in a shaded Wallingford bungalow.

The Failures Really Are Different

In Seattle the calls we get are about history catching up with a floor. Black stains where a dog lived in 1994 and the finish had already worn through. A pumpkin-orange living room where somebody put oil poly over fir. Paint that was rolled over a hallway in the 1960s and then partly stripped. Cupping in a kitchen above a crawlspace that never got a vapor barrier. These are diagnosis jobs — we spend real time figuring out what happened before we decide what’s possible, and sometimes the honest answer is a recoat plus acceptance of the scars, because sanding deep enough to remove them would spend wood the floor can’t spare.

On the Eastside the calls are about wear and light. The lane from the garage door to the kitchen has gone dull. The dog has written its name in front of the slider. One half of the great room is visibly lighter than the other because of a wall of west-facing glass. The kitchen has micro-scratches from thirty years of bar stools. These are almost all recoat conversations — the wood is fine, the finish is tired, and $1.99/sq ft plus one day fixes it, provided nobody waits until the traffic lane goes through to bare wood.

There’s a third Eastside category worth naming: condos. Downtown Bellevue and Kirkland have a lot of hardwood in buildings with HOA rules, elevator reservations, noise windows, and neighbors sharing a wall. The floor work is straightforward; the building is the project. We wrote that up separately in condo refinishing, HOAs, and noise.

Where Each Side Gets Hard

Where Seattle jobs get complicated

  • Remaining thickness — the answer may be “no more full sands”
  • Unknown old coatings that a new finish won’t bond to
  • Soft fir that dents, dishes, and shows every machine mark
  • Seasonal gaps from crawlspaces and old foundations
  • Access: street parking, tight stairs, plaster, radiators

Where Eastside jobs get complicated

  • Engineered flooring with a wear layer too thin to sand
  • Factory aluminum-oxide coatings that resist a simple screen
  • Micro-bevels that disappear permanently in a full sand
  • Uneven sun fade across big open rooms with a lot of glass
  • Squeaks and truss movement that refinishing does not fix

Bellevue Sits in the Middle, and That’s the Point

Bellevue is the most interesting city we work in precisely because it contains both stories. There’s the 1950s and 1960s stock — the older neighborhoods near downtown, parts of West Bellevue and Enatai — with solid oak over board subfloor and the same age-related questions as a Seattle house. There’s the enormous 1985–2005 wave that defines most of the city, with solid red oak entries and dining rooms that refinish beautifully and predictably. And there’s everything built since, with wide-plank engineered floors, radiant heat in some homes, and open plans that make one continuous sanding job out of what used to be five rooms.

What that means for a Bellevue homeowner is that you genuinely cannot predict your own answer from a neighbor’s. On the same street we’ll recommend a $1.99/sq ft recoat on one house, a $3.99/sq ft full sand on the next, and on the third tell someone their engineered floor has one sand left in it and they should save it for when they actually need it. That’s not evasiveness — it’s why we walk the floor, measure at a threshold, and test a section before writing a number.

What This Should Change About Your Next Move

If your house is pre-war Seattle: assume nothing about how many refinishes are left, and get somebody to measure before they promise. Be suspicious of a low bid on an old fir floor, because the ways to save money on that job all involve sanding faster and coarser than the wood can take. Expect a conversation about what stays — some marks on a hundred-year-old floor are history, not damage, and chasing them all is how a floor gets used up.

If your house is Eastside and post-1985: find out what you actually own before you shop. Pull a vent cover and look at the edge of a board; if you see thin veneer over plywood layers, you have engineered flooring and the whole conversation changes. Then get honest about whether you need a sand at all — most of the Eastside floors we look at need a recoat, not a refinish, and the difference is roughly half the price and four fewer days. Eastside vs. Seattle Floors: Common Questions

Why did my Seattle neighbor's quote differ so much from my Bellevue one?
Almost always because the floors are different animals. Pre-war Seattle fir raises questions about remaining thickness and unknown old coatings; Eastside oak and engineered plank raise questions about wear layers and factory finishes. Our rates are identical on both sides — the scope of work isn't.
Is Douglas fir harder to refinish than oak?
It takes more care. At 660 on the Janka scale fir is about half as hard as red oak, so it cuts faster, dents easier, and shows machine marks that oak would hide. We use a finer grit sequence and finish with the Lägler TRIO to flatten without leaving a directional scratch pattern.
How do I tell if my floor is engineered?
Pull a floor vent cover and look at the cut edge of a board, or check a threshold. Solid wood shows the same grain all the way down; engineered shows a thin top veneer over layers of plywood. If you see layers, the refinishing question becomes how thick that top veneer is.
Are prefinished factory floors harder to recoat?
Somewhat. Factory aluminum-oxide coatings are very hard and need proper abrasion for a new coat to bond — that's a real step, not a formality, and skipping it is how recoats peel later. It's doable and we do it regularly; it just isn't a quick scuff-and-go.
Do old Seattle floors really have fewer refinishes left?
Often, yes. A hundred-year-old floor may have been sanded two or three times already, and each sand spends thickness that never comes back. We measure at a fastener or a floor register before promising a full sand, and sometimes recommend a recoat specifically to preserve what's left.
Why does my Eastside floor fade unevenly?
Big south- and west-facing glass. Newer open-plan homes get hours of direct sun on part of the floor and none on the rest, so oxidation and UV work at different rates across one room. A full sand resets it to a single color; rotating rugs and UV-filtering film slow the return.
Which side of the lake takes longer to refinish?
The work itself is the same: 3 to 5 days for a full sand, one day for a recoat. Seattle jobs more often add time for repairs, unknown coatings, and access; Eastside jobs more often go faster because of open plans and driveway parking. Neither is a rule.
Do you charge more to come to one side or the other?
No. Same rates across King and Snohomish County: $1.99/sq ft for a screen and recoat, $3.99 for a full sand with natural finish, $6.50 with stain, $55 to $75 per stair tread, with a 500 sq ft minimum. Travel isn't a line item on our estimates.
