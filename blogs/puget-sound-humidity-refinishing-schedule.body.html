Three Different Things Humidity Does

It changes the wood. Wood is hygroscopic — it takes on moisture from humid air and gives it up in dry air, and it moves as it does. Around Puget Sound that means boards swell slightly through the wet months and shrink through the dry ones. Sand a floor when it’s swollen and you’re flattening a surface that will change shape after you leave.

It slows the finish. Waterborne finishes cure partly by water evaporating out of the film. Damp air holds less additional water, so evaporation slows. Cold air slows it further. Neither ruins the finish; both stretch the time between coats and push the last coat later in the day.

It has almost no effect on the sanding. This is the part people expect and it’s the least true. Sanding is mechanical, our dust containment handles the airborne part, and a damp week outside makes no difference to how a drum sander cuts. Weather affects the coating schedule, not the machines.

Those three mechanisms have three different answers, which is why blanket advice about “winter refinishing” is useless. Our broader take on that is in the best time of year to refinish floors in Western Washington and in refinishing during the rainy season. This piece is the scheduling version.

The Wood Comes First — and It’s the Only Real Postponement

If a floor is cupped — boards higher at the edges than in the middle, the classic washboard you feel through socks — there is a moisture problem underneath it, and sanding is the wrong first move. Flatten a cupped floor with a sander and you remove more material from the raised edges; when the wood dries out and flattens on its own, those edges are now low and you’ve created permanent crowning. We wrote the full diagnosis in cupped hardwood floors and what causes them, and the short version is: find the moisture source, fix it, let the floor equalize, then sand.

In Everett the source is usually one of four things — a crawlspace with no vapor barrier or standing water, a leaking appliance or supply line, a basement or daylight-level room that’s wetter than the rest of the house, or a house that simply hasn’t had heat on. Three of those are fixable in days to weeks. All four are cheaper to fix than a floor sanded at the wrong moment.

The opposite problem shows up in late summer and midwinter heating season: gaps between boards as the wood shrinks. That one is mostly cosmetic and mostly seasonal — our piece on why hardwood floors gap covers which gaps to fill and which to leave alone. For scheduling purposes: gapping doesn’t delay a refinish, cupping does.

We Control the Building, Not the Weather

Here’s the practical answer to nine days of rain: none of the cure happens outdoors. What matters is the air in the room, and that’s a controllable quantity. On a wet-season job we run the heat, put dehumidifiers in the space if the indoor reading is high, keep windows and exterior doors shut, and use air movers to keep air moving across the floor rather than sitting on it. In a normally heated Everett house in February, indoor humidity is often lower than it is in an un-air-conditioned house in August.

The instinct we talk people out of most often is opening the windows to “help it dry.” On a rainy Everett day that pulls damp outside air across a curing finish and slows everything down, and it invites debris into a wet coat. Closed house, heat on, air circulating, dehumidifier if needed — that’s the recipe, and it’s the same one whether it’s raining or not.

The one thing we won’t do is coat a floor in conditions outside what the manufacturer specifies. Bona and Pallmann publish temperature and humidity ranges for their systems, and applying outside them risks a finish that dries cloudy, stays soft, or doesn’t bond properly. If we can’t get a house into range that day, we wait until we can — which usually means running heat overnight, not rescheduling the job.

This Is Where Oil-Based Loses the Argument Locally

Oil-modified polyurethane dries by solvent evaporation plus oxidation, and both slow badly in cool, damp air. A one-coat-per-day product becomes a one-coat-every-day-and-a-half product; a three-coat job that takes most of a week in Phoenix can stretch past it here between October and May, with the house smelling like a paint store the entire time and a 30-day cure still ahead of you.

Waterborne finishes are far less weather-sensitive indoors — 2–3 hours between coats, a full system down in a day, socks the same evening, furniture at 48–72 hours, rugs at seven days, full cure around a week. That difference is a big part of why waterborne became the regional default, and we lay out the whole comparison in water-based vs oil-based finish. If someone quotes you an oil-modified job in a Puget Sound February, ask specifically how many days the coating schedule will take. The answer is the point.

Everett Specifics: Marine Air, Crawlspaces, and Old Houses

Everett sits on the water, and marine air keeps outdoor humidity high for much of the year — the wet stretch from November through March is the one that shapes our calendar. But the outdoor number isn’t what governs a job; the indoor number is, and indoors is where Everett’s housing stock creates the actual variation.

The mill-era and pre-war houses in Bayside, Riverside, and the streets around Rucker and Colby often sit over crawlspaces that were built before vapor barriers were standard. A damp crawlspace pushes moisture up into the subfloor year-round, and it’s the most common reason we walk into a house and find cupped fir or oak that nobody expected. Anyone refinishing an older Everett home should look under it first — a poly sheet over the dirt is inexpensive and it changes the moisture story of the whole house. When we quote hardwood floor refinishing in Everett on a house from that era, checking the crawlspace is part of the estimate.

The postwar and Boeing-era houses out toward Silver Lake and Evergreen Way are usually simpler: heated, occupied, decent subfloors, and floors that behave predictably. Those are jobs we run all winter without special measures. The genuinely tricky ones are vacant houses — a listing that’s been empty and unheated for a month in January has cold, damp air standing in it, and the fix is turning the heat on two days before we arrive rather than waiting for spring. And around the waterfront and the older multi-family buildings, we ask about leak history before quoting, because a floor with a water event in its recent past needs to dry and equalize before it earns a sander.

What to Expect if You Book in February vs July

Booking in the wet season isn’t worse, it’s just less elastic. Plan for the top of the 3–5 day range, expect the crew to be running heat and possibly dehumidifiers, keep the house closed, and don’t schedule the furniture delivery for the afternoon after the last coat. The step-by-step sequence doesn’t change — it’s the same one in our refinishing timeline — the individual steps just get a little more breathing room.

There are two real advantages to the wet season worth naming. Scheduling is easier: summer is peak season for every flooring contractor in King and Snohomish County, and winter dates are available on shorter notice. And your floor’s dimensions in February are closer to their annual average than in a dry August, which slightly favors filling and lacing work. Neither is a reason to rush a job, but neither is winter a reason to postpone one. The only genuine postponement is a cupped floor with an unresolved moisture source — and the useful background for living with a floor through our seasons is in how humidity affects hardwood and laminate flooring.

Humidity and Refinishing: What Everett Homeowners Ask Us

Can hardwood floors be refinished in winter around Puget Sound?
Yes, and we do it all winter. What matters is the air inside the house, not the forecast — with heat on and the house closed up, indoor conditions in February are usually well within what the finish manufacturers specify. Plan for the job landing at the longer end of the 3 to 5 day range, and keep the windows shut while we're working.
Does rain outside affect the finish drying inside?
Only indirectly, through indoor humidity. A closed, heated house isn't tracking the weather closely. Where it shows up is a cold or vacant house, where damp air sits still and coats take noticeably longer to dry. That's why we heat and, if needed, dehumidify rather than reschedule around the forecast.
Should I open windows to help the floor dry faster?
No — that's the most common well-meaning mistake. On a wet Puget Sound day, open windows bring damp air across a curing finish and slow it down, and they let debris land in a wet coat. Closed house, heat on, air movers circulating, dehumidifier if the reading is high. That combination dries a floor faster than fresh air does.
My floor is cupped. Can you still refinish it?
Not yet, and this is the one situation where we genuinely tell people to wait. Cupping means moisture is coming from below or from a leak, and sanding a cupped floor flat leaves you with crowned boards once it dries. Find the source — crawlspace, appliance, plumbing — fix it, let the floor equalize, then sand. Rushing this ruins floors permanently.
What indoor humidity do you need to apply finish?
Manufacturers generally specify somewhere in the 30 to 50 percent relative humidity range, along with a temperature range. We measure rather than guess. In practice, getting an Everett house into that band means running the heat and sometimes a dehumidifier, which is a matter of hours rather than a reason to move the job.
Is oil-based polyurethane a bad idea here in winter?
It's a bad idea here in most seasons and a genuinely poor one in winter. Oil-modified poly dries by evaporation and oxidation, both of which crawl in cool, damp air — one coat a day becomes slower than that, the house holds solvent smell throughout, and a 30-day cure follows. Waterborne finishes go down in a day and are far less weather-sensitive.
Does a damp crawlspace matter for refinishing?
It matters enormously, especially in Everett's older housing. A crawlspace without a vapor barrier feeds moisture into the subfloor continuously, which shows up as cupping, gaps that never stabilize, and finish problems. Laying poly sheeting over the dirt is inexpensive relative to a floor, and it's the first thing we check on a pre-war house.
Is there a best month to refinish floors around here?
Late spring through early fall is easiest, and it's also when every contractor in the region is booked. Winter jobs go fine in a heated, occupied house and are easier to schedule. The bigger factor than the month is the condition of your specific floor and whether the house can be conditioned — we'd rather look at that than a calendar.
