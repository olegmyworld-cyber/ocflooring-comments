Why the Vacancy Window Is Worth Real Money

Price the same job twice and the empty unit wins every time. Occupied, a refinish means furniture moved and staged room to room, appliance handling billed by the piece — $160 for a washer/dryer pair, $80 per other appliance — and a schedule built around a family’s life, sometimes room-by-room across extra days. Empty, the crew walks into a clear floor plan, sands without interruption, and coats the entire unit as one surface in one sequence. The work is faster, the logistics line items are zero, and nobody’s living arrangements depend on the cure schedule.

There’s a quality angle too, not just a cost one. An empty-unit refinish gets coated wall to wall with no cut-ins around staged furniture and no compromise sequencing, which is how you get the clean, uniform result that photographs well in a listing. And whatever dust and finish-day disruption the project involves lands on nobody. The same logic drives investors on a schedule — we walked through it for fix-and-flip projects in King County — but it applies to every ordinary turnover: the vacancy is a free upgrade to the job itself.

The Turnover Ladder: Recoat Often, Refinish Rarely

The most expensive habit in rental flooring is binary thinking — “the floor is fine” for six years, then “the floor is destroyed” and a full refinish. Finish wear doesn’t work that way: the protective film thins gradually under traffic, and as long as wear stays in the film, a $1.99/sq ft screen & recoat rebuilds it in a day. Once traffic wears through the film and starts graying the wood itself, you’re into a $3.99/sq ft full sand. The whole strategy is to stay on the cheap side of that line, which for a typical rental means a recoat every turnover or two — timed to vacancies, when the recoat costs the least and disrupts nobody. We ran the honest math on this in whether recoating is worth it, and the dividing line between the two services is the subject of our screen-and-recoat vs full-refinish guide.

The full refinish still has its place on the ladder: gray or bare traffic paths, deep scratches and gouges past what recoating hides, water marks from the planter nobody mentioned, or a floor you’ve inherited at purchase with unknown history. Do it once, properly, with a commercial finish — then protect the investment with the recoat cadence so you don’t buy it again in five years.

The Rental Spec: Boring on Purpose

What we put on rental floors is deliberately unexciting. Natural, no stain. A natural finish runs $3.99/sq ft against $6.50 for stain work, and the savings is only the first reason. Stained floors show scratches as light lines through the color, demand color-matching at every future repair, and tie the unit to a fashion moment; natural oak hides wear better, patches invisibly, and reads clean to every applicant. Satin or matte sheen, because gloss is a scratch amplifier under tenant traffic. Commercial two-component waterborne, 2–3 coats — the Bona and Pallmann systems we run are what gets specified for restaurant and gym floors, which is roughly the duty cycle a rental sees. Skipping a coat or dropping to consumer-grade finish to save on a rental is the false economy: the finish is the only part of the floor tenants actually touch.

The spec that survives tenants

  • Natural color — $3.99/sq ft, hides wear, repairs match invisibly
  • Satin or matte sheen — scratches disappear into low luster
  • Commercial 2-component waterborne, 2–3 coats
  • Felt pads under anything you furnish; breathable mats only
  • A recoat line item in the turnover budget every cycle or two

Skip these in a rental

  • Dark or trendy stains — every scratch shows, every repair is a color match
  • Gloss sheens — traffic lanes appear in months
  • Oil-modified poly — a ~30-day cure doesn’t fit any turnover
  • Bargain one-day sand-and-coat specials — the wear layer is where they save
  • Waiting for “really worn” — that’s the expensive side of the line

The Turnover Timeline, Hour by Honest Hour

Here’s how the schedule actually fits. A screen & recoat is one working day, walkable in socks that evening, ready for furniture and normal traffic in 48–72 hours — it slots into any turnover week alongside paint and cleaning without moving a single other date. A full refinish runs 3–5 days of work; with the waterborne systems we use, the floor takes sock traffic the evening of the final coat, furniture at 48–72 hours, and rugs after 7 days when the film is fully cured. Practically: start the floor at the front of the vacancy, paint while coats dry if trades are sequenced carefully (painters first is cleaner), and a two-week turnover absorbs the whole thing with cure time to spare before move-in photos. The full walk-on-it schedule is in our refinished-floor timeline guide.

The one chemistry note that matters to landlords: this math only works with waterborne finish. Oil-modified polyurethane needs a day between coats and roughly 30 days to cure hard — a fresh oil-finished floor is at its most vulnerable exactly when your new tenant’s moving dolly arrives. On rentals the finish decision isn’t aesthetic; it’s whether your vacancy is one week or five.

Rental Floors Between Tenants: What Renton Landlords Ask

Is it cheaper to refinish a rental while it's empty?
Meaningfully, yes. An empty unit eliminates furniture staging and appliance handling — $160 for a washer/dryer pair and $80 per appliance in an occupied home — and lets the crew sand and coat the whole unit in one uninterrupted sequence. The work is faster, the result is cleaner, and the disruption lands on nobody.
Can floor refinishing really fit inside a normal turnover?
A screen and recoat is one day and ready for furniture in 48 to 72 hours — it fits any turnover. A full refinish is 3 to 5 working days; with waterborne finish taking sock traffic the same evening and furniture at 48 to 72 hours, a two-week vacancy absorbs it comfortably, including cure time before move-in.
Should I recoat every turnover or wait until the floor looks bad?
Recoat on a cadence — every turnover or two — rather than waiting. Wear that stays in the finish film is a $1.99 per square foot fix; wear that reaches the wood is a $3.99 per square foot full sand. The whole game is staying on the cheap side of that line, and vacancies are the free moments to do it.
Should I stain the floors in a rental?
We advise against it. Natural finish costs $3.99 per square foot versus $6.50 with stain, hides scratches and wear far better, and makes future board repairs invisible instead of a color-matching project. Satin or matte natural oak also shows well to essentially every applicant, which is what a listing needs.
What finish holds up best to tenants?
A commercial two-component waterborne system — we run Bona and Pallmann — in 2 to 3 coats, satin or matte. These are the finishes specified for restaurants and gyms, which is a fair preview of rental traffic. Oil-modified poly is the wrong tool here: its roughly 30-day cure means the floor is softest exactly when the movers arrive.
The rental has worn carpet over hardwood. Which way should I go?
Check a corner — Renton's Boeing-era ramblers usually hide sound red oak under old carpet. Refinishing what's there typically beats another carpet cycle over two turnovers, ends carpet-related deposit disputes, and raises the listing photos. If the carpet is due anyway, the vacancy you'd use to replace it is the same window a refinish needs.
How soon after refinishing can a tenant move in?
Furniture at 48 to 72 hours after the final coat, rugs and full normal life at 7 days when the waterborne film is fully cured. Schedule the floor at the front of the vacancy and the cure completes before the lease starts — hand the incoming tenant the rug date in writing.
Do you work around occupied units if there's no vacancy coming?
Yes — plenty of our work is in occupied homes, with furniture staging and room-by-room scheduling. It costs more and takes longer than the same job empty, which is exactly why this post exists: if a vacancy is on the calendar, that's the moment.
What should I tell tenants about caring for the floor?
Three things prevent most damage: felt pads under furniture, no steam mops ever, and breathable mats rather than rubber-backed ones at sinks and doors. Put all three in the lease or move-in packet. A hardwood-safe cleaner spray and microfiber mop cost less than one deposit argument.
