What Homeowners Insurance Typically Covers

The phrase that decides most floor claims is “sudden and accidental discharge of water.” A supply line bursts behind the fridge, a water heater lets go, the washing machine hose fails while you’re at work, a toddler floods a bathroom — these are the classic covered events, and hardwood damaged by them is generally part of the claim: drying, board replacement where needed, and refinishing. Storm damage that breaches the house (a tree through the roof, wind-driven rain through a broken window) usually qualifies too. Fire and the water used to put it out, likewise.

Two practical notes from the jobs we’ve worked. First, coverage applies to the damage, not the cause: the burst pipe itself is usually your plumber’s bill, while the floor it ruined is the claim. Second, speed matters enormously with hardwood — water that sits for days does exponentially more damage than water extracted the same morning, and adjusters notice mitigation. Shut the water off, get the standing water up, start air movement, and photograph everything before it’s dry. What that damage looks like as it develops — cupping first, crowning and buckling later — is covered in our guide to cupped hardwood floors.

What’s Almost Never Covered

Everything gradual. The dishwasher that seeped for six months, the slow toilet-seal leak that blackened the subfloor, the crawlspace humidity that cupped the whole main level — insurers classify these as maintenance failures, and the denial letter will use words like “long-term,” “repeated seepage,” or “wear and tear.” The rough rule adjusters apply: if the damage happened over weeks or months, it was your job to catch it. Also on the excluded list: pet scratches and stains, sun fading, dents and scratches from furniture, finish worn through in traffic lanes, and — critically for our region — surface water and groundwater flooding, which requires separate flood coverage. Water pushing up through a slab or into a basement from saturated ground is a flood policy’s problem, not your homeowners policy’s.

The honest takeaway: insurance is for events, and floors mostly die from habits. The floor problems we see most — worn finish, pet wear, gradual moisture — are exactly the ones no policy pays for, which is why the unglamorous stuff in our floor-care guidance is worth more than any coverage rider.

The Matching Question: Where Claims Get Interesting

Here’s the part most homeowners don’t know going in. Say a dishwasher line ruins 80 sq ft of a kitchen that flows into a dining room and hallway — one continuous hardwood surface. Repairing just the wet boards and refinishing just the kitchen would leave a visible seam: new finish against fifteen-year-old finish never matches, a fact we explain at length in why spot repairs show. Industry practice — and the position a good contractor’s estimate takes — is that the unit of repair is the continuous flooring area: replace the damaged boards, then sand and refinish the entire connected surface so it reads as one floor. Many policies and adjusters accept this; some push back and offer to refinish only the damaged room. This is precisely where a detailed, well-argued estimate earns its keep, and why you want your flooring contractor engaged before the scope is settled, not after. We write estimates for adjusters regularly and we itemize the way they need: board replacement, drying allowances, refinishing at $3.99/sq ft across the continuous area, stain work at $6.50 where applicable.

The usual sequence on a water claim: stop the water, call the insurer, mitigation company dries the structure (days to a couple of weeks — hardwood must come down to a stable moisture reading before anyone sands, or the refinish fails later), adjuster scopes the damage, and then the flooring work happens in two possible shapes. If boards survived drying flat, it’s repair-and-refinish. Insurance & Hardwood Floors: What Everett Homeowners Ask Us

Does homeowners insurance cover refinishing hardwood floors?
Only as part of repairing covered damage — typically sudden water events like a burst pipe or failed appliance line. Insurance never pays to refinish a floor that's simply worn out. If a covered event damages part of a continuous floor, the claim scope often includes refinishing the whole connected area so it matches.
What's the difference between a covered leak and a denied one?
Speed. A pipe that bursts today is sudden and accidental — covered. A connection that seeped for months is classified as maintenance you should have caught — denied. The physical damage can look identical; the timeline is what the adjuster investigates.
Will insurance pay to refinish the whole floor or just the damaged part?
It varies by policy and adjuster. The industry-practice argument is that a continuous hardwood surface is one unit — spot-refinishing part of it leaves a visible mismatch. A detailed contractor's estimate that explains this is often the difference between getting the whole area and getting one room. Ask before the scope is finalized.
Should we file a claim for minor floor damage?
Do the math first. If the repair is a few hundred dollars over your deductible, weigh that against the premium impact of a claim on your record. For small, localized damage, paying out of pocket for a repair and refinish is often the better long-term deal. We'll quote it either way so you can decide with real numbers.
The floor got wet. Can it be saved or does it need replacing?
Depends on how much water, for how long, and how fast drying started. Boards that dry to a stable moisture reading and lie reasonably flat can usually be sanded and refinished. Boards that buckled off the subfloor, cupped severely after drying, or show mold underneath need replacement. A moisture meter makes the call — not appearance.
How long does floor restoration take after a leak?
Drying comes first and can take one to three weeks depending on saturation — sanding wet wood guarantees a failed finish later. Once the floor is dry and any board replacement is done, the refinish itself runs a typical 3 to 5 days. Plan for the drying to be the slow part.
Do you work directly with insurance adjusters?
Yes. We provide itemized estimates in the format adjusters need, walk the loss with them when useful, and perform the restoration — board replacement, sanding, and finishing. We've done this since 2013 and we're licensed and insured, which your adjuster will ask about.
Does insurance cover water damage from flooding in Everett?
Not homeowners insurance — surface water and groundwater are excluded, and that requires a separate flood policy. The distinction matters near the Snohomish River and in low-lying neighborhoods: water from a burst pipe inside is covered; water rising from outside is not.
What should we do in the first hour after finding water on hardwood?
Stop the source, get standing water up with towels or a wet-vac, move furniture off the wet area, start fans and open airflow, and photograph everything before conditions change. Then call your insurer's claim line. Fast mitigation both limits the damage and demonstrates the diligence adjusters look for.
