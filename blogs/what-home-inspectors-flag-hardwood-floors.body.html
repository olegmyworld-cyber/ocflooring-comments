The Moisture Flags: Cupping, Staining, and Soft Boards

Moisture evidence is the flag class inspectors weight heaviest, because floors are where hidden water problems surface first. Cupping — boards curled up at the edges, washboard-feeling underfoot — reads as moisture arriving from below, and an inspector will note it even when it’s historical; our post on cupped floors explains how we tell an old, dried-out event from a live one with a moisture meter. Dark staining near dishwashers, refrigerator lines, exterior doors, and bathrooms gets written up almost automatically. Soft or spongy spots are the serious one — possible rot in the board or subfloor, and the flag most likely to trigger a structural follow-up. What matters for both sides: none of these are refinishing problems until the water source is found and fixed. We’ve talked sellers out of pre-listing refinishes over live leaks more than once — new finish over active moisture fails fast, and the buyer’s own inspector will find it anyway.

The Structural Flags: Slope, Squeaks, and Gaps

Seattle’s older homes settle — a century on glacial till and original post-and-beam foundations will do that — so inspectors here are practiced at describing floors that lean. Slope gets measured and noted; gentle, uniform slope in a 1920s house is usually old news baked in decades ago, while localized dips point at specific framing and get the “further evaluation” treatment. Squeaks are technically trivial — wood moving against fasteners — but they show up in reports because buyers ask about them on the walkthrough; worth knowing: refinishing doesn’t fix squeaks, which come from beneath the boards, not the surface. Gaps earn a note when they’re wide or uneven. Seasonal gapping is normal physics — our guide to why floors have gaps covers the difference — but gaps that fan wider toward one wall corroborate a settling story, and inspectors read them exactly that way.

The Cosmetic Flags: What Buyers Actually Negotiate On

Worn finish, scratch fields, UV fade rectangles where rugs lived, pet stains — inspectors dutifully note all of it, usually in one line: “floor finish shows wear consistent with age.” On paper it’s the mildest flag class. In practice it’s the one that moves offers, because buyers don’t price floors line by line — they price the impression of care, and a gray-worn traffic path through the living room tells a story about the furnace and the roof, fairly or not. This is where the economics get interesting for sellers: worn-but-sound floors are exactly what a pre-listing refinish is for, and when budget or timeline won’t stretch that far, a $1.99/sq ft screen and recoat — the star of our cheapest-upgrade-that-passes-inspection piece — resets the wear story in a single day. What to Fix Before Listing — and What to Leave Alone

Our honest triage, refined over a lot of pre-listing estimates: fix worn finish, because it’s cheap relative to how much buyer-impression it buys, and fixable inside a week — 3–5 days for a full refinish, one day for a screen and recoat. Investigate, then fix anything with a moisture signature, because refinishing over an unresolved leak is money burned twice — once now, once when the buyer’s inspector flags it anyway. Leave alone uniform slope, seasonal gaps, and squeaks in a century-old house: buyers of older Seattle homes expect character, inspectors describe these calmly, and money spent chasing them returns almost nothing at offer time. Sellers with pet stains or isolated damage: replace the affected boards as part of the refinish rather than hoping finish hides them. Fresh finish hides nothing — it’s a spotlight, not a blanket.

The Seattle Wrinkle: Fir, Craftsman Stock, and 100-Year-Old Floors

A big slice of Seattle’s pre-1940 housing — the Craftsman belts of Wallingford, Ballard, West Seattle, Beacon Hill — has old-growth Douglas fir underfoot, not oak, and inspectors flag fir floors constantly for the sin of being fir: soft (660 on the Janka scale against red oak’s 1290), dented by a century of heel strikes, and worn through in doorways. That’s not damage in the rot sense; it’s a softwood aging exactly as softwood does, and it refinishes beautifully with the right touch — we wrote a whole guide to refinishing Seattle’s old fir floors. The distinction matters at negotiation: “softwood wear, cosmetic” and “moisture damage, investigate” deserve very different responses, and reports don’t always separate them clearly. Inspections and Hardwood Floors: What Seattle Sellers and Buyers Ask Us

Will a home inspector fail a house over worn hardwood floors?
No — inspections don't pass or fail on cosmetics, and worn finish gets a one-line note at most. The cost shows up indirectly: buyers read tired floors as a proxy for overall maintenance and adjust offers accordingly. That's why pre-listing refinishes are so common — the fix is cheap relative to the impression it changes.
What does “recommend further evaluation” actually mean?
It means the inspector saw something outside their scope and is handing it to a specialist — it's a referral, not a verdict. For flooring flags, that evaluation is often quick: a moisture meter reading and a look at the boards tells us whether a stain is an old story or a live problem. About half the time the answer is reassuringly boring.
Should I refinish my floors before listing my Seattle home?
If the floors are structurally sound but visually tired, usually yes — it's one of the highest-return prep projects, and a 3–5 day job fits most listing timelines. If there's any sign of active moisture, fix that first; refinishing over a live problem wastes the money and the buyer's inspector will flag it anyway.
Can a refinish hide water damage or pet stains from an inspection?
No, and we won't try. Sanding and clear finish make stains more visible, not less — fresh finish is a spotlight. Deep pet and water stains need board replacement or honest disclosure. Attempting to conceal known damage isn't just poor ethics; in a disclosure state it's a legal liability that vastly outprices the repair.
Do squeaky floors hurt an inspection?
They get noted and buyers ask about them, but structurally they're trivial — wood moving against fasteners as it ages. Worth knowing: refinishing doesn't fix squeaks, since the noise comes from beneath the surface. The fix is refastening boards to joists, ideally from below, and it's a modest repair when access allows.
My report flagged cupping but the floor looks flat to me. Who's right?
Possibly both. Inspectors check floors in raking light where subtle cupping shows, and mild cupping can also relax seasonally. A moisture meter settles it: readings in the normal range mean a historical event that a refinish can flatten; elevated readings mean water is still arriving and needs finding first.
As a buyer, should a flooring flag scare me off a house?
Usually not — most flooring flags are cosmetic or long-stabilized settling, and even genuine moisture flags are frequently a fixed-leak-old-stain story. The move is to price the flag before negotiating: a refinish quote and a moisture check turn a scary paragraph into a number, and numbers negotiate better than fear.
How fast can floors be refinished between inspection and closing?
A screen and recoat is one day. A full sand and refinish runs 3–5 days, walkable in socks the evening of the final coat, furniture at 48–72 hours. We've fit both inside closing windows many times — the schedule pressure is real but manageable if you call as soon as the repair addendum takes shape.
