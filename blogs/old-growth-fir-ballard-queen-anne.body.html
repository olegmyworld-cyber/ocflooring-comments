What “Old-Growth” Actually Means in Your Floor

Look at the end of an exposed board, or the top face of a clean one, and count the grain lines per inch. Modern plantation-grown fir, harvested at a few decades old, grows fast and puts on wide, soft rings — you can count them at a glance. The fir milled around Puget Sound in the early 1900s came out of trees that had grown slowly, in competition, for two or three hundred years or more, and the rings sit so close they read as fine pinstripes. That density is not cosmetic: the tight latewood bands are the hard part of every ring, so old-growth fir carries far more of its hard tissue per inch than new fir does. Same species, materially tougher floor.

Most of these floors were also cut vertical-grain — quartersawn, so the pinstripes run straight and parallel down each board instead of in wide flame patterns. VG fir is more stable, wears more evenly, and is the look people now pay heavily for in salvage yards, because here’s the economic punchline: this material cannot be produced anymore. The trees are gone or protected, and today’s VG fir is second-growth — wider rings, softer, and expensive anyway. Whatever your floor cost in 1912, its replacement cost today makes refinishing at $3.99/sq ft look like what it is: a bargain applied to an irreplaceable surface.

Ballard and Queen Anne: Two Neighborhoods, One Wood

The two neighborhoods got the same wood for the same reason — the mills were right there — but wore it differently. Ballard was a mill and fishing city in its own right until Seattle annexed it in 1907, and its housing stock is working-class and practical: one-and-a-half-story bungalows and boxes through Adams, Whittier Heights, and Sunset Hill, built by Scandinavian shipwrights and mill hands who used fir for everything because fir was what the town made. In these houses fir usually runs wall to wall through every room, laid tight and square by people who worked wood for a living. Queen Anne — especially the south and east slopes above the Counterbalance — built a little earlier and a little fancier: Victorians, Foursquares, and the classic Seattle Box, where you’ll often find oak in the entry and parlor for show, and fir everywhere the guests didn’t go — upstairs, bedrooms, kitchens, back halls. That oak-downstairs-fir-upstairs split is so consistent we can usually predict it from the sidewalk, and we covered how the two woods behave side by side in Douglas fir vs oak in a Craftsman.

Both neighborhoods share the same twentieth-century biography: the fir got varnished or shellacked, waxed for decades, then carpeted over sometime between the 1950s and the 1980s and forgotten. Carpet turns out to be decent long-term storage — under the padding and staples we usually find a floor that has lost less wood than a floor sanded every decade would have. The staple holes and tack-strip nailing at the edges look alarming and sand out or fill discreetly; it’s the same rescue sequence we walk through in refinishing a 1920s Seattle bungalow.

Sanding a Soft Floor Without Ruining It

At Janka 660, fir removes fast under a drum sander — which cuts both ways. The good news is that a century of grime, varnish, and shallow damage comes off efficiently. The risk is that every mistake is amplified: a drum that pauses digs a visible dip, an aggressive edger leaves halos, and coarse-grit scratches that oak would shrug off telegraph straight through a clear finish on fir. Our process leans on lighter passes and finer grit progressions than an oak job, with the Lägler TRIO doing the final flattening pass — the TRIO’s three-disc action is far more forgiving on soft species than a drum finishing cut, and on fir that difference is visible from the doorway.

Old fir floors also come with old-house features that need handling before finish: gaps between boards from a century of seasonal movement (normal, and mostly left alone — filling wide moving gaps just produces cracked filler), face nails to set below the surface, and patches where walls moved or floor furnaces came out, which we either splice with salvaged fir or accept as visible history, your call. The one hard limit is thickness: some of these floors have been sanded several times since 1912, and a floor cut too close to the tongue is done being sanded. That check — done on your knees with a ruler at a heating vent, before any machine starts — is the first thing we do at an estimate, and the full version of that conversation lives in when floors can’t be refinished.

The Finish Decision: Honey, Not Pumpkin

Fir is already warm — fresh-sanded old-growth fir is a pale honey-rose that deepens naturally with light over its first few years. Put an ambering oil-modified polyurethane over that and the warmth compounds: honey goes orange, orange goes pumpkin, and in five years you have the floor that makes people think they hate fir. A huge share of the “can you fix what the last refinisher did” calls we get from Ballard are exactly this, and the fix is a re-sand. A clear waterborne finish — we run Bona and Pallmann two-component systems, two to three coats — locks in the color the floor has on sanding day and keeps it there; the full chemistry argument is in our water-based vs oil-based comparison, but fir is the species where it’s least close.

Two related choices. Stain: we talk most clients out of it — fir’s ring structure takes pigment unevenly and blotches, and the wood’s own color is the point. Sheen: matte or satin, which suit the era of these houses and hide the small dents fir will inevitably collect far better than anything glossier. A satin waterborne over natural old-growth VG fir is, in our opinion, one of the best-looking floors that exists in Seattle housing — and we sand oak all day, so that’s not species loyalty talking.

Living With Fir Afterward

Refinished fir asks two things of you. First, accept that it will dent — chair legs without felt pads, a dropped cast-iron pan, dog claws on a soft board will all leave marks that oak wouldn’t. In a century-old house we’d argue the accumulating marks are the patina working as intended, but the felt pads, entry rugs, and trimmed claws are still worth doing. Second, use the maintenance path: a $1.99/sq ft screen and recoat every five to seven years renews the wear layer without touching wood, which matters more on fir than on oak precisely because the wood underneath is finite and soft. Treated that way, the refinish you do now is plausibly the last full sanding the floor needs in your tenure — and the floor’s next hundred years get off to a better start than its last thirty under the carpet. Old-Growth Fir Floors: What Ballard and Queen Anne Owners Ask

How do I know if my fir floor is old-growth?
Look at the grain density. Old-growth boards show growth rings packed like fine pinstripes — often too many to count casually — while modern fir shows wide, obvious rings. If your house was built before roughly 1930 with its original floors, it’s old-growth; the new stuff didn’t exist yet.
Is fir too soft to be a real floor?
It’s soft — Janka 660 against red oak’s 1290 — and it dents. But the floors we sand in Ballard have carried a century of boots already, because tight-ringed old-growth wears far better than the species’ modern reputation. Fir asks for felt pads and reasonable care; in exchange it’s outlasted most of the houses built around it.
Can you match new boards where a wall was removed or a furnace came out?
Yes, with the right material — new fir looks wrong next to old because the ring density doesn’t match, so we patch with salvaged old-growth fir, which is available from reclaimed-lumber suppliers around Puget Sound. A good splice with matched grain, sanded and finished with the field, is very hard to find afterward.
Should we stain fir, maybe darker to match oak elsewhere?
We usually advise against it. Fir’s hard and soft ring bands absorb stain at different rates, so pigment tends to blotch and stripe rather than deepen evenly. The wood’s natural honey color is its best feature, and a clear finish keeps it. If you need visual continuity with oak, matching sheen and tone family works better than forcing matching color.
The floor is painted. Is it recoverable?
Usually, yes. Paint adds sanding time — and lead-safe handling in houses of this age, which is standard practice for us — but under most painted Ballard bedroom floors is the same fir as everywhere else. The one caveat: paint sometimes went on to hide damage or patched plumbing scars, so we scout at the estimate rather than promise from the doorway.
What finish should go on old fir?
A clear two-component waterborne, two to three coats, in matte or satin. Fir is already warm-toned, and ambering oil-modified poly pushes it to orange — the single most common complaint we hear about past refinishes. Waterborne keeps the honey color you see on sanding day and lets you stay in the house during the work.
What about the gaps between boards?
Century-old fir moves with the seasons, and most gaps open in winter heating and close in summer. We leave normal seasonal gaps alone — filler in a moving joint cracks out within a year or two. Consistently wide, non-seasonal gaps can be addressed board by board, but a fir floor with hairline seasonal gaps is behaving normally for its age.
Wouldn’t we be better off replacing it with oak?
Almost never, and we sell oak floors. New oak means demolition, materials at $4–$10+ per square foot plus $3–$4.25 install labor, and losing quartersawn material you cannot buy back. Refinishing runs $3.99/sq ft and yields a floor that’s both historically right for the house and — in the ways that matter — better wood than the replacement would be.
