Why Baseboards Stay On — and How We Sand That Close

The big machine that does most of a refinish can’t reach the last few inches at the wall. That perimeter belongs to the edger — a low, powerful disc sander designed to cut flat right up to a vertical surface — and to hand work in the corners the edger can’t swing through. Between them, the floor gets sanded to the baseboard’s face, under its slight overhang, with the trim never leaving the wall. (Where each machine operates, and why each exists, is in our field guide to drum, belt, and orbital sanders.)

Could we take the baseboards off instead? Sure — and it would make almost every job worse. Painted trim that’s been caulked to the wall for twenty years doesn’t come off clean: the caulk line tears, paint pulls, MDF snaps at nail points, and reinstallation means re-nailing, re-caulking, and repainting a whole house’s worth of trim. That’s a painting project stapled to a flooring project, and it buys nothing — the edger already reaches everything that needs sanding. So the trade’s default, and ours, is simple: trim stays, machines adapt.

The Quarter-Round Question

Quarter-round — the small rounded molding at the bottom of some baseboards, also called base shoe — is the one piece of trim that’s genuinely in play. It sits directly on the floor, which means the sander has to deal with it one way or another. Where it’s present, we usually recommend removing it: the edger then sands fully under where it sat, and afterward the shoe goes back on — or, more often, gets replaced with new primed stock, because decades-old shoe tends to be brittle, paint-welded to the baseboard, and unwilling to survive removal. New shoe is cheap, installs fast, and gives the refinished floor a crisp new frame; a painter’s hour of caulk and touch-up finishes the picture.

Plenty of homes — including most newer Sammamish construction — have no shoe at all, just baseboard run tight to the floor. Those floors get the straight edger-to-the-face treatment described above. Neither setup is better or worse for refinishing; they just route around the wall line differently, and we’ll tell you which yours is at the estimate. The Cosmetic Fine Print, Honestly Stated

Three small things belong in your expectations. First, the hairline reveal: sanding removes about 1/32 of an inch of floor height, so the bottom edge of the baseboard — the sliver that was pressed against the old floor surface and never painted — can peek out as a thin raw line. On white trim over pale floors it’s often invisible; on dark floors it can show. The fix is a caulk bead or a touch-up pass from a painter, and if you have painting planned anyway, sequence it after the floors.

Second, contact marks: an edger works inches from painted trim for hours, and even careful operators occasionally leave a scuff on the baseboard’s face. We treat trim as something to protect, not something the floor job is allowed to consume — but the honest expectation on any refinish, ours included, is “a few touch-up spots,” not “showroom trim untouched by human events.” Third, dust: wall lines and trim ledges are where sanding dust settles most stubbornly. Our machines run with vacuum capture, and the dust-containment option (+$250) seals the gap between “professionally managed” and “white-glove,” which matters in an occupied house — more on living through a refinish in our room-by-room scheduling guide.

When Trim Really Should Come Off

The short list. If we’re installing new hardwood rather than refinishing — a different job with different rules — baseboards typically do come off, because the new floor’s height changes the whole wall line. If there’s water damage at the wall, swollen or stained baseboard is evidence, and removing it lets everyone see how far the moisture traveled before finish seals anything in. If you’re converting carpet to hardwood, tack strips live right at the trim line and their removal sometimes argues for pulling the base too. And if a full repaint or new taller baseboards are already in your plans, removing trim before the refinish costs nothing extra and gives the painter a clean slate — we’ll happily coordinate that sequencing.

What should make you ask questions is the opposite pitch: a refinishing quote that includes removing and reinstalling all baseboards as a standard line item. It adds labor, risk, and a painting bill to a job that didn’t need any of the three — the kind of quiet padding we flagged in our piece on hidden costs in refinishing quotes.

The Sammamish Wall Line: Tall, Painted, and Tight to the Floor

Sammamish trim is unusually consistent, because Sammamish housing is: the plateau filled in fast through the 1990s and 2000s, and those builders ran tall painted baseboards — usually MDF — tight to the floor, mostly without shoe molding. That profile is actually the best case for a refinish. Nothing to remove, a clean vertical face for the edger to work against, and MDF’s razor-crisp paint lines make touch-ups nearly invisible. It’s also the profile with the most to lose from unnecessary removal, since MDF survives prying worse than any solid wood trim — one more reason the leave-it-on default is right for these houses.

The plateau’s open-plan great rooms add one more wall-line consideration: with kitchen, dining, and living flowing together, the baseboard run is long, continuous, and visible from everywhere, so consistency matters — one scuffed or mismatched section stands out in a way it wouldn’t in a chopped-up 1950s floor plan. It’s the kind of detail we plan around on every hardwood floor refinishing job in Sammamish: long sightlines reward careful edging, and the estimate is where we’ll walk your specific wall line with you.

Baseboards and Refinishing: What Sammamish Homeowners Ask

Do baseboards have to be removed to refinish hardwood floors?
No. The edger — a disc sander built to cut flat against vertical surfaces — sands right up to the baseboard's face, and corners are finished by hand. Trim removal is reserved for special cases like water damage, new installations, or a planned repaint, not standard refinishing.
Will the sander damage my baseboards?
Careful edging leaves trim essentially untouched, but the honest expectation over a whole house is a few minor scuffs at the bottom edge — touch-up paint territory, not repair territory. We protect trim as part of the job and point out anything that needs a painter's minute at the walkthrough.
What is quarter-round, and does mine need to come off?
Quarter-round, or base shoe, is the small rounded molding where some baseboards meet the floor. Since it sits on the floor itself, we usually remove it so the sander reaches everything, then reinstall it — or replace it with new primed shoe, because old shoe is often too brittle to survive removal. Homes without shoe skip this entirely.
Why is there a thin unpainted line at the bottom of my baseboard after refinishing?
Sanding lowered the floor by about 1/32 of an inch, exposing the sliver of baseboard that sat below the old surface and was never painted. It's cosmetic and normal. A caulk bead or paint touch-up erases it — and if you're planning any painting, schedule it after the floors.
Should I paint baseboards before or after floor refinishing?
After, ideally. The floor work happens first, then the painter caulks and touches up the wall line over the finished, cured floor with drop cloths down. Painting first means paying to touch up twice. If the paint is already fresh, tell us — we adjust how we work the edges.
Does finish get on the baseboards when you coat the floor?
No — finish is cut in carefully at the wall line, the same way a painter cuts in a ceiling. The coats stop at the baseboard's face. What you see afterward is a clean line where floor meets trim, not a lapped or splashed edge.
We're replacing our baseboards anyway. What order should things happen?
Remove the old trim, refinish the floors, then install and paint the new baseboards over the finished floor. That order gives the sander a completely open edge, the new trim a flat finished surface to land on, and the painter the last word. It's the cleanest sequence and we coordinate it with trim carpenters regularly.
Is dust behind the baseboards a problem during sanding?
Dust doesn't get behind properly installed baseboards, but it does settle along wall lines and trim ledges — the corners are where any sanding job shows its housekeeping. Our equipment runs with vacuum capture, and our +$250 dust-containment option adds sealed barriers for occupied or furniture-heavy homes.
