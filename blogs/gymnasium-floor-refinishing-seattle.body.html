Why Gym Floors Are Their Own Discipline

Three things separate a gymnasium from a very large living room. First, the construction: most gym floors are built as floating systems — maple over sleepers or resilient pads, engineered to flex under athletes — which means the floor moves underfoot and the sanding approach has to respect that. Second, the optics: thousands of square feet of pale maple under high, even lighting is the most unforgiving canvas in our trade, where a wave or sanding mark that a furnished room would swallow is visible from the bleachers. Flatness is why we finish every sand with the Lägler TRIO — the machine we bought for exactly this problem. Third, the duty cycle: court shoes, folding chairs, rolling bleachers, and the occasional forklift pallet of chairs at a community hall. Maple at 1450 Janka shrugs off most of it; the finish is what wears, which is the entire logic of the maintenance rhythm below.

The Two Services, and the Rhythm Between Them

Healthy gym floors live on a cycle: recoat often, resand rarely. The annual (or every-other-year) screen and recoat abrades the existing finish and lays fresh wear coats — no wood removed, court closed for days, not weeks. It’s the same honest logic as the residential version in our screen-and-recoat guide, scaled up: you spend the sacrificial layer, not the maple. The full resand — down to bare wood, new lines, new finish system — happens roughly once a decade, or when the coating stack has grown heavy and uneven, when boards need repair, or when the lines themselves need to change for new league requirements. Skipping recoats to save budget is the classic false economy: it converts several cheap closures into one long, expensive one, plus the millimeter of maple a premature resand spends.

Game Lines, Logos, and the Order of Operations

The part facility managers ask about most: what happens to the lines? In a recoat, nothing — they’re part of the existing finish stack and the new coats go over them. In a full resand, everything: lines, logos, and keys sand away with the old finish, and that’s an opportunity as much as a cost, because the new layout gets painted fresh — updated league dimensions, added pickleball or volleyball courts, a new center-court logo. The order of operations matters and can’t be rushed: sand, seal the bare maple, paint lines and graphics on the sealed surface, then bury everything under the finish coats so the lines wear as long as the finish does. Line work adds real days to the schedule — paint needs its own dry time before it can be coated — which is why a resand is a one-to-two-week commitment and why we map the calendar with you before anyone unloads a machine.

Scheduling: The Actual Hard Part

Every gym has a natural window, and the job bends to it. For schools that’s summer — long enough for a full resand with slack for the unexpected, which is why our school-gym calendar fills months ahead. Churches usually give us a six-day week and a hard Sunday deadline, which suits recoat work well. Community centers and clubs negotiate around league seasons and rental commitments. Two properties of modern waterborne systems make all of this workable. The finishes are low-VOC, so the rest of the building — classrooms, offices, the sanctuary upstairs — stays occupied while we work; nobody relocates a school for a floor anymore. And they cure on a known clock: walkable quickly, equipment-ready in days, full-intensity play at about a week — the same dry-versus-cure logic we laid out in our walk-on timeline, with higher stakes, because a tournament date doesn’t move. We build the cure week into the schedule explicitly rather than letting it surprise anyone.

It’s a recoat year when…

  • The finish is dulling in the key and center court but intact everywhere else
  • Lines are crisp and the layout still fits your leagues
  • The floor just needs its annual wear layer back before the season
  • Your window is a school break or a slow rental month, not a whole summer
  • The last full resand was less than a decade ago

It’s a resand year when…

  • Finish has worn through to gray maple in traffic zones
  • The coating stack is heavy, uneven, or peeling anywhere
  • You need new line layouts, added courts, or a new logo
  • Board repairs, water damage, or dead spots need addressing first
  • Recoats have been skipped so long there’s nothing left to recoat

Gym Floor Refinishing: What Seattle Facility Managers Ask

How often should a gym floor be recoated?
Every one to two years for a floor in regular league and PE use — annually for the busiest courts. The recoat spends a sacrificial finish layer instead of the maple, and keeping that rhythm is what pushes the expensive full resand out to roughly once a decade.
How long is the court out of commission?
A screen and recoat: a few days of work plus early cure — plan on about a week before full-intensity play. A full resand with new game lines: one to two weeks including line painting and cure. We build the schedule backward from your reopening date and put it in writing.
Can the rest of the building stay open during the work?
Yes. The commercial waterborne finishes we use are low-VOC, so classrooms, offices, and halls adjacent to the gym remain occupied throughout. Sanding days are loud — we schedule those against your calendar — but nobody needs to relocate a school for a floor project.
What happens to our game lines and center logo?
In a recoat they're preserved under the new coats. In a full resand they're removed with the old finish and repainted fresh on the sealed floor before the finish coats go over them — which is the moment to update layouts, add courts, or change a logo at minimal extra cost.
How soon after finishing can we host a tournament?
Give the final coat about a week before full-intensity play. The finish is walkable in hours and equipment-ready in days, but it reaches final hardness over the first week, and hard cuts on day-three finish shorten its life. We schedule so cure week ends before your first whistle.
Our gym floor is from the 1960s. Is it worth refinishing?
Usually more than worth it — mid-century maple is excellent stock, and unless it's been resanded many times or water-damaged, it likely has decades left. Old floors buried under heavy coating stacks are exactly what a full resand is for. We'll assess thickness honestly at the walkthrough.
Do you handle school summer schedules?
Yes — summer is gym season for us, and school work books months ahead. If you're eyeing a full resand for next summer, the walkthrough and quote should happen during the school year so the window is locked before it fills.
What does gym floor refinishing cost?
It's quoted per project after a free walkthrough — line complexity, coating history, and schedule constraints move the number too much for an honest flat rate. What holds universally: recoat years cost a fraction of resand years, and a maintained cycle is far cheaper per year than rescue work.
