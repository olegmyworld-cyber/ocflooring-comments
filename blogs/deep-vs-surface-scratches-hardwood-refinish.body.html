The Fingernail Test, and the Water-Drop Backup

Kneel down and drag a fingernail slowly across the scratch, perpendicular to it. A finish-level scratch feels like almost nothing — your nail passes over with a faint tick, and if you look at the mark from a low angle it reads as a dull or white line in an otherwise reflective surface. A deep scratch catches the nail. There’s a groove, your nail drops in and stops, and the scratch usually shows a color change: pale raw wood on a natural floor, a light line cutting through a dark floor’s stain, or gray-to-black if the cut is old and moisture has been getting in.

The backup test is a single drop of water on the scratch. If it beads and sits, the finish film under it is still continuous — the damage is cosmetic. If the drop darkens the wood within a couple of minutes, water is reaching bare fibers, which means the film is breached and the clock is running: an open scratch takes on moisture, dirt, and pet accidents, and what would have sanded out clean this year becomes a gray stain next year.

Two honest caveats. White, chalky scratch marks on factory-finished floors sometimes sit in the aluminum-oxide wear layer and look worse than they are — those usually screen out. And on floors with a stain color, even a shallow scratch can read as dramatic because it cuts through the color layer; more on that trap below.

Surface Scratches: Damage the Finish Took for the Team

Finish scratches are the film doing its job. Grit tracked in on shoes, chair legs, dog claws on the way to the door, a toy dragged down the hallway — all of it carves microscopic and not-so-microscopic lines into the polyurethane, and over a few years the sum of them is a floor that looks tired and gray in the traffic lanes even though the wood underneath is untouched. This is the single most misdiagnosed condition we see: homeowners assume a dull, scratched-up floor “needs refinishing,” when what it needs is a new topcoat.

The fix is a screen & recoat: we abrade the existing finish with a fine screen — deep enough to erase the scratch field and give the new coat something to bond to, shallow enough to never touch wood — then lay down a fresh coat of commercial waterborne finish. At $1.99/sq ft it’s half the price of a refinish, it’s done in a day, and you’re walking on it in socks that evening. Done every three to five years, it also means the floor may never need a full sand again in your ownership — you’re replacing the sacrificial layer before wear reaches anything that matters.

Deep Scratches: When the Wood Itself Is Cut

Once a scratch is through the film and into wood fiber, no coating applied on top will hide it — new finish is clear, and it will faithfully seal the groove, the torn fibers, and any discoloration under a fresh layer of plastic. We’ve been called to more than one floor where a previous contractor recoated over deep scratches; the result is a shiny floor with all the same scratches, now harder to fix.

The legitimate fix is a full sand: we take the floor down past the damage with progressively finer abrasives, flatten it with a Lägler TRIO final pass, and rebuild the finish from bare wood — 2–3 coats of Bona or Pallmann waterborne. That’s the $3.99/sq ft job, 3–5 days on a typical house, and it removes deep scratches completely as long as they’re shallower than the sanding depth — which almost all claw marks, chair gouges, and grit scratches are.

What sanding can’t always rescue: gouges from dropped cast iron or furniture dragged without pads, deep pet-urine staining that has gone black, and damage in floors that have already been sanded several times and are running out of wood above the tongue. Those cases get individual board replacement woven into the refinish, or an honest conversation about whether the floor is past refinishing. It’s a small minority of the floors we see.

The In-Between Cases That Fool People

Scratches on stained floors. This is the cruelest category. On a stained floor, the color lives in a thin layer at the surface of the wood — so a scratch that would be invisible on a natural floor cuts through the stain and flashes a bright raw-wood line. The scratch may be physically shallow, but a recoat can’t restore color that’s been cut away, and spot-staining a scratch to match a ten-year-old floor is touch-up artistry that rarely disappears completely. Widespread through-color scratches on a stained floor generally mean a full refinish; the consolation is you get to pick the color again.

Traffic-path graying. A dense web of fine scratches in front of the sink, at the back door, along the hallway — individually each is a finish scratch, but if the field is worn enough, patches of it will already be bare wood. The water-drop test settles it. If any part of the path darkens, the finish is gone there and the floor needs sanding; caught earlier, it’s the poster child for a recoat.

Pet households. Claw scratches are usually finish-level on oak and maple, and frequently wood-level on soft species like fir. If the scratches keep coming, the fix matters less than the plan — harder species, a lower sheen that hides micro-scratching, and a recoat cadence. Why Waiting Converts Cheap Fixes into Expensive Ones

The economics of scratches are lopsided. A finish that’s scratched but intact can be recoated for $1.99/sq ft more or less indefinitely. The moment wear breaks through — anywhere — the recoat option is off the table for that floor, because you can’t screen bare wood and coat over gray. The whole floor now needs the $3.99 sand, plus whatever the exposed wood absorbed while it sat unprotected. On an 800 sq ft main floor, that’s the difference between roughly $1,600 and $3,200, decided by timing. (Both numbers assume our 500 sq ft minimum; small areas price differently.)

So the practical advice is unglamorous: when the traffic lanes go dull and the scratch count starts bothering you, that annoyance is the correct trigger. Floors don’t heal, and the window where the cheap fix works closes without an announcement. Deep vs Surface Scratches: What Homeowners Ask Us

How can I tell if a scratch is just in the finish?
Drag a fingernail across it. If the nail glides over and the mark shows no color change — just a dull or whitish line — it's in the finish. If the nail catches in a groove, or you see pale raw wood or gray discoloration, the scratch has cut into the wood itself. A drop of water is the tiebreaker: beading means the finish is intact, soaking in and darkening means it's breached.
Can deep scratches be fixed without refinishing the whole floor?
Individually, sometimes — a single gouge can be filled or a badly damaged board replaced. But scattered deep scratches across a room can't be spot-sanded invisibly, because sanding one patch changes its color and sheen against the rest of the floor. If deep scratches are widespread, a full sand of the affected rooms is the fix that actually looks right.
Will a screen and recoat remove scratches?
It removes scratches that live in the finish — which is most of them on a typical worn floor. The screening abrades away the scratched topcoat and the new finish replaces it. It does nothing for scratches that reach the wood; clear finish over a deep scratch just seals it in place.
Why do scratches look white on my dark floor?
Two reasons. Shallow scratches scatter light, which reads as a white line against a dark background. Deeper ones cut through the stain color and expose undyed wood. The first kind recoats away; the second needs sanding and restaining, because the color layer itself is cut. The fingernail test tells you which you have.
Do scratches get worse if I ignore them?
Finish scratches accumulate but don't deepen on their own. The risk is wear-through: once any area loses its finish completely, water and dirt reach bare wood, gray staining sets in, and the one-day recoat option is gone for good. A scratch that catches your nail is already past that point locally and worth addressing within months, not years.
Are some wood species more scratch-prone than others?
Substantially. On the Janka hardness scale, Douglas fir sits around 660, red oak at 1,290, white oak near 1,360, maple at 1,450, and hickory at 1,820. The same dog on the same day leaves finish marks on hickory and genuine dents and gouges in fir. Species doesn't change the triage logic, but it changes how often you'll be doing it.
Can I fix scratches myself with a repair kit or marker?
Touch-up markers can make a deep scratch less noticeable on a stained floor, and for a rental or a pre-sale touch-up that may be enough. They don't restore the finish film, so the scratch remains open to moisture, and the color match is rarely exact. We'd rather you use a marker honestly than pay anyone for spot repairs that won't blend.
How much does it cost to fix a badly scratched floor?
If the scratches are in the finish, a screen and recoat runs $1.99 per square foot and takes a day. If they're into the wood, a full sand with a natural finish is $3.99 per square foot over 3–5 days, or $6.50 with a stain. Board replacements for gouges are quoted per repair. Our 500 sq ft minimum applies to any of these.
