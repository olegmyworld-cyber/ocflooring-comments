What Your HOA Will Ask For (and What We Hand Them)

Most Bellevue associations want four things before any contractor touches a unit: proof of license, a certificate of insurance — often naming the association as additional insured — a scheduled work window, and sometimes advance board or manager approval with a scope of work. Buildings with elevators add a reservation and protective pads for the freight or service car; some ask for floor protection along the corridor path too. It sounds bureaucratic because it is, and it exists because one uninsured contractor with a flooded sink can ruin three units below.

We’ve been licensed and insured since 2013, certificates are a routine request our office handles the same week, and we’d rather work in a building with strict rules than a lax one — strict buildings have fewer surprises. The practical advice for you: email your property manager before booking anyone, ask for the remodel or flooring policy in writing, and forward it to us. Ten minutes of paperwork up front beats a work stoppage on day two.

The Noise Map: What’s Actually Loud, and When

The reputation of a refinish is a week of roaring machines. The reality is more specific: the big sander and edger are genuinely loud — this is the part your neighbors will hear — and they run during the first one or two days. After that the job gets dramatically quieter: repairs and detail work are intermittent, and applying finish is nearly silent. So a typical condo refinish is one to two days of real noise inside the allowed window, then quiet coating days where nobody in the building can tell we’re there.

We schedule the loud days like the neighbors matter, because they do: machines start after the window opens (no 7 a.m. warm-ups), and we front-load the sanding so the noisy phase ends as early in the week as possible. A note on the door of adjacent units with the dates and our phone number costs nothing and has defused more grumbling than any decibel meter. Waterborne finish helps here too — with 2–3 hour recoats, the whole finish system fits inside one or two work windows instead of dragging across a week the way slow-drying oil finishes would.

Shared Air: Why Finish Chemistry Matters More in a Condo

In a detached house, a smelly finish is your family’s problem. In a condo it drifts into the corridor, rides the elevator shaft, and introduces itself to every neighbor with a nose — which is why many associations now regulate finish odor explicitly, and why oil-modified polyurethane at 450–550 g/L of VOCs is effectively unusable in multifamily buildings whatever the rulebook says. Our systems are waterborne — Bona and Pallmann, with Traffic HD at roughly 65 g/L and GREENGUARD Gold certified — and the practical difference is that hallway odor is mild and short-lived instead of a week-long building event. The full chemistry story is in our water-based vs oil-based comparison, and the health angle in our low-VOC guide.

Dust gets the same shared-space respect. Our sanders run bagged and vacuum-assisted as standard, we protect the corridor path, and for units where air migration is a concern — open doorways to hallways, shared returns — full dust containment is available as a $250 line item. In a building, the mess you prevent is measured in neighbor relationships, not just cleanup time.

The Tower Question: Is Your Floor Even Solid Wood?

One condo-specific check comes before any of this: what’s actually on your slab. Bellevue’s older garden condos usually have solid oak strip over a wood subfloor — sand away. But many tower units, especially 2000s and newer, have engineered hardwood glued to concrete, and engineered floors can only be refinished if the veneer wear layer is thick enough to survive a sanding. A 3 mm+ wear layer refinishes fine, once or twice; a 1 mm veneer does not, and sanding it is how a floor dies. We covered the measurement and the decision in our engineered-hardwood wear layer guide — and it’s the first thing we check at a tower estimate, because everything else is moot if the answer is no.

Related tower fine print: some buildings require approval for flooring changes (hard flooring over bedrooms, acoustic underlayment specs) but treat refinishing existing wood as maintenance. Refinishing doesn’t touch the acoustic stack — we’re renewing the surface, not replacing the floor — which usually makes the approval conversation short. Usually. Ask anyway.

Logistics and the Week Itself

Condo units are smaller than houses, which cuts both ways. The good news: a 900 sq ft unit sails past our 500 sq ft minimum, and the whole job — sanding, any repairs, 2–3 coats — typically lands at the short end of the usual 3–5 days. At $3.99/sq ft for a natural full sand, that unit runs about $3,600; a $1.99 screen and recoat, if your floor qualifies, is a single quiet day at half the disruption. The tighter part is staging: furniture has nowhere to hide in a condo, so plan for a POD, a garage bay, or a friendly neighbor’s spare room; we move what’s manageable and coordinate the rest at the estimate.

Living through it is easier than most owners expect. With waterborne finish you can sleep in the unit the same night a coat goes down — socks on the floor that evening, furniture back at 48–72 hours, rugs after 7 days. The full day-by-day is in how long before you can walk on refinished floors. If your unit is one open room, the practical move is two nights elsewhere during sanding — not for fumes, just because there’s nowhere to stand.

Condo Refinishing: What Bellevue Owners Ask

Do I need HOA approval to refinish my condo floors?
Usually you need to notify and schedule rather than seek full approval — refinishing existing wood is maintenance, not a flooring change. But most Bellevue buildings still require a certificate of insurance, work-hour compliance, and often manager sign-off on the scope. Get your building's policy in writing and forward it to your contractor before booking.
How loud is it, and for how long?
The genuinely loud part — the big sander and edger — runs one to two days, inside your building's allowed work hours. After that, repairs are intermittent and coating is nearly silent. Neighbors mostly experience a condo refinish as two noisy days, not a noisy week.
Will the hallway smell?
Mildly and briefly, with the finishes we use. Our waterborne systems run around 65 g/L VOC — GREENGUARD Gold certified — versus 450 to 550 for oil-based polyurethane, which is why oil finishes are effectively unusable in occupied buildings. Most neighbors notice nothing beyond a faint fresh-paint note for a day.
Can engineered floors in a tower be refinished?
Only if the wear layer is thick enough — roughly 2 mm or more for one careful sanding, 3 mm+ for comfort. Many tower floors qualify; thin-veneer products don't, and sanding them destroys the floor. We measure the wear layer at the estimate before promising anything, using a vent or a closet edge.
Can I stay in my unit during the work?
Yes, most owners do. Waterborne finish takes sock traffic the same evening, so the unit is livable throughout. The exception is small open-plan units during the one or two sanding days, when the working area is simply the whole home — many owners plan those days at the office or a friend's place.
What does refinishing a condo cost?
The same published rates as houses: $1.99/sq ft for a screen and recoat, $3.99 for a full sand with natural finish, $6.50 with stain, with a 500 sq ft minimum. A typical 900 sq ft Bellevue unit runs about $3,600 for a natural full sand. Building requirements like COIs don't add cost — they're part of the job.
Who handles the elevator and building paperwork?
Both of us: you request the elevator reservation and any owner forms (buildings only accept those from residents), and we supply the certificate of insurance, the written scope, and the schedule your manager wants. We've done this circuit enough that the paperwork rarely takes more than a few days.
Do weekends or evenings ever work for condo jobs?
Almost never for the loud phase — most Bellevue buildings prohibit weekend construction noise outright. Quiet work like final coats can sometimes flex. It's the main reason condo refinishes are scheduled as consecutive weekdays; fighting the building's rules costs more than working within them.
