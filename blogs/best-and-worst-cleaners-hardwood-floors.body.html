Quick answer

The best cleaner for a finished hardwood floor is a pH-neutral spray made specifically for hardwood — Bona’s and Pallmann’s house-brand cleaners are what we use and recommend — applied with a barely-damp microfiber pad. The worst are steam mops, vinegar, oil soaps, and anything with “polish,” “restore,” or “shine” on the label. That second list doesn’t just fail to clean; it either attacks the finish directly or leaves residue that can turn a $1.99/sq ft recoat into a $3.99/sq ft full sand down the road. Here’s the whole roster, with the chemistry behind each verdict.

Here’s the mental shift that makes every cleaning decision easy: you are never cleaning wood. On a finished floor, you’re cleaning a thin plastic film — polyurethane — that happens to have wood under it. Once you see it that way, the cleaner aisle sorts itself out. Anything that would damage or coat a plastic surface is wrong for your floor, no matter what the label says about hardwood, and no matter that your grandmother swore by it.

We have a stake in this being understood, and we’ll admit it: a meaningful share of the refinishing work we do exists because of cleaning products. Floors dulled by years of vinegar, floors gummed with oil soap, floors wearing so many coats of acrylic “restorer” that new finish physically cannot stick to them. All preventable, all common. The Best: A Short List on Purpose

The everyday routine that keeps a finish healthy for fifteen years costs under fifty dollars a year and involves exactly three tools. First, a microfiber dust mop or a vacuum with the beater bar off — grit is the real enemy of finish, working like sandpaper underfoot, and removing it is 80% of floor care. Second, a pH-neutral hardwood spray cleaner: Bona Hardwood Floor Cleaner and Pallmann Hardwood Floor Cleaner are the two we hand to clients, made by the same companies whose finishes we put down, and formulated to evaporate clean without residue. Third, a microfiber mop pad, misted lightly — the floor should look damp for seconds, not minutes, and never show standing liquid.

That’s the entire list. Notice what’s not on it: buckets. A bucket-and-string-mop setup puts far too much water on the floor, and water is the one “cleaner” that damages the wood itself, wicking into board seams and swelling the edges. If a routine needs a bucket, it’s a routine designed for tile that wandered into the wrong room. The fuller weekly-and-monthly schedule is in our guide to cleaning and maintaining hardwood floors — but tools-wise, you’ve just read all of it.

The Worst: Five Products, Five Different Ways to Lose

Steam mops top the list, and it isn’t close. Steam forces hot water vapor through the finish’s microscopic pores and into the wood, where it swells fibers, clouds the finish from underneath, and over months delaminates the coating in gray, peeling patches. Every finish manufacturer we work with voids their warranty at the sight of one, and so do we. We wrote a full autopsy in can you steam clean hardwood floors; the short version is that “chemical-free cleaning” is a great pitch and a terrible physics.

Vinegar and water is the beloved family recipe that quietly eats the shine. Acetic acid dulls polyurethane a little with every pass — no single mopping does visible harm, which is why the myth survives, but three years in, the floor has a flat, hazy look that no cleaner will bring back, because the surface itself has been micro-etched. Ammonia and all-purpose cleaners do the same work from the alkaline end of the pH scale, faster.

Oil soaps — the old-fashioned bottles that promise a nourishing clean — were designed for waxed floors that haven’t been standard since the 1960s. On polyurethane, the oil has nowhere to soak in, so it sits on top as a soft, streaky film that collects dirt and shows every footprint. And “polish,” “restorer,” and “shine” products are the most expensive mistake in the aisle, which is why they get their own section.

Why “Polish” Products Cost More Than They Look

Floor polishes and restorers work by depositing a layer of acrylic — essentially a mop-on liquid plastic — over your finish. The first coat genuinely does look shinier, which is the trap. The acrylic is far softer than polyurethane, so it scuffs and clouds within weeks, which the label helpfully solves with reapplication. A year in, the floor wears a soft, smeary jacket of build-up that grabs dirt, shows haze, and can’t be cleaned because the “dirt” is now inside the layers.

Here’s where it gets expensive. The normal cure for a dull but healthy finish is a screen & recoat: we abrade the surface lightly and lay a fresh coat of commercial finish over it — $1.99/sq ft, one day, done. But new finish will not bond to acrylic polish, silicone, or wax. On a contaminated floor, a recoat peels within months, so the only honest fix is a full sand to bare wood at $3.99/sq ft. The polish that promised to postpone refinishing is the very thing that doubles its price. When we assess a floor for a buff and coat versus a full sand, contamination is the first thing we test for — a wipe of mineral spirits in a corner tells us in thirty seconds whether the finish is clean enough to recoat.

The Middle Shelf: Products That Are Merely Okay

Disposable wet-pad mops of the Swiffer type deserve an honest middle grade. The pre-moistened pads won’t destroy a finish the way steam will, and for a quick kitchen touch-up they’re fine. But the solution is a general-purpose formula that leaves a faint film with repeated use, the pads are cost-per-clean expensive, and the flat head pushes grit around more than it lifts it. If you like the format, buy the same style of mop with washable microfiber pads and spray the floor with a proper hardwood cleaner instead — better result, less money, no residue.

Grocery-store cleaners labeled “for hardwood” vary from decent pH-neutral formulas to glorified polish in disguise. The label test we teach: if it promises shine, restoration, or a protective layer, it’s depositing something and belongs on the no list. If it just promises to clean and lists no oils or waxes, it’s probably fine. When in doubt, the boring answer — the finish manufacturer’s own cleaner — is never wrong. It’s also what keeps our workmanship warranty intact on floors we’ve refinished, a detail covered in how to clean refinished hardwood without voiding the warranty.

Been Using the Wrong One for Years? Here’s the Damage Report

The prognosis depends on which wrong product, and for how long. Vinegar and ammonia dull the finish but leave it clean — those floors usually recoat beautifully, and the haze disappears under the new coat. Oil soap and polish build-up need the residue professionally stripped or, more often, a full sand, because nothing trustworthy bonds over them. Steam damage is the saddest category: once moisture has clouded the finish from below or delaminated it, sanding to bare wood is the only reset, and boards with swollen edges sometimes need time with a dehumidifier first.

The encouraging part: every one of these floors is fixable, and none of them is a reason for shame — the misinformation is genuinely everywhere, including on the product labels. It’s also worth knowing what professional “wood floor cleaning” services can and can’t do here: a deep-clean service removes grime, but it cannot un-etch a vinegar haze or bond new finish over polish, which is why we wrote an honest comparison of refinishing versus floor cleaning services. Hardwood Cleaners: What Homeowners Ask Us

What cleaner do you actually use on your own floors?
Bona Hardwood Floor Cleaner on a microfiber pad, with regular vacuuming in between. We finish floors with Bona and Pallmann systems, and their cleaners are formulated to evaporate without leaving residue on those finishes. It is genuinely that boring, and that is the point.
Is vinegar really that bad? My family has always used it.
It's bad slowly, which is why the myth survives. Vinegar is acetic acid at roughly pH 2 to 3, and every mopping micro-etches the polyurethane a little. No single cleaning shows damage; three years of weekly cleanings leave a permanent haze that only recoating fixes. It also doesn't clean any better than a neutral product.
Can I ever use a steam mop on sealed hardwood?
No. Sealed is not waterproof — finish has microscopic pores, and pressurized hot vapor gets through them into the wood. Finish manufacturers void warranties over steam mops, and so do we. The damage shows up as clouding, edge swelling, and eventually peeling finish, and the only fix is a full sand.
What about Murphy's Oil Soap? The bottle says it's for wood.
It was designed for waxed wood floors, which mostly disappeared decades ago. On a polyurethane finish the oil can't penetrate, so it builds a soft film that streaks, grabs dirt, and interferes with any future recoat. If you've used it for years, mention it when you get a refinishing estimate — it changes the prep.
My floor looks dull. Should I use a polish to bring back the shine?
No — dullness means the finish is worn or etched, and polish only buries the problem under soft acrylic that soon looks worse. The right fix is a screen and recoat at $1.99 per square foot, which puts down real finish. Polish build-up actually blocks that option and forces a $3.99 full sand instead.
How often should I damp-mop?
Weekly for kitchens and busy households, every two weeks elsewhere — always after dust-mopping or vacuuming first, and always barely damp. Between moppings, a dry microfiber pass whenever you see grit. Cleaning more often than that with the right products does no harm; doing it with the wrong ones does.
What should I clean with right after a refinish?
For the first week: a dry dust mop only, nothing wet, while the finish cures. After the first week, ease into the normal routine with a pH-neutral hardwood cleaner. Rugs stay off for 7 days too — the finish under them needs the same air the rest of the floor gets.
Can polish or wax build-up be removed without sanding?
Sometimes. Light acrylic build-up can be stripped with dedicated removers and hard work, and we test a corner before quoting. Heavy or old build-up, and anything involving silicone, usually can't be removed reliably enough to trust a recoat over it — that's when a full sand becomes the honest recommendation.
