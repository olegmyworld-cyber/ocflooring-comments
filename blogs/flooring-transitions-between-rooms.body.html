Where a Refinish Can Stop — and Where It Can’t

The rule we quote at estimates: a refinish should end where the floor already ends. A doorway with a door in it is the classic clean break — we stop the sanding and the new finish directly under the closed door’s edge, so with the door shut neither room shows the other’s floor. A change of material is the other natural boundary: where hardwood meets the kitchen tile or the bedroom carpet, the transition already exists and refinishing simply runs up to it.

What doesn’t work is stopping on a continuous run of wood — partway down a hall, or at a wide cased opening with no door. Sanding removes years of amber and wear from one side of an invisible line and not the other; even with the identical finish, the refinished side is flatter, clearer, and differently colored, and the seam between old and new coats catches light at every angle. We decline those stopping points more weeks than not, and it’s the same reason room-by-room projects need sequencing rather than hard lines — a logistics problem we covered in our guide to refinishing an occupied Redmond home room by room.

Wood Meeting Wood: The One-Job Argument

Most Redmond main floors are a single connected field of hardwood — entry, hall, kitchen nook, family room — and the best transition between those rooms is no transition at all: sand and finish the whole field in one job, and every room matches in color, sheen, and flatness because it’s literally one surface. This is also the honest economics: our 500 sq ft minimum exists because mobilizing machines for one room costs nearly what a whole main floor costs — the math behind minimums is a post of its own on why small jobs cost more per foot.

The harder wood-to-wood case is old floor meeting new: an addition, a wall removed, hardwood extended into a former carpeted room. Weaving new boards into the old field — “lacing in” — then sanding and finishing everything together is the gold standard, because the new boards take the same finish at the same moment and the seam disappears into the floor. Butting new prefinished boards against old site-finished ones with a metal strip between is the shortcut, and it always looks like the shortcut. We’ve written about adding hardwood to existing hardwood; the short version is that lacing in plus a full refinish is how additions stop looking like additions.

Wood Meeting Tile: Kitchens and Bathrooms

Tile is the transition partner that behaves — it doesn’t move seasonally like wood, its edge is straight, and the meeting point is usually a doorway where a line belongs anyway. The variable is height. When tile (plus its backer board) sits proud of the hardwood, the joint needs a reducer to ramp the difference; when the two surfaces land flush, a simple caulked or bordered joint can be nearly invisible. What we don’t do is sand a height difference away — thinning the wood side to chase tile height throws away wear layer you’ll want decades from now.

During a refinish, an existing wood-tile joint mostly needs protection and respect: we tape and shield the tile edge during sanding, and the new finish gets a crisp cut line at the seam. If you’re mid-decision about the kitchen itself, the wood-versus-tile question has its own trade-offs — underfoot comfort, dropped-dish mercy, water tolerance — that we walked through in hardwood vs tile in the kitchen. Either answer transitions fine; the mistake is deciding by accident.

Wood Meeting Carpet: Bedrooms and Stairs

Hardwood-to-carpet is the most common transition in the houses we work on, and it’s refreshingly forgiving: carpet compresses, so minor height differences vanish underfoot, and the joint hides under a tucked carpet edge at the bedroom door. During a refinish we sand cleanly up to the tack strip line and the carpet folds back over the meeting. One caution — carpet edges near sanding zones get taped and masked, because finish overspray on carpet fibers is forever.

The strategic question is whether the carpet stays. A lot of Redmond refinishing projects start as “do the main floor” and grow when owners pull a corner of bedroom carpet and find the same oak underneath. If there’s hardwood under your carpet, refinishing it in the same job as the main floor costs the same per foot, gets one consistent color through the doorways, and removes the transition entirely. If the carpet stays, we simply respect the line — but check before we sand, because doing that bedroom two years later means a second mobilization and a floor that will never quite match the first pass.

Heights, Toe-Catchers, and the Details That Age Well

The transition failures people live with daily are height failures: a proud metal strip in a hallway, a T-molding that drums under foot traffic, a quarter-inch step nobody warned the movers about. Good transitions get the heights right first and choose hardware second — and where finish-time decisions can help, we make them, like feathering a repair flat or setting a new flush threshold instead of re-using a worn ramped one. A refinish is also simply the right moment to fix bad transitions: the machines are there, the finish is open, and a replacement threshold gets coated with the floor so it matches forever.

The perimeter deserves the same attention. Sanding runs to the walls and into every doorway casing, and the trim either comes off or gets worked around — a choice with real trade-offs we covered in what happens to your baseboards during a refinish. Doorway casings, in particular, are where careless work shows: finish slopped on painted trim, or a visible sanding halo around a case that stayed. Ask any bidder how they handle casings and thresholds; the answer tells you how the whole job will go.

The Redmond Pattern: One Wood Field, Many Neighbors

Redmond’s housing stock makes transitions a predictable story. The 1990s–2000s builds on Education Hill and around Grass Lawn mostly follow one blueprint: hardwood through the entry, hall, and kitchen; carpet in the bedrooms and often the family room; tile in the baths and sometimes the laundry. That means a typical Redmond refinish is one connected wood field with six or eight transitions guarding its edges — a job that wants to be done as a single pass, with the doorway lines handled cleanly. The common upgrade we see: extending hardwood into the family room and lacing it in, which turns two floors and a T-molding into one floor and nothing.

Newer townhomes and the odd 1970s–80s rambler east of downtown add their own wrinkles — floating LVP additions that need T-moldings against real wood, and older homes where decades of remodels left three flooring generations meeting in one kitchen doorway. We see all of it doing hardwood floor refinishing in Redmond, and the estimate walk always includes the same lap of the house: every doorway, every seam, every height change, written into the scope before machines arrive. Transitions handled on paper cost nothing; transitions improvised on day three cost mornings.

Flooring Transitions: What Redmond Homeowners Ask

Can you refinish just one room of hardwood?
If that room ends at doors, carpet, or tile — yes, cleanly. If its floor flows continuously into other hardwood, stopping mid-field leaves a permanent visible line in color and sheen, and we’ll usually recommend doing the connected area as one job. Our 500 sq ft minimum also makes single small rooms uneconomical on their own.
Where exactly does a refinish stop at a doorway?
Under the closed door’s edge. With the door shut, each room sees only its own floor, so a color or sheen difference between rooms never shows. Doorways without doors — cased openings and archways — don’t offer that hiding place, which is why they make poor stopping points.
Will refinished hardwood match the hardwood in the next room if we skip that room?
No. Sanding removes oxidation and old amber, so a freshly refinished floor is cleaner and lighter than an unrefinished one of the same wood — even under identical finish. Through an open doorway the difference is obvious. Refinishing connected rooms together is how floors match.
What do you do where hardwood meets tile?
Protect the tile edge during sanding, then finish to a crisp line at the joint. If there’s a height difference, a reducer ramps it; if the surfaces are flush, the joint can be nearly invisible. We don’t sand wood thinner to chase tile height — that spends wear layer you’ll want later.
What about where hardwood meets carpet?
That’s the forgiving one. We sand up to the tack strip line, mask the carpet against dust and finish, and the tucked carpet edge hides the joint. If you suspect hardwood under the carpet, check before we sand — refinishing it in the same job costs the same per foot and matches perfectly.
Can you get rid of the metal strips between my rooms?
Often, yes. If both sides are solid hardwood at the same height, the strip can usually be replaced with a flush wood threshold — or eliminated entirely if the floors can be sanded and finished as one field. Strips have to stay where a floating floor needs room to move.
We added new hardwood next to old — will refinishing blend them?
That’s exactly what a full refinish is for. If the new boards were laced into the old field, sanding and finishing everything together makes the seam effectively disappear. If the two fields just butt together with a transition strip, refinishing improves color match but the joint itself remains.
Do transitions cost extra in a refinishing quote?
Standard doorway lines, carpet edges, and tile joints are part of the job at our regular rates — $3.99/sq ft for a natural-finish refinish. New thresholds, lacing in boards, or stair nosings are line items we price in the written scope, so you see them before work starts.
How long will the job take, and can we live here during it?
A typical refinish runs 3–5 days, and most families stay, living on the carpeted and tiled side of the transitions while the wood field is done. Waterborne finish takes sock traffic the same evening and furniture at 48–72 hours, so rooms come back online in sequence.
