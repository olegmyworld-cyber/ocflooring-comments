Why Assembly Floors Wear Differently Than House Floors

A living room floor wears in a lazy oval around the furniture. An assembly floor wears in hard, narrow lines, because everyone walks the same route. The center aisle of a sanctuary, the eight feet in front of the coffee urn, the path from the double doors to the stage, the strip in front of the kitchen pass-through — those lanes see traffic that a house hallway would take twenty years to accumulate, and the rest of the room looks nearly new. We’ve walked into halls where the finish was gone entirely along one traffic path and perfectly intact three feet away.

Then there is the furniture problem, which is specific to these buildings. Folding chairs are the single most destructive object commonly placed on a wood floor: eight to sixteen pounds each, dragged rather than lifted, with steel or hard-nylon feet that have usually lost their glides. Stacking racks are worse — a loaded rack rolls with a few hundred pounds on four small casters, and if one caster locks it plows. Six-foot banquet tables get dragged corner-first. None of this is anybody’s fault; it’s what happens when a room gets reset by volunteers twice a week for thirty years.

The third factor is what the floor is made of. Douglas fir, which is under a great many older Everett buildings, has a Janka hardness of 660 — less than half of red oak at 1290 and well under maple at 1450. Fir takes a chair-leg dent the way a hardcover book takes a thumbnail. That doesn’t mean fir floors can’t be restored beautifully; it means the maintenance interval is shorter and the expectations at handoff need to be honest ones, which is the same argument we make about refinishing old fir in houses.

The Two Services, and How to Tell Which One You Need

Almost every assembly floor question resolves into one of two jobs. A screen and recoat abrades the existing finish with a buffer and fine screen, then lays a fresh coat of waterborne over the top. It renews the film, it evens out the sheen, and it makes the room look dramatically better in a day — but it does not remove anything below the finish. Scratches that reached the wood stay. Stains stay. A full sand and refinish takes the floor down to raw wood with belt or drum equipment, works the edges by hand, flattens the whole surface with a Lägler TRIO pass, and builds two to three coats of new finish. It fixes everything a sander can reach, and it costs twice as much and takes most of a week.

The test is simple enough that a trustee can do it before we arrive. Kneel in the worst traffic lane with a flashlight held low. If the light skids across a continuous, if dull, film — you’re a recoat. If you see wood fiber that has gone gray, fuzzy, or noticeably lighter than the boards on either side, the finish is gone there and a recoat will not stick to it properly; you need a sand. Splash a tablespoon of water on that lane, too: if it beads for a minute, the film is intact; if it darkens the wood within seconds, it isn’t. A screen and recoat will hold you when…

  • The finish is dull and scuffed but continuous — water still beads in the aisle
  • Scratches are visible at a low angle but you can’t feel them with a fingernail
  • The last refinish was inside about eight years and nothing has flooded
  • You need the room back in one day, not one week
  • The building is phasing work and needs the cheap option to buy time

You’re past a recoat when…

  • Bare, gray, or fuzzy wood shows in the center aisle or entry
  • Chair-leg dents and drag marks have cut through into the boards
  • There are black stains from long-standing leaks or planter pots
  • Previous coats are peeling or flaking in sheets
  • The floor has been waxed at any point — wax and waterborne do not coexist

Pews, Stages, and the Things Bolted to Your Floor

Here is the honest scope conversation, because it is the one that surprises committees. We sand and finish floors; we are not a pew contractor. Fixed pews are typically lag-bolted through the finish flooring into the subfloor or joists, and getting a sander under and around them is impossible. That leaves three real options, and they cost very different amounts.

The first is to work around them: sand and finish the aisles, the chancel, and the perimeter, and leave the covered strips under the pews as they are. Nobody sees under a pew, and the color difference is invisible in service. This is what most congregations choose, and it is the cheapest honest answer. The second is to have a millwork or general contractor unbolt, remove, and re-set the pews so we can take the whole floor at once — a real expense on top of ours, plus the bolt holes need plugging, but it gives you one uniform floor and a chance to inspect the subfloor. The third, on buildings converting to flexible seating anyway, is to remove the pews permanently and treat the room as an open hall; then the floor is a straightforward big-room sand, and it’s the best value per square foot we can offer.

Stages get their own note. A raised platform is usually a different species and a different age than the room floor, and it has frequently been painted at some point in its life. Painted platforms are not a recoat candidate — paint has to come off entirely before finish goes on, and if the building is old enough that the paint may be lead-based, testing comes before sanding, full stop. Built-in altar rails, radiators, and floor vents all add hand-work time, the same way they do in houses with radiators and built-ins.

Building the Schedule Around Sunday

Every assembly building has a calendar it cannot move, and in our experience it is never just Sunday. There is the Tuesday AA meeting, the Wednesday choir rehearsal, the Thursday food bank, the preschool that runs weekday mornings in the classroom wing, and a wedding somebody booked eleven months ago. The scheduling work on these projects is genuinely harder than the sanding.

What makes it solvable is the finish chemistry. Waterborne systems from Bona and Pallmann recoat in two to three hours instead of the eight to twenty-four that oil-modified polyurethane demands, and they cure to sock traffic the same evening, furniture at 48–72 hours, and rugs at about seven days. Practically, that means a recoat can go down Thursday morning and the room is walkable Thursday night, chairs back Saturday, service Sunday. The same job in oil-modified poly would be one coat per day, a month of curing, and a building that smells like a paint store through two Sundays — which is why we don’t propose it for occupied assembly space, and why the low-VOC argument matters even more here than it does in homes with kids and pets.

Two more scheduling levers. First, phasing: there is no rule that says the whole building goes at once. Sanctuary in the spring, fellowship hall in the fall, classroom wing next budget year — each phase is its own contract, and each one has to clear the 500 sq ft minimum on its own. Second, containment: if any part of the building stays occupied while we sand, dust containment runs $250 and is not optional in our book when a nursery or commercial kitchen shares air handling with the work area. We wrote up how that works, including sealing returns, in our guide to HVAC and dust control during refinishing.

What Everett Buildings Actually Have Underfoot

Everett grew as a mill town, and its older assembly buildings were built when Douglas fir was the cheapest structural and finish lumber within a mile of the water. In the church and lodge halls we see around downtown, Rucker Hill, and the Riverside and Bayside neighborhoods, the floor is very often vertical-grain old-growth fir — tight rings, remarkable color, and soft enough that a century of folding chairs has written its history into the surface. Those floors are worth restoring and worth restoring carefully; the grain in old Everett fir is finer than anything being milled today, and once you sand a fir floor thin, you don’t get the thickness back. We usually push for the least aggressive option that solves the problem, the same argument we make about old-growth fir in Ballard and Queen Anne.

The mid-century wave — parish halls, fraternal lodges, and community rooms built through the 1950s and 60s as Everett expanded east — tends toward red oak strip or northern hard maple, and those are the easiest floors in this entire category. Maple at 1450 Janka takes chair abuse well, sands flat beautifully, and holds a commercial waterborne for years. Then there are the buildings around Silver Lake and the Casino Road corridor from the 1970s onward, where a “wood floor” is as likely to be engineered plank or a floating system, and the first thing we do is check wear-layer thickness before anyone promises a sand — the same measurement question we cover for engineered floors generally. And scattered across the county are the granges and community halls, most of them fir over a crawlspace, most of them with a moisture story to read before we quote. Church & Community Hall Floors: What Building Committees Ask Us

Can you do the work between Sunday services?
For a screen and recoat, yes — that is the normal pattern. We come in Monday through Thursday, finish the coat by afternoon, and the room takes sock traffic the same evening and chairs by Saturday. A full sand needs 3 to 5 days on the floor, so it takes one full week out of the building calendar, but it still lands you back in service the following Sunday.
Will the building smell during and after the work?
There is a mild odor during application that clears within hours. The waterborne systems we use run around 65 g/L VOC and Bona Traffic HD is GREENGUARD Gold certified, which is why we can coat a room on Thursday and hand it to a nursery on Sunday. Oil-modified polyurethane, at roughly 450 to 550 g/L, would still be off-gassing weeks later, so we don't propose it for occupied assembly buildings.
What happens to the floor under the pews?
Unless the pews are removed, that flooring stays as it is. We sand and finish the aisles, chancel and perimeter, and the covered strips keep their old finish. Nobody sees under a pew and the difference is invisible in service. If your building is converting to flexible seating anyway, removing the pews first gets you a uniform floor and the best price per square foot we can offer.
Our hall floor has folding-chair scars everywhere. Is a recoat pointless?
It depends whether the scars broke the finish or the wood. Run a fingernail across one: if it catches, the scratch is into the board and only sanding removes it. If it doesn't catch, a recoat will fill and hide most of it. Many halls we look at have a mix, and the practical answer is a recoat now with a sand budgeted for a later year.
Is there a minimum job size?
Yes, 500 square feet per job. Most sanctuaries and fellowship halls clear that several times over, but a small classroom, a choir loft, or an entry alone may not. If you are phasing work across budget years, we can group small rooms into one visit so each phase clears the minimum instead of paying a small-job premium.
Can you match a new floor to an existing hall floor?
Sometimes closely, rarely perfectly. Species, age, prior finish and sun exposure all move the color, and old fir in particular has ambered for decades in a way new lumber has not. What we can do reliably is sand and finish both together so they share the same finish and sheen, which reads as intentional even when the tone differs slightly between the old and the new boards.
Do you carry insurance and can you provide documentation?
Yes. We're licensed and insured and can furnish certificates for a facilities or trustee file before work starts, along with a line-item written scope so a board can review it without needing anything translated. We've been doing this since 2013 and have finished more than 1,000 floors.
How often should a hall floor be recoated?
For a room seeing weekly assembly use, plan on every three to five years, and put it in the maintenance budget the way you would a roof inspection. Recoating on that cycle costs a fraction of a full sand, keeps the wood itself out of the sander, and means the floor never reaches the state where the cheap option is off the table.
What if our floor has been waxed?
Tell us before we quote, because it changes everything. Waterborne finish will not bond over wax residue, and a recoat over a waxed floor peels. Waxed floors have to be sanded to bare wood, which moves the project straight to the full-refinish price. A quick solvent test on an inconspicuous spot tells us in a minute, and we'd rather find out at the estimate than on coating day.
