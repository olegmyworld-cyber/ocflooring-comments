The Landlord’s Version of a Good Floor

Change the objective function and every spec decision changes with it. A homeowner is optimizing for how the floor looks in their own light on a Saturday morning. A landlord is optimizing for cost per year of service, plus vacancy days, plus the number of arguments about the security deposit. That leads somewhere specific:

Forgiveness beats perfection. A floor that hides a scuff is worth more than a floor that photographs beautifully and shows every heel mark by the third month of a lease. Repairability beats uniqueness. Anything you can’t touch up or partially recoat becomes a whole-floor job the moment it gets damaged. Schedule beats sticker price. A cheaper finish that costs you five extra vacant days is not cheaper. And the floor should outlive the fashion. Gray floors were mandatory in 2018 and dated by 2024; natural wood has never been out of style in a rental listing.

Almost everything below is a consequence of those four sentences.

The Recoat Cycle Is the Whole Strategy

Here’s the piece most owners miss, and it’s worth more than every other paragraph on this page. A finish is a sacrificial layer. As long as there’s still finish between shoes and wood, the floor is fine and a $1.99/sq ft screen and recoat resets it: we abrade the existing coating, clean, and lay a fresh coat over the top. One day, no stain decisions, no bare wood, minimal dust.

The moment the finish wears through in the traffic lanes, dirt gets into open wood fiber, the lanes go gray, and no coating fixes that from above — you’re into a full sand at $3.99/sq ft, and you’ve spent a slice of the floor’s remaining life. A solid ¾-inch floor only has so many sandings in it, a topic we cover in how many times a hardwood floor can be refinished. Recoats are unlimited. Sands are not.

So the landlord’s calendar looks like this: recoat on turnover when the floor looks tired but the finish is intact, and sand only when it has genuinely worn through or you need a color change. Two or three recoat cycles between sands is realistic on a well-specced floor, and each one buys years. Our breakdown of whether recoating is worth it — and what delaying costs runs the arithmetic, and the mechanical difference between the two services is spelled out in sand and refinish vs screen and recoat.

The failure mode is always the same: an owner defers because the unit is occupied and the tenant hasn’t complained, then defers again, and the recoat window closes. There’s no way to buy it back.

Where to Spend and Where to Stop

Worth paying for in a rental

  • Commercial two-component waterborne — the finish is the cheapest place to buy durability
  • Satin sheen, every time
  • Lacing in damaged boards properly before the finish goes on
  • Refinishing the stairs with the floor instead of a year later
  • A recoat on turnover, while the finish is still intact

Skip it in a rental

  • Dark stain — it shows dust, scratches lighter wood underneath, and costs $6.50/sq ft
  • Matte and high-gloss sheens — one shows smears, the other shows scratches
  • Fashion colors (gray, white-wash) that will date before the floor wears
  • Extending hardwood into laundry rooms and full baths
  • Waiting to see whether the next tenant is hard on floors

Everett Rentals: What the Housing Stock Actually Gives You

Everett is one of the better rental markets in Snohomish County for hardwood, because so much of the stock has real wood under it. The pre-war and mill-era neighborhoods — Bayside, Riverside, the streets climbing up from Rucker and Colby — are full of 1900s–1940s bungalows and craftsman houses with old-growth fir or narrow oak strip, much of it split into duplexes and upstairs units decades ago. That wood is usually thick, tight, and worth saving; it also tends to be old enough that a previous owner already sanded it once or twice, so the recoat-versus-sand discipline matters more than average.

The second group is the postwar and Boeing-era housing spreading south and east — the ramblers and split-entries around Silver Lake, Evergreen Way, and the Casino Road corridor, built from the 1950s through the 1980s, red oak strip under carpet in a lot of cases. Those floors often come out of carpet in good condition and take a natural waterborne beautifully. They’re also the units most likely to turn over on a tight schedule, which is where the one-day recoat earns its keep. Whatever the vintage, we quote hardwood floor refinishing in Everett the same way: what does this floor need, and what’s the cheapest thing that keeps it out of trouble for the next five years.

Two local realities to plan around. First, Everett winters are wet and long, and tenants track that in — entry mats and a strict no-shoes-past-the-entry note in the lease do more for a rental floor than any upgrade you can buy. Second, a lot of these buildings have basement and garage-level conversions where hardwood was never a good idea; if a lower unit has moisture history, that’s a floor to replace rather than refinish, and we’ll say so at the estimate rather than take the job.

Refinish, or Replace It With Vinyl Plank?

We’re a refinishing company and we’ll still tell you: some rental floors should be replaced, not saved. If the wood is worn through in the lanes and has already been sanded to the nail heads, if there’s water damage under a kitchen or a laundry stack, or if it’s a thin-wear-layer engineered product installed in the 2000s, luxury vinyl plank is a legitimate answer for a rental. It’s waterproof, it’s scratch-tolerant, and a damaged plank swaps out.

But when there’s solid wood with life left in it, the math is lopsided. Refinishing runs $3.99/sq ft natural; new installation is $3–$4.25/sq ft in labor plus $4–$10 or more per square foot in material, before demo and disposal. That’s the comparison we lay out in is it cheaper to refinish or replace, and for a landlord the gap is even wider than for a homeowner, because refinishing also takes fewer days off the calendar. Investors turning a whole unit at once may also want our notes on flooring decisions on a fix-and-flip — the priorities overlap more than you’d think.

Scheduling Around a Vacancy

The vacancy is the expensive part, so plan the floor around the calendar rather than the other way around. A screen and recoat is one day, plus a night for the finish to harden — a unit can be re-shown almost immediately. A full sand with natural finish runs 3–5 days including the finish schedule; add a day if we’re staining. With our waterborne systems the floor takes sock traffic the same evening, furniture at 48–72 hours, and rugs at seven days, which means a tenant can generally move in within a few days of the last coat. An oil-modified polyurethane, by contrast, wants a day per coat and a 30-day cure — a schedule almost no rental turn can absorb.

Practical details: our minimum job is 500 sq ft, so a small one-bedroom is usually best combined with the hall and stairs or scheduled alongside another unit in the same building. Moving a washer-dryer pair is $160, other appliances $80 each. Dust containment is $250 and is usually unnecessary in an empty unit — worth every penny in an occupied one. If the unit is still occupied and you’re doing the work between leases on paper only, read refinishing rental hardwood between tenants first; the sequencing there is what keeps a turn from slipping a week.

One last thing worth putting in the lease conversation rather than the scope: pet policy. Nail scratches from an enthusiastic dog are the most common rental floor damage we see, and they’re recoatable right up until the finish wears through. Rental Floor Refinishing: What Everett Landlords Ask Us

What finish holds up best in a rental?
A two-component commercial waterborne — Bona Traffic HD or the Pallmann equivalent — in satin. It's the hardest-wearing film available for residential floors, it's low-odor so a unit can be re-occupied fast, and it recoats cleanly years later. The finish is the cheapest place in the whole job to buy durability, and skimping there is what turns a five-year floor into a three-year floor.
Should I stain rental floors dark?
We'd advise against it. Dark stain costs $6.50 per square foot instead of $3.99, adds a day to the schedule, shows dust and pet hair constantly, and makes every scratch glow lighter where it cuts through to the wood below. It also has to be color-matched on any future repair. Natural wood costs less, hides more, and never goes out of style in a listing photo.
How often should a rental floor be recoated?
Treat it as a turnover item and inspect it every time the unit is empty. If the finish looks dull and scratched but hasn't worn through anywhere, that's a $1.99 per square foot screen and recoat and it should happen now. If you can see bare or gray wood in the traffic lanes, the recoat window has closed and you're into a full sand.
Can I recoat with a tenant still living there?
Usually yes, with waterborne finish — it's one day, low odor, and the floor takes sock traffic that evening. The practical problem is furniture: everything has to come off the floor, which is a lot to ask mid-lease. Most owners find it easier to schedule it into a turnover, when the unit is empty anyway.
How many times can I sand a rental floor before it's done?
It depends on thickness and how much wood previous sandings took, but a solid ¾-inch floor typically has several sandings in its life and old Everett floors have often used one or two already. That's the argument for the recoat cycle: recoats don't consume wood at all, so every recoat you do is a sanding you don't have to do.
Is vinyl plank a better choice for a rental than hardwood?
It's the better choice when the wood is finished — worn through, water damaged, or a thin engineered product. It's the worse choice when there's solid wood with life in it, because refinishing at $3.99 per square foot beats installation labor of $3 to $4.25 plus $4 to $10 or more in material. Existing hardwood is also a genuine listing advantage in most Everett neighborhoods.
How fast can a tenant move in after refinishing?
With our waterborne systems: socks the same evening, furniture at 48 to 72 hours, area rugs at seven days. A full refinish takes 3 to 5 days of work, so a realistic turn is under a week from start to move-in. Oil-modified polyurethane would need a day between coats and a 30-day cure, which is why we don't recommend it for rentals.
Do you work with owners who have several units?
Yes, and there's real efficiency in it — scheduling two units in the same building back to back, or timing recoats across a portfolio so we're not remobilizing for 500 square feet at a time. Our minimum job is 500 square feet, which is easier to meet when work is grouped. Tell us the portfolio at the estimate and we'll sequence it.
