The Real Timeline, Working Backward From a Date

Start from the immovable date — closing, photos, or the moving truck — and count backward. A full refinish is 3–5 working days of sanding, coating, and curing between coats. The finish takes sock traffic the same evening it goes down, shoes and furniture at 48–72 hours, and rugs at seven days. So a floor that must be furniture-ready on the 15th needs its final coat by the 12th, which means starting no later than the 7th, which — allowing for scheduling lead time and the fact that we’re usually booked one to three weeks out — means calling us the day the need appears, not the week of. A screen & recoat compresses all of that to a single working day plus an overnight, which is exactly why it’s the workhorse of real-estate flooring; the full decision logic between the two lives in our refinishing process timeline guide.

One deadline peculiarity of sale floors: they must look their best empty. An occupied-home refinish can hide the odd compromise under furniture; a floor that will be photographed bare, walked by an appraiser, and inspected by a buyer’s agent at the final walkthrough has nowhere to hide. Build the schedule so the finish has its 48–72 hours of hardening before anyone with a clipboard or a camera arrives.

Seller Side: The Inspection-Response Scramble

The common seller scenario: the inspection flagged the floors, or the buyer’s offer came with a flooring credit request, and now there’s a negotiated agreement to refinish before closing — typically with two to four weeks to do it, in a house that may still contain the seller’s entire life. Three honest options, in order of preference. First: if the move-out happens before closing anyway, schedule the refinish for the empty week between the truck and the signing — clean access, no furniture shuffle, best result. Second: if the house stays occupied, a room-by-room refinish is possible — we do them regularly, and the playbook is in our occupied-home guide — but it adds days, so it needs the full negotiation window, started immediately. Third: if the dates genuinely don’t work, talk to your agent about converting the repair into a credit. An honest contractor tells you when the calendar has already decided; a credit that closes on time beats a rushed refinish that muddies the walkthrough.

And a word on scope: sellers under deadline are the number-one audience for the screen & recoat. If the floors are dull and lightly scratched rather than worn to wood, one day at $1.99/sq ft delivers most of the visual transformation for a fraction of the schedule risk — the honest breakdown is in the cheapest floor upgrade that satisfies an inspection.

Buyer Side: The Golden Window

If you’re the buyer, you hold the best refinishing card in real estate: the empty days between closing and move-in. The house is yours, it contains nothing, and nobody is living on the floors while they cure. This is when refinishing is cheapest in disruption, fastest in execution, and best in quality — no furniture moves to pay for ($80–$160 per appliance when we do move them), no room-by-room sequencing, no compromises around beds and sofas. A full sand, even with stain at $6.50/sq ft, fits comfortably in a week; a natural refinish at $3.99 fits with days to spare. We book these from mutual acceptance with a start date keyed to your closing — and if closing slips a day or two, as Kirkland closings occasionally do, a job in an empty house reschedules far more gracefully than one wedged inside someone’s move.

The single most common buyer mistake: scheduling the movers for the day after closing and the floors “sometime after we settle in.” Once furniture is in, you’ve traded the golden window for an occupied-home project — more logistics for the same floors. If the floors are on your list at all, push the truck one week.

The Mistakes That Blow Up Closing Week

A short field guide to the disasters we’re asked to prevent (or repair). Rugs too early: the stager’s rugs go down on day three, and the seven-day rule turns them into permanent sheen ghosts on a brand-new finish. Photos too early: listing photos shot the morning after the final coat, with the photographer’s shoes doing the walking — finish takes socks that evening, not boots at 8 a.m. The walkthrough surprise: a buyer’s final walkthrough scheduled while the house still smells faintly of finish work invites questions no seller wants; with the low-VOC waterborne systems we run (~65 g/L, versus 450+ for oil poly), the smell is minimal and short-lived, but even minimal is worth a day’s buffer. The mystery contractor: a bargain crew booked for speed who takes shortcuts a buyer’s inspector then finds — the floor version of paying twice. Deadline jobs are precisely where the boring virtues — licensed, insured, warrantied, on schedule since 2013 — stop being marketing and start being risk management.

Refinishing on a Sale Deadline: What Clients Ask Us

Can you refinish floors while we're under contract on the sale?
Yes — it's one of the most common jobs we do. A full refinish needs 3 to 5 working days plus 48 to 72 hours before furniture; a screen and recoat needs one day. The requirement is calling early: we're typically booked one to three weeks out, so the schedule works when it's built the week the contract is signed.
How close to closing can the work realistically happen?
The final coat should be down at least three days before anyone important walks through — appraiser, photographer, or buyer's final walkthrough. Counting backward, a full refinish should start no later than eight to ten days before closing. A one-day recoat can run later, but even that deserves a buffer day.
We're buyers. Should we refinish before or after moving in?
Before, without question, in the window between closing and move-in. The house is empty, the finish cures undisturbed, there are no furniture moves to pay for, and the job runs its fastest. Push the moving truck back one week and the whole project fits inside it.
The inspection response gives us three weeks. Is that enough?
Usually, yes — if scheduling starts now. Three weeks accommodates our booking lead time plus a full refinish with margin, even in an occupied house done room by room. What three weeks does not accommodate is two weeks of deciding followed by one week of hoping.
Is a screen and recoat enough to satisfy a buyer or inspector?
If the floors are dull and finish-worn rather than damaged to bare wood, yes — a recoat restores uniform sheen and erases light scratching, which is what photographs and walkthroughs actually show. If there are gray traffic lanes, water marks, or exposed wood, only a full sand fixes it, and an inspector can tell the difference.
What happens if closing slips after we've scheduled the refinish?
In an empty house, usually nothing dramatic — we adjust the start date. This is one more argument for doing the work in the vacant window rather than wedging it against the move: an empty-house job reschedules gracefully; a job coordinated with movers and stagers does not.
When can stagers put rugs down on the new finish?
Seven days after the final coat — the waterborne finish needs that week to cure, and rugs laid early can leave permanent sheen differences. Furniture on felt pads can come back at 48 to 72 hours. Build the staging date from the final-coat date, not the start date.
Will the house smell like finish at the final walkthrough?
Barely, with the systems we use — our waterborne finishes run around 65 g/L VOC versus 450-plus for traditional oil poly, and the light odor fades within a day or two. We still recommend finishing the coating at least two or three days before a walkthrough, because 'faint and explainable' is worse than 'nothing at all.'
Can you coordinate directly with our agent?
Yes, and it usually helps — agents think in the same backward-from-closing logic we do. We'll confirm scope and dates in writing so the inspection-response paperwork can reference a real schedule from a licensed, insured contractor, and we've been keeping those dates since 2013.
