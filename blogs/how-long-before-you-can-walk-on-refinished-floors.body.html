“When can we walk on it?” is the second question at every estimate, right after the price, and it deserves a better answer than the shrug-and-a-range most contractors give. The honest answer has two parts. First: it depends almost entirely on finish chemistry — waterborne and oil-based finishes live on different calendars, and the difference is days versus weeks. Second: “walk on it” is really five separate questions — socks, shoes, furniture, rugs, and dogs — and each has its own clock.

Everything below assumes the commercial waterborne systems (Bona and Pallmann) we run on nearly every job, with the oil-based numbers alongside for contrast. If you’re still deciding between the two chemistries, our water-based vs oil-based comparison is the place to start — this post is about what happens after you’ve chosen.

Why the Wait: Dry Is Not Cured

A finish is “dry” when the water or solvent has evaporated and it no longer feels tacky. It’s “cured” when the chemistry has finished cross-linking into its final, hardest form — and those are very different days on the calendar. Waterborne finishes dry in hours and cure in about a week; oil-modified poly dries overnight and keeps curing (and off-gassing) for a month. In between, the film is like a cast that hasn’t fully set: fine for gentle use, vulnerable to concentrated pressure. That’s why socks are fine on night one but a 200-pound bookcase isn’t until day three, and why a rug on day two is the classic self-inflicted wound — it seals off air the curing film still needs and can leave a permanent ghost of itself in the sheen.

The good news is that the waterborne cure is boringly predictable indoors. Normal household heat covers it, even in a Redmond January — unlike oil finishes, whose dry times stretch badly in cool, damp Northwest air.

The Full Project Clock, Not Just the Last Coat

The walk-on question usually hides a bigger one: how long is my house a job site? Moving Back In Without Wrecking Week One

Most finish damage we get called about didn’t happen during the job — it happened during move-back. The rules are simple and they’re all about pressure and dragging. Furniture gets carried, never slid, and lands on felt pads from day one; a felt pad turns a chair leg from a chisel into a slipper. Appliances ride on protective runners — we handle these moves ourselves on request, $160 for a washer/dryer pair and $80 for other appliances, partly because refrigerator wheels have ended more new finishes than any dog ever has. Rugs wait the full week, and so do rubber-backed mats in particular. Dogs are fine in the house from day one — it’s the sprint-and-skid on day two we ask you to manage for a few days. Walking on Refinished Floors: What Redmond Homeowners Ask

How soon can we walk on the floors after the final coat?
With the waterborne finishes we use, in clean socks about 2 to 3 hours after the final coat — the same evening. Shoes and normal traffic are fine at 24 to 48 hours. With oil-based polyurethane, wait a full day before careful sock traffic.
When can furniture go back?
At 48 to 72 hours for waterborne finishes — carried into place, never dragged, with felt pads under everything. Oil-based finishes need 4 to 7 days. Concentrated pressure is the test furniture poses, and it's a harder test than footsteps.
Why do rugs have to wait a week?
The finish is still curing, and a rug both blocks airflow the curing film needs and can bond or leave a sheen ghost on soft finish. Rubber-backed mats are the worst offenders. At day 7 on waterborne — day 30 on oil — rugs are safe.
Can our dog be in the house during and after the job?
Yes. Low-VOC waterborne finishes make it fine for pets to be home the same day. What we ask is a few days without full-speed sprinting and skidding — claws on day-two finish can scratch what day-ten finish would shrug off.
Can we sleep at home the night a coat goes down?
With our waterborne systems, most clients do — the finishes run around 65 g/L VOC, a fraction of oil-based products. With oil-modified poly we recommend staying elsewhere during coating days, especially for kids, pregnancy, asthma, or birds.
Does the time of year change these timelines?
Indoors with normal heat, waterborne cure times barely move year-round — one of the reasons we run them in the Northwest. Oil-based dry times stretch significantly in cool, damp weather, which can add days between coats from October through May.
How long is the whole project, start to finish?
A typical full refinish is 3 to 5 days of work: sanding, then 2 to 3 coats with hours between them. Add the furniture window and you're at about a week to fully normal. A screen and recoat is one day of work plus the same overnight walk-window.
What happens if we put furniture back too early?
Legs sink in and leave permanent impressions, pads stick, and sliding anything shears the soft film — damage that can't be buffed out, only refinished out. When in doubt, wait the extra day; the finish spends years paying you back for it.
Does sheen choice change the timeline?
No — matte, satin, and semi-gloss in the same product line dry and cure on the same schedule. Sheen changes how the floor looks and how it hides wear, not when you can walk on it.
