Quick answer

A curbless shower is not a regular shower minus the curb — it is a bathroom floor rebuilt so the slope happens below floor level. The floor has to be opened and recessed so the pan can fall toward the drain and still meet the rest of the room flush; a linear drain usually makes that geometry work; and the waterproofing has to extend far beyond the shower, because without a curb there is no line where water agrees to stop. It is the best-looking, safest shower you can build and the most structural tile project in the house — which is exactly why quotes for it vary so wildly.

What “Curbless” Actually Means Structurally

Every shower floor must fall about a quarter inch per foot toward its drain. In a standard shower, that slope sits on top of the bathroom floor, and the curb is the retaining wall that hides the height difference. Delete the curb and the arithmetic does not go away — the slope now has to happen below the level of the surrounding floor, so that the shower’s high edge lands flush with the bathroom tile. There are only three ways to get there, and all of them are structural work before they are tile work.

Recess the framing. In a wood-framed floor — which is most Mercer Island houses — we open the floor and drop the shower zone: furring the joists lower, heading off and re-supporting the bay, or sistering alongside with a stepped profile, depending on which way the joists run and what lives below the bathroom. Build the rest of the room up. When the framing cannot be touched — a beam in the wrong place, a finished ceiling below that must survive — the bathroom floor outside the shower is raised instead, which cascades into door heights and hallway transitions and needs equally careful planning. Cut the slab. On a slab-on-grade floor the recess is ground or saw-cut into the concrete — noisy, dusty, and in some ways the most straightforward of the three. Which path your bathroom takes is decided by the structure, not by preference, and it is the first thing we look at on a site visit.

The Linear Drain: Why It Changed the Math

The traditional center drain needs the floor to slope toward it from four directions — a shallow bowl, tiled in small mosaic because only small tile can follow a compound curve. A linear drain — a long, narrow channel set against a wall or across the shower entrance — replaces the bowl with a single flat plane tilting one direction. That one change is most of why curbless showers stopped being exotic: a single plane is easier to frame accurately into a recess, easier to waterproof, and it accepts the large-format porcelain everyone actually wants underfoot, cut to size instead of chopped into mosaic.

Placement matters more than brand. A drain against the far wall pulls water away from the entry, which is where you want it heading; a drain across the threshold catches everything at the boundary and lets the main bathroom floor stay dead flat, at the cost of a more exposed detail underfoot. Both work; the geometry of the room usually picks. What does not change is that the drain body, its flange, and the membrane have to be one integrated, tested system — the drain is the only hole in the most waterproofed floor in your house, and it earns proportionate attention.

Waterproofing Does Not Stop at an Invisible Line

A curb is not just a step — it is a legal boundary for water. Remove it and water will explore: sheeting off a body, flicking off an elbow, riding the squeegee. A curbless bathroom is therefore designed as a wet room lite: the bonded membrane runs across the entire shower, out under the surrounding bathroom floor for a meaningful distance, and up the walls at the perimeter. Heated floors pair naturally with this build — the whole floor is already open, and warm tile dries standing water faster than any fan. The layer-by-layer logic of what goes under the tile is the same one we walk through in our underlayment guide, with the volume turned up: on a curbless job, the assembly under the tile is the project.

This is also why the classic remodel shortcut — leave the old floor, work around it — simply does not exist here. There is no version of a curbless build that does not open the floor, which is the same honesty we bring to tiling over existing tile: some jobs allow shortcuts, and this one structurally cannot. Budget accordingly or build a curbed shower well; both are respectable, and only one of them is sold as the other.

What It Costs, and When a Curb Is the Honest Answer

Tile work runs $14–$26 per square foot installed with labor from $11, and a standard shower already lives at the top of that range because the waterproofing assembly is most of the labor. A curbless build sits above the top, because the structural work — opening the floor, the recess carpentry, sometimes an engineer’s sign-off — is real scope that a per-square-foot number cannot honestly contain, and we price it as its own line rather than smearing it into the tile rate. What moves the number most is your framing: joists running the convenient direction with accessible depth make for a clean recess; a beam under the shower or a finished ceiling below adds complexity that deserves daylight at the estimate, not discovery at demo. And the honest counterweight: when the structure fights hard and the budget is finite, a low-curb shower built impeccably — proper pre-slope, bonded membrane, flood test, a 3–4 inch curb — delivers ninety percent of the experience for meaningfully less money and risk. We would rather build you that, well, than a compromised curbless shower that fakes its slope in mortar. Curbless Showers: What Homeowners Ask Us

What is a curbless shower, exactly?
A shower with no raised threshold — the bathroom floor flows into the shower at one level, and the slope that drains the water happens below floor level, built into the structure. That is the defining difference from a standard shower, where the slope sits on top of the floor and a curb hides the height. Curbless means the floor itself was rebuilt to make the geometry work.
Do curbless showers leak or flood the bathroom?
Not when they are built as designed — the waterproofing membrane extends across the shower, out into the room, and up the perimeter walls precisely because there is no curb to stop wandering water. The failures you hear about come from curbless looks without curbless engineering: slope faked in mortar, membrane stopping at an invisible line, no flood test. The build is unforgiving of shortcuts, which is an argument for the right builder, not against the shower.
Does a curbless shower require opening up the floor?
In almost every wood-framed home, yes — the floor is opened so the shower bay can be recessed and re-supported, or the surrounding floor is built up instead, which has its own cascade of consequences. On a concrete slab, the recess is cut or ground into the concrete. There is no honest version that leaves the existing floor untouched; the slope has to come from the structure.
Why do curbless showers use linear drains?
Because a linear drain lets the whole floor slope as one flat plane toward a single edge, instead of a four-way bowl toward a center drain. One plane is easier to build accurately into a recess, easier to waterproof, and it accepts large-format tile — the four-way bowl only accepts small mosaic that can follow the curve. Point drains still work in curbless builds; linear drains just make the geometry and the aesthetics dramatically easier.
How much does a curbless shower cost compared to a standard one?
Plan on a meaningful premium over an already-premium standard shower. Tile work runs $14–$26 per square foot installed, showers sit at the top of that, and curbless adds structural scope — opening the floor, recess framing, extended waterproofing — that honest contractors price as its own line. The exact premium depends on your framing; joist direction and what sits below the bathroom decide more than any design choice.
Is a curbless shower worth it for aging in place?
It is the single best bathroom investment for staying in a home long-term. A zero-threshold entry serves every age and mobility level without ever looking medical, and while the walls are open you can add grab-bar blocking and a hand shower for almost nothing. For Mercer Island households planning in decades, it removes the most common reason a beloved house eventually stops working for its owners.
Can any bathroom be made curbless?
Most can, but not all at the same cost. The friendly cases: joists running the right direction with depth to spare, a crawl space below, or a slab that can be cut. The expensive cases: a beam under the shower, a finished ceiling below that cannot be disturbed, or a structure that forces the whole room to be built up instead. A site visit that looks below the floor answers it in twenty minutes — before anyone commits to a number.
Do you still flood test a curbless shower?
Absolutely — more carefully than a curbed one, because a leak here has no curb slowing it down. The finished pan is plugged and holds water for 24 hours before any tile goes on, and we photograph the test and keep it on record. On any shower, curbless especially, a builder who will not flood test is asking you to take on faith the one thing that can be cheaply proven.
