One Material, Two Grades: What Actually Differs

Walk a tile aisle and the signage suggests two different species. Chemically, porcelain and ceramic are the same family: clay, shaped and kiln-fired. Porcelain is what happens when the recipe is tightened — finer, denser clays pressed harder and fired hotter, until the body vitrifies into something close to glass. The practical definition is the absorption test: a tile that takes on 0.5% or less of its weight in water qualifies as porcelain; standard ceramic absorbs several times that. Everything else people say about the two — durability, frost resistance, price, weight, how they behave under a wet saw — flows downstream from that one number.

Density buys three real things. Strength: porcelain resists cracking and chipping under impact better, and its hard surface shrugs off the grit that slowly sands softer glazes dull in entryways. Water indifference: a body that cannot soak up water cannot be damaged by it — or by the freeze-thaw cycles that split water-logged ceramic on a Pacific Northwest porch. Consistency through the body: many porcelains are colored through their thickness or glazed over a matching body, so a chip — when it eventually happens — shows a similar color underneath instead of a white scar. Ceramic’s softer body is not a defect; it is a different point on the same dial, and in the right rooms it is the better buy.

Where Ceramic Is the Smart Money

Ceramic’s case is simple: on a wall, nearly everything porcelain is better at never happens. Nobody walks on a backsplash. Nothing freezes in a powder room. A tub surround’s waterproofing lives in the membrane behind the tile, not in the tile body — the same layer logic we lay out in our underlayment guide. In those locations, ceramic’s lower price is pure savings, its lighter weight is friendlier to vertical installation, and its softer body cuts faster and cleaner — which shows up as less labor time on fussy, cut-heavy work like outlets and edges. Many of the most charming handmade and hand-glazed looks — zellige, artisan subway — are only made as ceramic anyway. Spending porcelain money on a backsplash is not wrong; it is just buying capability the wall will never use.

Where Porcelain Earns Its Premium

Floors, first and always. A floor takes point loads, grit abrasion, water, and the occasional cast-iron pan from counter height, and porcelain’s density is the difference between a floor that shrugs and a glaze that slowly records every insult. Wet rooms, second: the tile is not your waterproofing, but a body that ignores water is welcome margin in a room full of it. Entries and mudrooms, third — in our climate they are honorary wet rooms from October to May. And anything outdoors is porcelain by rule, not preference: absorbent tile plus a Renton freeze is a split tile by spring. Add the market reality that wood-look planks and big-format slabs — the looks driving most of today’s remodels — are made almost exclusively in porcelain, and the premium usually buys itself. The Installer Matters More Than the Material

Now the paragraph this article exists for. In the failed tile jobs we get called to fix, the tile itself is almost never the culprit. Cracked floors trace to substrates that flexed or were never flattened. Leaking showers trace to membranes that were skipped or botched — the reason we flood-test every pan for 24 hours and photograph it. Popped tiles trace to mortar coverage a tap test would have caught in one visit, the same test we run before deciding whether an old floor can even be tiled over. Premium porcelain set on a bad assembly fails exactly as fast as bargain ceramic, only more expensively.

The material choice does change the installation, though, and this is the honest reason porcelain costs more to install: its hardness cuts both ways. It is miserable to cut without proper saws and diamond blades, unforgiving of a substrate that is not flat — especially in the large formats — and it demands the right mortar and real back-buttering technique. A bargain installer with porcelain is a worse outcome than a careful one with ceramic. If the budget forces a choice, choose the installer, then let the room’s demands choose the tile — that ordering is the whole secret, and it is free.

Porcelain vs Ceramic: What Homeowners Ask Us

What is the actual difference between porcelain and ceramic tile?
Porcelain is a denser grade of ceramic — finer clay, pressed harder, fired hotter — defined by absorbing 0.5% or less of its weight in water, where standard ceramic absorbs several times that. The density makes porcelain stronger, effectively waterproof as a body, and frost-safe. Every practical difference between the two flows from that one absorption number.
Is porcelain tile worth the extra money?
On floors, in wet rooms, at entries, and anywhere outdoors — yes, and the premium is smaller than most people expect at the material level. On walls and backsplashes, usually not: nothing there tests the qualities porcelain charges for, and ceramic delivers the same decades of service for less. The honest rule is to let the room’s demands decide, not the showroom hierarchy.
Can you use ceramic tile in a shower?
On the walls, yes — the waterproofing in a properly built shower lives in the membrane behind the tile, not in the tile body, so glazed ceramic walls over a real membrane are a legitimate assembly. We still prefer porcelain where water is constant, for the extra margin. On the shower floor, porcelain: it takes the traffic, and pans are where you want zero compromises.
Do porcelain tiles crack more easily than ceramic?
The opposite — porcelain is the stronger, denser body and resists cracking and chipping better. When porcelain floors do crack, the cause is almost always underneath: a subfloor that flexes, a hump that was never flattened, or missing mortar coverage under a corner. Tile of either kind is only as strong as the assembly below it, which is why the installer matters more than the material.
What does a PEI rating mean when I am comparing tiles?
PEI grades the glaze’s wear resistance, from 1 (walls only) to 5 (heavy commercial traffic). For a home floor, PEI 3 or higher is the practical bar, and most floor-rated porcelain clears it easily. It is a useful check and a poor obsession — it says nothing about the substrate, the membrane, or the mortar coverage, which are what actually decide how a floor ages.
Is wood-look tile porcelain or ceramic?
Essentially always porcelain. The long plank format needs porcelain’s strength to survive manufacturing, shipping and floors, so that is how the industry makes it. The same goes for today’s large-format slabs. If your heart is set on either look, the porcelain-vs-ceramic debate is already settled — what remains is finding an installer who can flatten a floor to what those formats demand.
Which is better for a kitchen backsplash?
Ceramic, in most kitchens. A backsplash carries no foot traffic, no impacts, and no standing water, so porcelain’s advantages sit unused while its harder cutting slows the fussiest, most cut-dense part of the job. Ceramic costs less, cuts cleaner around outlets and windows, and includes some of the best-looking handmade and glazed options on the market.
Can porcelain tile go outside in Western Washington?
Yes — and it is the only tile that should. Our winters deliver enough freeze-thaw cycles that absorbent ceramic will eventually take on water, freeze, and split or spall. Porcelain’s sub-0.5% absorption makes it frost-proof in practice, which is why porch steps, patios, and outdoor kitchens around here are porcelain by rule. Pair it with an exterior-rated setting system, and slope every surface to drain.
