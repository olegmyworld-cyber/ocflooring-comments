Refinishing quotes get won and lost on one number — the per-square-foot price — so that’s the number some contractors polish until it shines. The rest of the job’s cost doesn’t disappear; it migrates. None of this is illegal. It’s just a pricing style — and once you know its five favorite hiding places, it stops working on you.

Why Quotes Hide Costs in the First Place

It’s a structural problem, not a moral one. Most homeowners collect three bids and compare one number, so the winning move — if winning means getting signed — is to make that number as small as possible and recover the margin in extras. The contractor who quotes honestly at $4 loses to the one who quotes $2.50 and bills the missing $1.50 in add-ons, unless the homeowner knows to compare totals instead of rates. That’s the entire game. The defense is equally simple: ask every bidder for the out-the-door price for your actual job — your square footage, your stairs, your appliances, your finish — in writing. A contractor with nothing hidden produces that number in minutes. A contractor who resists producing it has answered a different question for you.

The Big Five, and What Each One Costs

Furniture. Somebody has to empty the rooms, and “not included” only surfaces on day one. We move normal furniture as part of the job; pianos, safes, and packed china cabinets are a conversation up front — our guide to furniture during a refinish covers who does what. Appliances. Disconnecting a gas range or a plumbed fridge is real work with real liability; our numbers are $160 for a washer/dryer pair and $80 per other appliance, printed on the estimate. Stairs. Treads are hand work and are priced per step — $55–$75 each with us. A whole-house quote that never mentions the staircase has left a four-figure item dangling. Dust containment. Ours is a flat +$250 for full containment; quotes that skip the topic usually mean bare-minimum plastic or none, and our dust-free refinishing post explains what proper containment involves. Finish upgrades. The quiet one: a low quote is frequently priced on a builder-grade single-component finish, and the commercial two-component product you assumed you were getting — Bona Traffic HD, in our case — appears later as a $1-plus-per-foot upgrade. Ask which exact product is in the base price.

Mid-Job Repairs: The One Honest Surprise — and How It Should Be Priced

We’ll defend contractors on one point: some surprises are real. Nobody can see what’s under carpet until it’s up — pet-stained boards, water damage, a previous owner’s creative patching — and no honest bid can price damage that’s still hidden. The difference between an honest surprise and a hidden cost is entirely in the handling. The honest version: work stops, you see the problem with your own eyes, and you get a written change order with a price before anyone proceeds. The other version: a verbal “we found some issues, it’ll be a bit more” delivered mid-sand, when half your floor is raw wood and your leverage is zero. Ask how discovered repairs are priced before you sign — it’s the question that best predicts how the whole job will go. If the answer involves a per-board rate and a change-order process, you’re in good hands, whoever’s hands they are.

The Lynnwood Version of This Problem

Lynnwood’s housing stock — heavy on 1960s–80s split-levels and ramblers — produces two hidden-cost scenarios more often than anywhere else we work. First: hardwood under carpet. A huge share of those homes have original oak that’s been carpeted over for decades, and quotes to refinish it are quotes on partially hidden wood, which is exactly where the mid-job-repair handling above earns its keep — carpet tack strips, pad staples, and the occasional pet legacy all live under there. Second: split-level staircases. The half-flights connecting a split-level’s levels are short, but there are usually two or three of them, and at $55–$75 per tread across a dozen-plus treads, an “oh, stairs are extra” quote grows by a thousand dollars-plus after signing. Signs of an honest quote

  • Every add-on has a printed number — or a printed $0
  • The exact finish product and coat count are named
  • Stairs, appliances, and containment addressed unprompted
  • Discovered repairs go through written change orders
  • The out-the-door total appears without a fight

Red flags worth heeding

  • A per-foot rate far below everyone else’s, with a vague scope
  • “Polyurethane finish” with no brand, grade, or coat count
  • “We’ll work out the details after we start”
  • Verbal pricing for anything discovered mid-job
  • Reluctance to put the total for your actual job in writing

Hidden Refinishing Costs: What Lynnwood Homeowners Ask Us

What's the most common hidden cost in a refinishing quote?
Stairs, in our experience. Treads are slow hand work, they're priced per step, and a surprising number of whole-house quotes simply omit the staircase. At $55–$75 per tread, a typical flight adds $700–$1,000 — real money to discover after you've signed. Always ask for the per-tread price and the tread count in writing.
Is a low per-square-foot price always a bait?
Not always — a legitimate $1.99/sq ft screen and recoat exists, and it's a genuinely cheaper service than a full refinish. The bait version is a full-refinish quote priced far below market with the difference recovered in add-ons. The test is the out-the-door total for your actual job: honest cheap survives that question, bait doesn't.
Should dust containment really cost extra?
It's honest either way, as long as it's priced up front. Containment takes real time and materials — plastic barriers, sealed doorways, HVAC protection — so some contractors build it into the rate and some list it separately, as we do at a flat $250. The problem isn't the line item; it's the quote that never mentions dust at all.
What should happen when the contractor finds damage mid-job?
Work should pause, you should see the problem yourself, and the price to fix it should arrive as a written change order before anyone continues. Ask about this process before signing — it's the best single predictor of how a contractor handles everything. Verbal mid-job pricing, when your floor is half-sanded and your leverage is gone, is the red flag.
How do I compare quotes that are structured differently?
Reduce them all to one number: the total, out the door, for your job — your square footage, your stairs, your appliances, the named finish product, containment included. Send each bidder the same list and ask for the total in writing. Structure differences vanish, and the quotes become directly comparable in a way per-foot rates never are.
Are appliance moving fees legitimate?
Yes — disconnecting and resetting a gas range or a plumbed refrigerator is skilled work with real liability if it's botched, which is why we price it explicitly at $160 for a washer/dryer pair and $80 per other appliance. The legitimate version is printed on the estimate. The illegitimate version is discovered when the crew arrives.
What does a 500 sq ft minimum mean for a small job?
It means a job under 500 sq ft is billed as if it were 500 — because setup, machinery, containment, and finish cure time cost nearly the same for one room as for three. Most contractors have some version of this; the honest ones state it before measuring. If you have a small area, ask whether combining rooms gets you better value from the same visit.
Which finish should the base price include?
You want a named, commercial-grade product and a coat count — not the word “polyurethane.” We quote Bona and Pallmann waterborne systems, two to three coats, in the base price. A quote built on an unnamed builder-grade finish isn't necessarily dishonest, but you should know that's what you're comparing against before the upgrade conversation arrives later.
