Why Living Through a Refinish Became Reasonable

Three things had to change, and all three did. Finish chemistry first: the commercial waterbornes we use cure fast and low-odor — recoat in 2–3 hours, sock traffic the same evening, and VOCs in the tens of grams per liter rather than the hundreds, which is the difference between “the house smells faintly of nothing much” and “book a hotel.” Our guide to low-VOC refinishing with kids and pets covers the health side in depth. Second, dust: modern vacuum-assisted sanding captures the overwhelming share at the machine, and for occupied homes we offer full dust containment — plastic barriers, sealed doorways, protected HVAC returns — for a flat +$250. Third, timelines: a waterborne system goes down in days, not the week-plus of one-coat-per-day oil work, so each phase of a phased job is mercifully short.

What hasn’t changed: sanding is loud, the crew is in your house, and a floor with wet finish on it is a room you don’t own for the evening. Phasing manages that reality; nothing eliminates it.

How Room-by-Room Phasing Actually Works

The house splits into zones along natural doorway lines — most commonly bedrooms-plus-hallway as zone one and kitchen-living-dining as zone two. Everything from zone one moves into zone two (or the garage), and we refinish zone one: sand, TRIO flattening pass, and 2–3 coats of waterborne, typically 2–4 days depending on footage. You live in zone two the whole time — kitchen working, sofa available, one bathroom always reachable, because we sequence around that on day one.

Then the swap. Zone one takes sock traffic the evening of its final coat and furniture at 48–72 hours — so beds go back, the family moves in, and zone two empties for its own 2–4 days. Rugs stay off everything for 7 days per zone while the finish cures, and the walk-on timeline governs each zone independently. Total calendar: usually 7–10 days for what would be a 3–5 day empty-house job. That’s the price of sleeping in your own bed, and most families consider it a bargain.

The Honest Trade-Offs

The seam. Where two phases meet — usually a doorway — we stop one phase’s sanding and finishing at the threshold and pick up there in the next. Done carefully, at a natural break, the transition is invisible; done mid-room it wouldn’t be, which is why zones follow doorways and we won’t split an open-concept great room into two phases. That single fact decides more phasing plans than any other: a Redmond home with a fully open main level phases the upstairs fine, but the main level itself is all-or-nothing.

The calendar. Two mobilizations take longer than one, and the crew’s schedule has to line up twice. Pricing stays $3.99/sq ft for a natural refinish or $1.99 for a buff and coat either way — with the note that each phase needs enough footage to make sense, since our 500 sq ft job minimum applies to the project and tiny phases serve nobody.

The furniture. Phasing means moving everything twice — into the living zone, then back. We move furniture as part of the job (appliances are the line item: $80 each, $160 for a washer/dryer pair), but a phased house lives crowded for a week; our furniture storage guide has the survival plan. Households that can’t tolerate a crowded week — or that have a piano — sometimes conclude a short move-out for a single-phase job is simpler, and they’re not wrong.

When We’ll Recommend Against Phasing

Some jobs shouldn’t phase, and we’d rather lose the booking than pretend. Open floor plans with no doorway to break at, as above. Whole-house stain jobs — color consistency across phases is achievable but unforgiving, and if you’re staining dark we’d push hard for a single-phase job with the house empty; stain color choices are stressful enough without a seam risk. Homes where the only bathroom sits inside the work zone. And any job where the client wants oil-modified poly — the fumes make occupied phasing a bad idea at any schedule, which is one more reason the finish chemistry choice comes first.

The alternative isn’t always a hotel. Some families phase by floor (live downstairs while upstairs cures), some borrow a week at grandma’s for a single-phase blitz, and empty-nesters with flexible timing sometimes just do one room a year on a maintenance cadence. The right plan is the one that matches your floor plan and your tolerance — that’s a conversation, not a formula.

Living Through a Refinish: What Redmond Homeowners Ask

Can we really stay in the house the whole time?
Most of our occupied-home clients do. With low-VOC waterborne finishes, sleeping in the house the night a coat goes down is routine — the phased schedule exists precisely so there's always a furnished, finished zone to live in. The genuinely rough patches are the sanding days in each phase, which are loud during work hours.
Does refinishing room by room cost more?
The per-square-foot rates are the same — $3.99 for a full natural refinish, $1.99 for a buff and coat. What phasing costs is time: a 3–5 day empty-house job becomes 7–10 days in two phases. Our 500 sq ft project minimum applies as usual, and very small phases aren't practical to schedule.
Will we see a line where one phase ended and the next began?
Not if the seam falls at a doorway or natural threshold, which is how we design the zones. Sanding and finishing stop and restart at that break, and on a natural-finish floor the transition disappears. Mid-room seams are a different story — which is why open-concept spaces get refinished as a single phase.
How long is each room out of service?
Count 2–4 working days per zone, then sock traffic the same evening as the final coat, shoes and furniture at 48–72 hours, and rugs after 7 days. A bedroom is typically back in full service — bed, dresser, everything but the rug — about three days after its final coat.
What about the kitchen? We have to eat somewhere.
The kitchen anchors the phasing plan. We schedule it in whichever phase leaves it available longest, and when its turn comes, the 48–72 hour furniture window is when you lean on takeout or the grill. Fridges and ranges sitting on hardwood get moved and reset by us — $80 per appliance.
Can the kids and the dog stay during the work?
Generally yes, with zone discipline: closed doors or gates between the household and the day's work area, and everyone including the dog off new finish until it's ready for traffic. The low-VOC finishes we use are the reason this is reasonable; with oil-based products we'd tell you honestly to relocate the household instead.
How much dust should we actually expect?
Modern vacuum-assisted sanding captures the large majority at the source, so the days of dust on every horizontal surface are gone. For occupied homes we recommend full dust containment — sealed doorways and protected HVAC returns — at a flat $250, which keeps the living zone genuinely livable rather than technically livable.
Is it better to just move out for a few days?
Sometimes, honestly, yes. A single-phase job in an empty house is faster (3–5 days total), slightly cheaper in disruption if not in dollars, and mandatory-adjacent for whole-house dark stain work or fully open floor plans. If you have somewhere easy to stay, we'll tell you when that's the smarter play at the estimate.
