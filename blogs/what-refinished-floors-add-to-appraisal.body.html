Quick answer

Refinished floors almost never show up as their own line item on a Sammamish appraisal — appraisers rate a home’s overall condition and compare it to recent sales, and your floors are one input into that rating rather than a separate credit. What refinishing reliably does is keep the house in the same condition bracket as its comps instead of a lower one, and take a visible objection off the table before buyers and inspectors start pricing it themselves. On a typical 1,200 sq ft main floor, a full sand and natural finish runs about $4,800 at $3.99/sq ft, and a screen & recoat about $2,400 at $1.99. The concession a buyer asks for over gray, worn floors is usually a bigger number than either.

How Floors Actually Enter an Appraisal

An appraiser is answering one question: what have comparable homes nearby recently sold for, and how does this one differ? The standard residential form rates a property’s overall condition on a six-point scale — new construction at the top, significant deferred maintenance at the bottom — and rates quality of construction separately. Your floors feed the condition rating along with the kitchen, the baths, the roof, the paint, and the windows. They don’t get their own dollar line, and there is no lookup table anywhere that says “refinished hardwood: +$6,000.”

What that means practically: refinishing rarely lifts a house above its comps. What it does is stop the house from dropping below them. A Sammamish plateau house with gray, worn traffic lanes and a finish scuffed through to bare wood in the kitchen doorway is not a C3 house with nice floors — it reads as a house with deferred maintenance, and everything else in the report gets read through that lens. That’s where the money actually moves, and it moves in the direction nobody wants.

The Bigger Effect Isn’t the Appraisal — It’s the Buyers

Appraisals mostly confirm a price that buyers already agreed to. So the more important question is what worn floors do to the offer, and there the effect is direct and expensive. Buyers who walk onto a floor with visible wear don’t mentally price a $4,800 refinish; they price “redo the floors,” and the number in their head is usually a replacement number — often two to three times what the work would actually cost. Then their agent writes that into the negotiation, or their inspector puts flooring on the summary page and it becomes a repair-request line.

Our post on what home inspectors flag on hardwood floors covers the specific things that end up in writing, and the cheapest floor upgrade that still passes a buyer inspection is the companion piece for sellers who want the minimum defensible spend. The pattern we see repeatedly: a $2,400 recoat prevents a $10,000 conversation. That is a better return than any appraisal adjustment would have been.

There’s a photography argument too, and it isn’t frivolous. On the Eastside, most buyers decide whether to visit from the listing photos, and refinished floors photograph as light, clean lines while worn ones photograph as gray smudges through the middle of every room. More showings is more competition is a higher price — a chain that never appears anywhere on the appraisal form.

When Refinishing Before Listing Pays, and When It Doesn’t

The honest version has a “don’t” side, and we say it at estimates even though it costs us work.

It pays when the floors are the first thing you notice walking in, when the comps in your Sammamish neighborhood have all been updated, when there’s a gray traffic lane down the hallway or bare wood at a doorway, and when there’s enough time to do it properly before photos. Those are the jobs where we’ve watched sellers get their money back and then some — not through the appraisal, but through the offer.

It doesn’t pay when the floors are simply a little dull and a $1.99/sq ft recoat would solve it — spending $3.99 there is buying sanding life the floor didn’t need to spend, and we’d rather sell you the cheaper job. It doesn’t pay when you’re already priced at the ceiling for your street, since no amount of finish moves a comp range. And it doesn’t pay when the buyer pool for the house is clearly going to gut it anyway. Our full argument on the timing question is in should you refinish floors before listing, and if you’re trying to decide between refinishing and replacing, we ran the numbers on that in refinish vs replace on 800 sq ft of oak.

One more decision that comes up constantly: color. If you’re refinishing to sell, natural wins on breadth of appeal almost every time — that’s the whole finding in do buyers pay more for natural vs stained floors. Stain is a personal choice, and personal choices narrow the buyer pool at the exact moment you want it wide.

Refinish before listing when…

  • There are gray traffic lanes or bare spots — wear that reached the wood
  • Your comps have all been updated and yours would read as the tired one
  • Pets, sun fade, or a failed previous refinish left visible damage
  • You have 2+ weeks before photos and can clear furniture
  • The floors are the first thing you see from the entry

Just recoat, or do nothing, when…

  • The finish is dull but unbroken — a $1.99/sq ft recoat handles it
  • You’re already at the top of the price range for the street
  • The likely buyer is going to remodel the whole main floor anyway
  • You’re under contract with days, not weeks, before closing
  • The wear layer is thin and the floor can’t take another sand

Refinished Floors and Appraisals: What Sammamish Sellers Ask

Will refinishing my floors raise the appraised value?
Not as a line item. Appraisers rate the home's overall condition and compare it to recent nearby sales; floors are one input into that rating, not a separate credit. What refinishing does is keep the house from being rated below its comps for deferred maintenance, which is where the real dollars are.
How much does refinishing cost for a typical Sammamish main floor?
About $4,788 for 1,200 square feet at our $3.99 per square foot natural refinish rate, or about $2,388 for the same area screened and recoated at $1.99. Stairs are priced separately at $55 to $75 per tread, and there's a 500 square foot minimum on any job.
Do buyers really notice worn floors?
Immediately, and they price them higher than the work costs. Buyers don't estimate a refinish, they estimate replacement, which is often two to three times the number. That estimate turns into a negotiating position or an inspection repair request, which is why a smaller spend up front usually prevents a larger concession later.
Should I stain the floors a trendy color before selling?
We'd advise against it. Natural, unstained floors have the broadest appeal and cost $3.99 per square foot instead of $6.50, so you spend less and narrow the buyer pool less. A stain color is a personal decision, and selling is the one moment when personal decisions work against you.
Is a screen and recoat enough before listing?
Often, yes. If the finish is worn but unbroken and there are no gray or bare patches, a one-day recoat restores the sheen and photographs like a new floor for half the cost. If wear has reached the wood, a recoat seals the discoloration in and the boundary between worn and unworn areas stays visible.
How long before listing should the work happen?
Leave at least two weeks. The work itself is 3 to 5 days for a full refinish, furniture goes back at 48 to 72 hours, and rugs wait about a week while the finish cures. Schedule so the floors are done before staging and photography, not after.
Does an appraiser want to see receipts for the work?
They won't price the invoice, but documentation helps anywhere the condition of the home is being discussed — the seller's improvement list, buyer questions, and the agent's marketing. Keep the estimate and invoice with the product name and date on them.
What if my floors can't be sanded again?
Then a screen and recoat is the answer, since it adds finish without removing wood. If the boards are past their sanding life or badly damaged, the conversation becomes refinish versus replace, and replacement runs $3 to $4.25 per square foot in labor plus $4 to $10 or more per square foot in materials.
