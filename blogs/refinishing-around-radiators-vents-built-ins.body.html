Every floor has a field and every floor has edges, and the edges are where refinishing jobs are won or lost. The big drum or belt machine that does the satisfying work — stripping a room to raw wood in long passes — physically cannot get within several inches of anything vertical. Around a cast-iron radiator, under its belly, against the face frame of a 1930s built-in china cabinet, at the rim of a heat register: all of that is done with edgers, detail sanders, scrapers, and hands. In a modern open-plan house, edge work might be 10% of the job. In the pre-war homes of the Edmonds bowl, with radiators in every room and built-ins in half of them, it can be a third of the labor.

Radiators: The Cast-Iron Question

The rule we follow: radiators stay connected. Disconnecting a hot-water radiator means draining the system, breaking unions that may not have moved since the Roosevelt administration, and re-pressurizing afterward — plumber’s work, plumber’s risk, and almost never worth it for the strip of floor involved. Instead we work around them: the field machine runs up to a respectful distance, the edger takes over, and the last inches under the belly get low-profile detail sanders and hand scrapers. Cast iron sits high enough on its legs that we can usually get a decent finish surface under all but the lowest units; where a radiator sits too low for tools, that shadow strip gets scraped and coated by hand as far as reach allows — honestly, the same thing every good refinisher has done for a century.

Two caveats we give Edmonds owners of steam and hot-water systems. First, protect-then-sand: we wrap radiators before machines start, both for dust and because an edger kissing a painted radiator leaves a mark on both parties. Second, timing — if your radiators are due for repainting or your system is being serviced, do that before the floors, for the same reason painting happens before floors: drips and boot traffic belong on the old finish, not the new one.

Floor Vents and Registers: The Easy Ones, Done Right

Forced-air registers are the simplest obstacle on the list because the visible part is removable. Covers come off, openings get masked so the ducts don’t inhale sanding dust, and the floor sands clean to the rim of the opening. Two upgrades worth considering while everything is apart. If your registers are the standard stamped-metal kind sitting proud of the floor, this is the natural moment to switch to wood flush-mount vents that sand and finish with the floor itself — the detail that makes a refinished floor look custom, which we covered in our hardwood vent guide. And if dust in the duct system is a worry — it should be, in a house with forced air — pairing the refinish with our HVAC dust-control practices and the $250 containment option keeps the system out of the sanding conversation entirely.

Built-Ins: Treat Them Like Furniture That Can’t Leave

Window seats, china cabinets, bookcases, breakfast nooks, the occasional Murphy-bed cabinet — pre-war carpenters built them in, and they’re staying. Our approach mirrors kitchen cabinetry: sand to the toe kick or base trim, keep the finish line tucked into the shadow, and protect every vertical face with masking and board where machines operate nearby. The floor under a built-in doesn’t exist as far as the refinish is concerned, and that’s correct — it was never finished in the first place on most old homes. The judgment call is the base shoe: where quarter-round trim meets the floor, we either work to it or pull and reinstall it depending on its condition and your preference — the trade-offs are the same ones from our baseboard guide. What we don’t do is sand into the built-in’s base trim to chase old finish; that trades a shadow line nobody sees for gouged woodwork everybody sees.

Hearths, Thresholds, and the Rest of the Obstacle Course

Rounding out the list: fireplace hearths get the same to-the-edge treatment as built-ins, with stone and tile masked heavily because sanding grit is harder than most hearth materials’ polish. Door thresholds and transition strips are usually removed and either reinstalled or replaced — refinishing is the cheapest moment you’ll ever have to swap a beat-up threshold. Stair nosings where a floor meets a staircase get hand-finished to match. Closets are their own small confession: the machines fit in most, and we sand them, but a very small closet is nearly all edge work, which is why it takes longer than its square footage suggests. None of this is exotic — it’s the ordinary anatomy of a real house, and it’s exactly what the flat-rate price is for. What you’re actually vetting when you hire someone isn’t whether they can sand a field — anyone can rent that ability — it’s whether their edge work blends into the field invisibly. Ask to see photos of finished floors at the radiator, not in the middle of the room.

Radiators, Vents & Built-Ins: What Homeowners Ask Us

Do radiators have to be removed to refinish the floor?
No — and we recommend against it. Disconnecting hot-water or steam radiators means draining and re-pressurizing the system, which is plumbing work with real risk in old houses. We protect the radiators, sand under and around them with edgers and hand tools, and finish as far as reach allows. The strip in the deepest shadow is handled by hand, as it has been for a hundred years.
Will there be a visible line where the machine stopped and hand work began?
Not on a properly done job — blending the edger's work into the field is the core skill of refinishing. Where you should look for evidence is a contractor's photos: ask to see finished floors at radiators and cabinet edges. The middle of a room proves nothing; the edges prove everything.
What happens to the heat registers during sanding?
Covers come off, duct openings are masked so dust stays out of the system, and the floor is sanded clean to the opening. It's also the ideal moment to upgrade to wood flush-mount vents that finish with the floor — worth deciding early so they can be ordered in your species.
Can you sand inside closets and under window seats?
Closets, yes — full sand and finish, though they're disproportionately slow because they're nearly all edge work. Under built-in window seats and cabinets, no — like kitchen cabinets, the floor there was usually never finished, nothing wears it, and reaching it would mean dismantling original carpentry. We finish to the toe kick.
Does a house full of obstacles cost more to refinish?
The per-square-foot price is the same — $3.99 natural, $6.50 stained. Obstacle-heavy homes shift more of the labor to hand work within that price. Where cost can appear is in options: dust containment (+$250) makes extra sense around built-ins, and flush-mount vent upgrades are priced per vent.
Should radiators be painted before or after floor refinishing?
Before, always. Radiator painting drips and requires working boots around the base — all of which belongs on the old floor. Same logic as wall painting: messy overhead trades first, floors last.
How do you keep sanding dust out of built-in shelving and radiators?
Masking and containment. Verticals get wrapped before machines start, our sanders run with vacuum collection, and the +$250 full containment option adds sealed barriers that matter most in homes where dust has a thousand shelf-feet to settle on. Emptying the built-ins beforehand is the one part we need from you.
Do old fir floors around radiators need special care?
Yes — fir is much softer than oak, so aggressive edger work dishes it visibly, especially in the tight zones around obstacles. It rewards patient hand work and punishes speed. If your Edmonds home has original fir, make sure whoever quotes it has actually refinished fir before.
Can you replace a rusted or damaged floor vent while you're at it?
Yes — thresholds, registers, and small trim pieces are easiest to swap during a refinish, while everything is apart and the finish will tie the new piece in. Flag anything you want replaced at the estimate and we'll include it in writing.
