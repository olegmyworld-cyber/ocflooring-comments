The Room-by-Room Verdict

Most carpet-versus-hardwood articles are written by someone selling one of them. We sell both, which is clarifying: there is no answer to defend, just a decision per room. The variables that actually decide it are always the same four — what touches the floor (bare feet, boots, paws, chair casters), what is under the floor (bedroom over a garage is a different question than bedroom over a slab), how much noise the room makes or receives, and what the room costs to do in each material. Run every room through those four and the house sorts itself out quickly.

Bedrooms: Carpet, and It Is Not Close

The case for hardwood in a bedroom is that it looks wonderful and that a future buyer might prefer it. The case for carpet is everything else the room actually does: bare feet at six in the morning in February, a floor a toddler can fall on, a room that absorbs sound instead of bouncing it, and the lowest installed cost of any flooring we offer. Bedrooms are also carpet's easiest maintenance case — no shoes, no spills to speak of, traffic in one predictable lane. A mid-grade carpet over a good pad in a bedroom routinely outlives the same carpet in a family room by years. If the budget forces a choice, put hardwood money into the rooms guests see and carpet the rooms you sleep in; we make that exact recommendation weekly.

Stairs: The Genuine Argument

Stairs are where the two materials fight on even terms. Hardwood stairs are the showpiece answer — they photograph beautifully and match a hardwood main floor. They are also hard, loud, and slippery in socks, which is why the households that choose them often add a runner within the year. Carpeted stairs give up the looks and win everything practical: traction going down, mercy when someone falls, and a staircase you cannot hear from the living room. In homes with small kids, older knees, or dogs that treat the staircase as a racetrack, we lean carpet and say so plainly — our guide to the best carpet for stairs covers fiber and construction, because stairs chew up soft carpet. If you have hardwood stairs already and love them, refinishing rather than replacing is usually the play; stair refinishing is priced per step and costs far less than people expect.

Family Rooms, Flow, and the Resale Nuance

The main floor is where resale thinking legitimately enters. Buyers in our market do pay for hardwood in the kitchen, dining and main living areas — it reads as quality, it flows in open plans, and it is what listing photos favor. But the version of that advice people repeat — hardwood everywhere, carpet kills value — is wrong on the second half. Buyers do not pay a premium for hardwood in bedrooms the way they do on the main floor, and fresh, well-chosen bedroom carpet reads as move-in ready, not as a compromise. What actually hurts a sale is old carpet: matted lanes and a decade of dinginess in the photos. New carpet in bedrooms plus refreshed hardwood in the public rooms is the highest-return combination we see, and if a listing is coming, refinishing before you list covers the hardwood half of that equation.

One more main-floor honesty: if your family room is where the actual living happens — kids on the floor, movie nights, a dog with opinions — carpet remains a completely defensible choice there, resale be damned. A big rug over hardwood splits the difference and is what most of our open-plan customers land on.

Basements: Carpet’s Quiet Comeback

Over a dry slab, carpet is the best thing that can happen to a basement — it turns the coldest, hardest floor in the house into the room the teenagers disappear into. Hardwood, meanwhile, mostly does not belong below grade at all; solid wood and concrete moisture are old enemies. The entire question is the word dry. If the slab has any history of dampness, seepage or that basement smell, honest answer: neither material — that is where a waterproof vinyl plank floor earns its keep, possibly with carpet in one dry corner. Carpet vs Hardwood: What Homeowners Ask Us

Is carpet or hardwood better?
Per room, not per house. Bedrooms favor carpet decisively — warmth, quiet and cost. Kitchens and open main floors favor hardwood. Stairs are a real argument that usually turns on who lives in the house. Basements depend on the slab. The best homes we work on mix the two deliberately instead of picking a side.
Is carpet cheaper than hardwood?
Substantially. Installed carpet starts at $1.49 per square foot with us — material, pad and labor. New hardwood costs $3 to $4.25 per square foot in labor before the wood itself is purchased. Carpet is the least expensive way to finish a room, which is exactly why pairing it with hardwood where hardwood matters is such an effective budget strategy.
Does carpet in bedrooms hurt resale value?
No — that is the most repeated piece of bad advice in this category. Buyers pay for hardwood on the main level; in bedrooms, fresh well-chosen carpet reads as move-in ready and nobody discounts an offer over it. What hurts a sale is old, matted carpet in the photos. New bedroom carpet plus refreshed main-floor hardwood is the highest-return combination we see before listings.
Which is better on stairs, carpet or hardwood?
Practically, carpet: traction going down, quiet, and mercy when someone slips. Aesthetically, hardwood — and it is genuinely beautiful — but it is hard, loud and slick in socks, which is why so many hardwood staircases grow a runner within a year. Households with young kids, older adults or big dogs should lean carpet and not feel one ounce of regret.
Can you put hardwood in a basement?
Solid hardwood, no — wood and below-grade concrete moisture are longstanding enemies. Carpet works well over a genuinely dry slab and is the warmest, quietest thing you can do to a basement. If the slab has any moisture history, the honest answer is a waterproof vinyl plank floor rather than forcing either traditional material to fight the concrete.
Is hardwood really worth the higher cost long-term?
On the main floor, usually yes — because hardwood is renewable rather than disposable. A tired hardwood floor gets screened and recoated at $1.99 per square foot or fully refinished at $3.99 and comes back to life; carpet at the end of its run is replaced. Over thirty years the hardwood room may cost less than the carpeted one. In a bedroom, where carpet lives its longest easiest life, the math rarely flips.
What if there is hardwood under my carpet?
Then stop shopping and look first — refinishing wood you already own is cheaper than buying either floor new. Pull a floor vent or check a closet corner: if you see wood strip flooring, refinishing at $3.99 per square foot for a natural finish is likely your best-value move, with carpet reserved for the rooms where you want carpet on purpose. We check at every estimate.

Yes and yes. We have installed carpet and wood floors across King and Snohomish County since 2013, so we have no side to argue. At the estimate we walk each room, tell you which material we would put in our own house, and leave a written price the same visit — including the rooms where the answer is "not the thing you called us about."
