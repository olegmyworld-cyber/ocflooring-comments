The Short Answer, and What It’s Based On

Paint before floors. Ceilings, walls, doors, casings, and the first coats on the baseboards all happen first. Then the floors get sanded and finished. Then, once the finish has hardened, the painter returns for a short visit to touch up the bottom inch of the baseboards and anything the floor work nicked.

That sequence minimizes the number of ways two crews can hurt each other. It costs one extra painter visit — usually a couple of hours — and it saves you from the two failure modes that actually ruin work: sanding dust settling into wet paint, and a painter’s ladder, tape, and drips landing on a floor finish that hasn’t cured. Both of those cost far more than a touch-up call.

Why Sanding Dust and Wet Paint Are a Bad Marriage

Even with modern dust containment — sealed rooms, negative air, vacuum-connected machines — floor sanding produces extremely fine airborne particulate. Our containment keeps it out of your HVAC and off your furniture, which is exactly what it’s for, and we’d rather run it than skip it. What it can’t promise is a surgically particle-free environment in the room being sanded, because that isn’t how sanding works.

Wet paint, meanwhile, is flypaper. Anything in the air lands in it and stays in it, and the fix isn’t a wipe — it’s sanding the wall and recoating. That asymmetry is the whole argument: dry paint shrugs off dust that a fresh coat would have captured permanently. If the paint is already cured, our containment and cleanup are the end of the story. We wrote up how the containment side actually works in HVAC and dust control during refinishing.

Why Painting After the Floors Is Worse Than It Sounds

Homeowners sometimes propose the reverse — floors first, so the painter can be careful — and it’s worth walking through what that actually involves. A painter working a room needs to put a ladder on the floor, spread drop cloths over the floor, set a tray on the floor, and cut a clean line at the baseboard, which usually means tape on the floor.

Every one of those is a problem on a floor whose finish is days old. Waterborne finish takes foot traffic the same evening, but it doesn’t reach full hardness for about a week, and that’s exactly the window a painter would be working in. Tape is the worst of it: adhesive on a not-fully-cured film can lift finish when it comes off, and the damage shows as a matte stripe along the baseboard that no amount of cleaner fixes. Add ladder feet, dropped brushes, and paint spatter that has to be scraped off rather than wiped, and you’re looking at a repair to a floor you just paid to have refinished. The cure-time details are in how long before you can walk on refinished floors.

To be fair to the other side: a careful painter can absolutely work over a cured floor without damaging it, and sometimes the schedule leaves no choice — a tenant turn, a closing date, a painter who can’t come sooner. When that’s the situation we say so and adjust: wait the full seven days, no tape on the finish, felt under the ladder, and accept that a small touch-up on the floor may be needed at the end.

The Baseboard Question, Which Is the Real Question

Here’s where the sequencing genuinely gets interesting. Our sanding equipment works right up to the baseboard, and the edger runs within a hair of it. On a normal job the baseboards stay in place, get protected, and come through fine — but “fine” in this trade means the bottom edge may pick up a scuff, and a hundred-year-old painted base is more fragile than a new one. That’s why the last coat on the baseboards should come after the floor, not before.

If the baseboards are being replaced, the order flips for that piece: old base comes off before we sand, new base goes on after the floor is finished, and it gets painted in place afterward. That sequence gives you the cleanest possible result — the new trim sits down onto a finished floor with no gap and no old paint line — and it’s the right call in a house where the trim was going to be updated anyway. If they’re staying, we work around them and you plan the touch-up. Either way, the full breakdown of what happens down there is in what happens to baseboards during a refinish.

Two related notes. Shoe molding and quarter round, if present, generally come off before sanding and go back after — that’s often where the touch-up burden actually lands. And if you’re changing baseboard height, do it in this same window; adding taller base after the floor is refinished is easy, while doing it before means painting around a floor you’re about to sand.

When the Rule Bends

Paint first — nearly always

  • Whole-house repaint before move-in or listing
  • Ceilings or walls changing color at all
  • Anything getting sprayed, which puts overspray everywhere
  • Baseboards staying in place and being repainted
  • You want one painter visit plus one short touch-up, not two full ones

Floors first — only when

  • New baseboards are going in and will be painted in place after
  • The painter genuinely cannot come before your closing date
  • Only a small accent wall is being painted, far from the floor work
  • Trim height is changing and the new base sits onto the finished floor
  • You accept the 7-day wait and a no-tape rule for the painter

Small Decisions That Save a Trip

A few things worth settling before either crew shows up. Decide the sheen and the wall color together, not separately — a matte floor under flat walls in a north-facing Redmond living room can read gloomier than either sample suggested, and it’s free to look at both at once. Have the painter leave you a labeled quart of the baseboard color; whoever does the touch-up will need it, and hunting for the formula six months later is its own small tragedy.

Get the closet interiors painted in the first pass, since we’ll be sanding closet floors too and nobody wants to be brushing inside a closet over a fresh finish. And be clear in both contracts about who protects what — our containment covers the floor work; the painter’s drop cloths cover theirs; the gap between the two is where damage arguments live. Neither trade minds being asked; both mind being surprised. If there’s a smell question hanging over the sequencing, waterborne finish at around 65 g/L is far milder than most people expect and clears fast, which we cover in how long the refinishing smell lasts.

Painting and Refinishing: Sequencing Questions

Should I paint before or after refinishing floors?
Before, in nearly every case. Paint the ceilings, walls, doors, and first coats on the baseboards, let it dry, then refinish. The only work that belongs after the floor is a short baseboard touch-up and any new trim being installed onto the finished floor.
Will sanding dust get on my freshly painted walls?
Some fine dust reaches every surface in a room being sanded, even with containment. On cured paint it wipes off. On wet or recently applied paint it embeds permanently and the wall has to be sanded and recoated. That difference is the entire reason paint goes first.
Can the painter work while you're refinishing?
No — the two trades can't share a space. Sanding dust ruins wet paint, and painters need to put ladders, tape, and drop cloths on floors that are curing. We sequence around each other rather than overlap, which usually costs a day or two total and saves far more.
How long after refinishing before a painter can come back?
Foot traffic in socks is fine the same evening, but for someone working in the room with ladders and equipment, wait at least 48 to 72 hours and preferably the full week to reach cure. And no painter's tape on the floor finish — adhesive on a young film can lift it.
What about the baseboards — do you paint them before or after?
First coats before, final touch-up after. Our edger works right up to the base and can scuff the bottom edge, so the last inch is best painted once the floor is done. If the baseboards are being replaced entirely, they come off before sanding and go on after.
Do you remove quarter round and shoe molding?
Usually yes, if it's present — it comes off before sanding and goes back after the finish. That produces a cleaner result at the wall line, and it also means the reinstalled molding often wants a touch-up, which is another reason to keep the painter's return visit in the plan.
We're moving in three weeks. Can both fit?
Usually. A whole-house repaint typically needs a week to ten days, a full refinish 3 to 5 days, then a couple of days before furniture. That's the standard sequence we run in Redmond between a closing and a move-in. Book the painter first — their schedule is the harder one to move.
Does a recoat change the sequencing?
It shortens it dramatically. A screen and recoat is one day at $1.99/sq ft with no bare wood exposed and far less dust than a full sand. It still belongs after painting, but it fits into a much smaller gap — sometimes a single weekday between other trades.
