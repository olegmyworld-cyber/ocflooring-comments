Why Restaurant Floors Wear Like Nothing Else

Understanding the wear pattern matters because it sets the repair menu. Hospitality wear is intense but shallow and predictable: chair-drag abrasion around every table, a matte river along the server lanes, a gray crescent at the service bar, wear-through at the front door where grit concentrates. It’s finish-level damage in ninety percent of the room — which means most of a restaurant floor’s life can be managed at the recoat level, where the job is measured in hours, not days. The floors that end up needing heroic multi-day work are almost always floors where recoat-stage wear was ignored until the traffic areas went gray and bare. Same triage we apply to home hallways, at commercial intensity: catch it while a topcoat still fixes it.

The species under Seattle’s bars helps too. A lot of the older rooms — Ballard Avenue, Pioneer Square, Capitol Hill’s converted storefronts — sit on maple or old-growth fir that has already survived a century of boots, and oak fills most of the rest. All of it sands and recoats well; the difference is only in how gently the sanding must treat it, which is our problem, not yours.

The Three Scheduling Models

Model one: the closed-day recoat. Most Seattle restaurants go dark Monday, many Sunday night through Tuesday. That window fits a full screen and recoat: furniture out after last service, abrade and vacuum in the morning, two coats stacked through the afternoon on the 2–3 hour recoat cycle, sock-walkable that night. The binding constraint is furniture — 48 to 72 hours before tables and chairs land safely — so the arithmetic that works is: coat Sunday night or Monday morning, reset the room Tuesday evening at the 48-hour mark with felt under every chair leg, serve Wednesday. One lost service day, maybe two, and the floor is renewed.

Model two: the phased full sand. When traffic areas have worn through, sanding is required, and sanding a room takes longer than a coat does. The phased answer splits the floor at natural boundaries — dining room first, bar second, private-dining third — with each section sanded, finished, and returned to service while the next goes offline. It keeps revenue flowing at reduced covers rather than zero, and it works best in rooms that already have architectural seams; we scope the phase lines at the walkthrough, because a phase boundary in the middle of a continuous field has the same blending problem a residential spot-repair does.

Model three: the dark-week reset. Sometimes the honest answer is the full 3–5 day job in one stretch — floors gone gray throughout, a stain change, or damage repair alongside. The move that makes it affordable is piggybacking: schedule it on the closure you were already taking — the January deep-clean week, a kitchen-equipment install, the remodel. We do a steady trade in January and February for exactly this reason, and Seattle’s hospitality calendar makes those the sanest weeks to spend dark anyway.

The Maintenance Math Every Owner Should Run

Here’s the spreadsheet version. A busy room that recoats every 12–24 months at $1.99/sq ft loses one service day per cycle and essentially never sees bare wood again — the finish wears, the finish gets replaced, the wood underneath stays untouched for decades. A room that skips maintenance rides the finish down to gray traffic paths, then needs the $3.99/sq ft full sand plus multiple dark days, and spends one of the floor’s finite sandings. Run over ten years, the recoat cadence is both cheaper and dramatically kinder to revenue — the same recoat-vs-refinish logic we preach to homeowners, with a P&L attached. The tell-tales that say “book the recoat now”: server lanes going matte, gray starting at the front door or service stations, and water no longer beading where the chairs drag. The tell-tale that says you waited too long: the floor is the color of the sidewalk.

Between cycles, three cheap habits stretch every coat: felt or teflon glides on every chair that moves (the single highest-leverage dollar in this article), walk-off matting at the entry that actually gets vacuumed, and neutral-pH cleaning — nightly mopping with harsh degreasers dulls and softens finish faster than the customers do. Rubber bar mats and runners stay off new finish for its first week while the coat cures through.

Working in Seattle Rooms, Specifically

Seattle adds two local wrinkles. First, the building stock: a large share of the city’s best bar floors sit in century-old brick buildings — Pioneer Square, Ballard Avenue, First Hill — with maple or fir that’s been sanded before, sometimes many times. We check remaining wood thickness before quoting any full sand, and in thin-floor rooms we bias hard toward the recoat cadence, because those boards need to last another century of shuffleboard. Old commercial floors also carry patches, borders, and repairs from past lives; most owners rightly want that character kept, which changes the sanding brief from “make it perfect” to “make it healthy” — flatten, seal, protect, and let the history show. Second, the neighbors: much of Seattle’s dining stock sits under apartments, and big sanding machines are not a 2 a.m. activity. The rhythm that works in mixed-use buildings is loud work (sanding) during civilized daytime hours on closed days, quiet work (coating) after close — a constraint we fold into the phase plan up front rather than discovering mid-job.

Restaurant and Bar Floors: What Seattle Operators Ask Us

Can you refinish our floor without us closing at all?
For a recoat, close to it — one service day covers the work, and clever calendars (coat Sunday night, reopen Tuesday dinner) can shave that. For a full sand of a single connected room, no: the room has to be empty of furniture and people while machines run and coats stack. Phasing gets you continuous partial operation, which for most rooms is the practical version of staying open.
How soon can tables and chairs go back on the new finish?
48 to 72 hours after the final coat — that’s the real scheduling constraint, not foot traffic, which the finish takes the same evening in socks and the next day in shoes. We plan backwards from your next full service: final coat goes down early enough that the 48-hour mark lands before reset. Felt glides under every chair leg on day one, please — they’re also the cheapest finish-life extender that exists.
When can the bar mats and rubber runners come back?
Seven days. Waterborne finish takes traffic quickly but cures fully over about a week, and rubber-backed mats laid on uncured finish can trap moisture and imprint. Same rule as rugs in a house: people in days, rubber in a week.
Will the dining room smell like finish during service?
Not meaningfully. The waterborne systems we use run around 65 g/L VOC — Traffic HD carries GREENGUARD Gold certification — and the mild odor dissipates within hours of application. Coating happens after close precisely so the smell’s short life overlaps with the room’s empty hours. Solvent-based finishes are a different story, which is one of several reasons we don’t bring them into food businesses.
How often should a busy room plan to recoat?
Every 12 to 24 months for high-volume rooms, stretching longer for quieter ones — the floor tells you, via matte server lanes and dulling chair zones. Owners who calendar it (many pick the same slow Monday each January) essentially never face a full-sand closure again; the wood stays sealed under a perpetually renewed topcoat.
Our floor is old and full of character — patches, old borders, hundred-year wear. Will sanding erase that?
Only if you ask it to. Sanding depth is a choice: we can chase every mark to bare uniform wood, or flatten lightly, keep the patina and patches, and seal the history under fresh commercial finish. Most owners of century rooms choose the second, and we agree with them. What we won’t do is over-sand a thin antique floor — we measure remaining wood first and say so if a floor needs gentleness.
Can you work overnight?
Coating, yes — it’s quiet, and finishing after close is standard practice. Sanding is loud enough that in Seattle’s mixed-use buildings with apartments overhead it belongs in daytime hours on closed days, and we schedule accordingly. Stand-alone buildings have more flexibility; the walkthrough settles what your building allows.
