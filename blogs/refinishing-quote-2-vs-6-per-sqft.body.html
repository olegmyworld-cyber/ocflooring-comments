Three Different Jobs Wear the Same Name

“Refinishing” gets used for three services that share a truck and nothing else. A screen and recoat abrades the top of your existing finish and lays a fresh coat over it — no bare wood, no color change, scratches in the wood stay. It’s a one-day maintenance job, and it’s brilliant when the floor qualifies; our full comparison covers who qualifies. A full sand with natural finish takes the floor to bare wood and rebuilds the finish system from zero. A full sand with stain adds a day of hand-applied color between those steps. Three jobs, three honest prices: $1.99, $3.99, $6.50.

So before comparing any two quotes, answer one question: is each contractor sanding to bare wood, or not? If one is recoating and the other is sanding, you’re not comparing prices — you’re comparing services. (And be careful with a fourth service that advertises hard on low prices: so-called sandless refinishing, which we took apart in our Renton reality check. It’s a chemical-etch recoat with better marketing.)

What the Low Quote Often Leaves Off the Page

Assume both contractors really are quoting a full sand. The rate on the flyer is the beginning of the price, not the end of it, and the cheap quote gets cheap by moving costs off the page. The big one is the finish itself: “three coats of polyurethane” can mean a commercial two-component waterborne at well over $100 a gallon or a builder-grade product at a third of that, and the floor can’t tell you which it got until year three, when one of them is wearing through. Coat count is next — two coats quoted as if they were three. Then the orbit of extras that show up as day-of add-ons: moving appliances, dust containment, stair treads, repairs, even furniture handling.

Our approach is to put every one of those numbers in writing up front — $250 for full dust containment, $160 for a washer/dryer pair, $80 for other appliances, $55–$75 per stair tread — precisely so the quote you accept is the amount you pay. A contractor who won’t itemize isn’t necessarily hiding something. But the ones who are hiding something never itemize.

What the High Quote Might Legitimately Be Buying

Fairness cuts both ways: a $6 full-sand quote isn’t automatically padded. Real costs sit behind higher numbers — a two-component commercial finish instead of a bargain one, three coats instead of two, machines like the Lägler TRIO that flatten a floor instead of just scratching it smooth, a crew that’s licensed, insured, and still answering the phone when a board pops next spring. A written workmanship warranty costs the contractor real money to stand behind; ours is a year, and we’ve eaten repairs under it. That’s all in the price somewhere.

And sometimes, yes, the high number is just a high number — a busy calendar, a big office, or a contractor who quotes high hoping you don’t shop. That’s exactly why the line items matter more than the total: the padded quote and the premium quote look identical until you make them both show their work. The Lynnwood Angle: Split-Levels and Carpet Surprises

Lynnwood’s housing stock — heavy on 1960s–1980s split-levels and ramblers — makes quote comparison harder in a specific way: a lot of these homes still have original oak strip flooring hiding under wall-to-wall carpet, and nobody knows its condition until the carpet comes up. That unknown is where vague quotes go to grow. One contractor bids the floor sight-unseen at a too-good rate and reprices it mid-job (“more repairs than we thought”); another builds a cushion into a high rate and keeps the difference if the floor turns out clean.

The fix is the same in either direction: get the inspection before the number, and get the repair pricing in writing as its own line. We pull carpet corners and check registers at Lynnwood refinishing estimates for exactly this reason — with a 500 sq ft minimum and published rates, the only honest way we can quote is to actually look. Reading a Quote in 60 Seconds

A quote you can trust shows…

  • Which service it is: recoat, full sand, or sand + stain
  • The finish brand, line, and number of coats — in writing
  • Itemized extras: dust containment, appliances, stairs, repairs
  • License, insurance, and a written workmanship warranty
  • A price that survives from signature to final invoice

Red flags worth walking away from…

  • A per-foot rate with no named service behind it
  • “Polyurethane” with no brand or grade specified
  • Repairs and moving costs “handled when we get there”
  • Pressure to sign before an in-home inspection
  • A total that undercuts everyone by 40% with no explanation

Refinishing Quotes: What Lynnwood Homeowners Ask

Why is one refinishing quote $2/sq ft and another $6?
Usually because they're different services. Around $2 is screen-and-recoat territory — a fresh coat over existing finish, like our $1.99 rate. Around $6 is a full sand with stain, like our $6.50. Ask each contractor one question: are you taking the floor to bare wood? That sorts most quote gaps instantly.
Is a $2/sq ft full refinish possible?
Not credibly, in this market. Sanding to bare wood takes days of labor, machine wear, abrasives, and gallons of finish — a legitimate full sand with a quality finish system costs more to deliver than $2 a foot collects. A full-sand quote at that price is either a recoat in disguise or a job that will be repriced midway.
What should a complete refinishing quote include?
The service named (recoat, full sand, sand plus stain), the finish brand and number of coats, itemized extras — dust containment, appliance moves, stair treads, repairs — the timeline, and the warranty terms. Our quotes list each of those as its own line so the signed number is the invoiced number.
Does the finish brand really matter that much?
It's one of the biggest hidden differences between quotes. Commercial two-component waterbornes cost several times what builder-grade finish costs per gallon, and the difference shows up in years of wear, not on day one. A quote that says just "polyurethane" is leaving its biggest variable unnamed.
What do the common add-ons actually cost?
With us, in writing: full dust containment adds $250, moving a washer and dryer pair is $160, other appliances are $80 each, and stairs run $55 to $75 per tread. Board repairs are itemized after we inspect. If a quote doesn't mention these, they usually arrive later as day-of surprises.
Why do you have a 500 sq ft minimum?
Because mobilization is real: machines, crew, setup, and finish products cost nearly the same to bring to a 300 sq ft job as an 800 sq ft one. The minimum keeps our per-foot prices honest instead of padding small jobs quietly. Most Lynnwood main floors clear it comfortably.
Should I just take the middle quote?
No — take the most transparent one. The middle number can be the vaguest of the three. Make every bidder name the service, the finish, the coat count, and the extras, then compare totals for the same scope. When quotes describe identical jobs, the honest ones land surprisingly close together.
How do I know if my floor even needs the expensive version?
Have someone look at it honestly. If the finish is dull but intact — water still beads on it — a $1.99 recoat may buy you years, and we'll say so at the estimate, because it's the right call even though it's our cheapest service. Gray boards, bare traffic paths, or a color change put you in full-sand territory.
