The Refinish Column: What $3,192 Buys

At $3.99/sq ft, the 800 sq ft refinish comes to $3,192 and includes the whole system: sanding to bare wood with vacuum-assisted machines, edge work, our TRIO flattening pass, and two to three coats of commercial waterborne finish. Want color instead of natural? The stain price is $6.50/sq ft — $5,200 for this floor. The add-ons that actually come up: full plastic-wall dust containment if you want it, +$250; moving a washer and dryer, +$160 the pair; other appliances +$80 each. So a realistic worst-case refinish of this floor — stained, contained, fridge and stove moved — is about $5,800. The Replace Column: What $5,600–$11,400+ Doesn’t Include

New hardwood is priced in two stacked layers. Install labor runs $3–$4.25/sq ft — $2,400 to $3,400 for this floor. Materials run $4 to $10-plus per square foot depending on species, width, and grade: builder-grade red oak strip sits near the bottom; the wide-plank white oak everyone screenshots from listing photos sits at the top, and can blow past it. That’s $3,200 to $8,000+ in wood. Total: $5,600 to $11,400 and up — and note what’s still missing. Tearing out and hauling away 800 sq ft of nailed-down oak is real labor with real disposal fees. Baseboards or shoe molding usually come off and rarely go back perfectly. Transitions to tile and carpet get rebuilt. Furniture is displaced longer, because demolition plus installation takes more days than sanding does. None of those line items exist in the refinish column; all of them are itemized (or quietly missing) in replacement bids — the full anatomy is in our installation cost guide.

What the Numbers Don’t Capture (Both Directions)

Two things favor refinishing beyond price. First, the wood itself: 1980s–90s oak strip was commonly milled from slower-grown stock with a thick wear layer, and a refinished solid oak floor is still a solid oak floor — it keeps its full future: several more sandings over the decades, versus a new floor that starts its own clock. Second, disruption: refinishing is measured in days and you stay home; replacement is measured in weeks of demo dust and staging. Two things favor replacement, and we’ll say them plainly. If you want a fundamentally different floor — wide plank instead of 2¼-inch strip, white oak instead of red, a different direction or pattern — no amount of sanding gets you there; refinishing restores a floor, it doesn’t redesign it. And if the floor has been sanded thin over the decades, refinishing may simply be off the menu; our guide on when floors can’t be refinished covers how we check, and it’s the first thing we look at during a Bellevue estimate.

The Decision, Compressed

Refinish the oak when…

  • The boards are solid, flat, and still have wear layer above the tongue
  • You like (or can learn to like) the species and board width you own
  • Budget matters — the gap is $2,400 to $8,000+ on this size floor
  • You need the house livable throughout — refinishing is 3–5 days, occupied
  • You’re selling: refinished original oak reads as an asset, at a third the spend

Replace it when…

  • The floor is sanded to the nail heads, buckled, or water-rotten
  • You want a different species, plank width, or layout — sanding can’t redesign
  • Large areas are patched with mismatched or damaged wood
  • Subfloor problems need the floor open anyway
  • It’s engineered wood with no wear layer left — check before assuming either way

Refinish vs Replace: What Bellevue Homeowners Ask Us

Is it really 2–3 times cheaper to refinish than replace?
On this floor, yes. 800 sq ft refinished natural is $3,192 at $3.99/sq ft. Replacement is $3–$4.25/sq ft labor plus $4–$10+/sq ft materials — $5,600 to $11,400+ before tear-out and disposal. Even the cheapest replacement scenario costs meaningfully more than a stained refinish, and the typical scenario costs two to three times as much.
When is replacement clearly the right call?
When the floor is out of wood — sanded down to nail heads or the tongue after decades of refinishes — or structurally gone from water damage, rot, or widespread buckling. Also when you want a genuinely different floor: wider planks, a different species, a new layout. Refinishing restores what exists; it can't redesign it.
How do you know if our oak has enough wood left to refinish?
We check exposed edges at vents and thresholds and measure the wear layer above the tongue. Solid oak starts with roughly three-quarters of an inch and can typically be fully sanded several times over its life. Most 1980s–2000s Bellevue oak we inspect has been sanded once or never — plenty of future left.
Does refinishing count as 'new floors' for resale purposes?
Functionally, yes. A refinished solid oak floor photographs and shows like new, and buyers respond to the surface, not the install date. Original hardwood in good condition is a selling point in Eastside listings — agents advertise it. Spending three times more on replacement rarely returns the difference at sale.
What if our floor is engineered hardwood, not solid?
Then the answer lives in the wear layer — the real-wood veneer above the plywood core. A thick one (3mm+) can often be refinished once or twice; a thin one can't be sanded at all, and replacement becomes the honest recommendation. We measure it at an edge or registry vent before quoting either path.
Can we just replace the damaged boards and refinish everything?
Often, yes — and it's the best-value repair there is. We weave new oak boards into the damaged area, then sand and finish the whole floor so the patch disappears into one continuous surface. A room with one water-damaged corner almost never needs full replacement.
How long does each option take?
Refinishing 800 sq ft: typically 3–5 days, and you can live in the house throughout with the waterborne finishes we use. Replacement: demolition, subfloor prep, installation, and then — for site-finished hardwood — the entire finishing process on top. Realistically a week or more, with more dust and displacement.
Do you offer financing for either option?
Yes, financing is available on both refinishing and installation projects. Given that replacement of this floor can run past $11,000 while a refinish is close to $3,200, plenty of clients finance a replacement but pay for a refinish outright. Either way you'll have exact written numbers before deciding.
