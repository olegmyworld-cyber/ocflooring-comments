Why the HVAC System Is the First Thing We Deal With

Sanding dust falls into two categories: the heavy stuff that drops within a few feet of the machine, and the fine airborne fraction that hangs in the air for hours. The fine fraction is what escapes rooms — and a running forced-air system is purpose-built to move it. Return vents pull room air (and everything suspended in it) into the ductwork, the blower pushes it through the furnace, and the supply runs distribute it house-wide. That’s the mechanism behind dust two floors from the work area: it didn’t drift there, it was delivered. Worse, some of it settles inside the ducts and keeps shedding into the airstream for weeks after the crew is gone.

So before a sander starts, the protocol is simple and non-negotiable: the system goes off while machines run, and every supply and return vent in and adjacent to the work area gets sealed with film and tape. Floor vents in the sanding area get covered anyway to keep debris out of the duct boots — a detail that matters more than it sounds, because an open floor duct is a funnel straight into your ductwork. In a December refinish the furnace isn’t off for days, to be clear: it’s off during sanding hours, and back on around them — the finish itself wants normal room temperatures to dry and cure properly, so keeping the house comfortable is part of doing the job right, not a courtesy.

Layer Two: Catching Dust at the Machine

The biggest change since the horror-story era is that professional sanders are now vacuum-assisted — dust gets pulled off the drum or discs at the point of contact, before it reaches the air, and lands in sealed collection rather than a cloth bag leaking at the seams. Our final-pass machine, the Lägler TRIO, is particularly good at this: its enclosed multi-disc design keeps the fine finish-sanding dust — the most airborne kind — contained. Edging work along walls and in corners is the dustiest part of any refinish, which is why edgers run with vacuum extraction too, and why you’ll see us vacuum as we go rather than saving cleanup for the end.

We’ve written a fuller comparison of dustless vs traditional sanding and an explainer on what “dust-free” refinishing actually involves, but the short version: source capture removes the large majority of dust before it ever becomes your problem. What it can’t do is repeal physics — some fine dust still escapes, which is what the next layer is for.

The Honest Section: What “Dustless” Doesn’t Mean

No one sands wood without making dust, and any contractor who promises literally zero is selling adjectives. What modern process changes is where the dust ends up: overwhelmingly in the machines’ collection systems instead of your air and furnishings. What remains, realistically, is a light fine film close to the work area — wiped up in normal post-job cleaning — and whatever the crew’s discipline lets escape, which is why process matters more than equipment brochures.

Two things happen at the end of every job that are part of dust control even though they don’t look like it. First, the floor itself gets vacuumed and tack-wiped to surgical standards before each coat — dust in the finish is a quality defect we’d have to fix, so our incentives and yours point the same direction. Second, our parting advice, which we’ll repeat here in writing: replace your furnace filter after the job. The filter spent the week catching whatever fine dust made it to the return side, and a loaded filter re-shedding into the airstream is where “the dust came back” stories come from. It’s a $20 errand that finishes the job. If anyone in the house is sensitive, run the fan with the fresh filter for an afternoon and the air is done clearing. Pair that with the low-VOC waterborne finishes we use — covered in our guide to refinishing with kids and pets at home — and the whole project stays livable.

Dust, Ducts, and Refinishing: What Mercer Island Homeowners Ask

Should the HVAC system really be off during floor sanding?
While the sanders are running, yes. A running forced-air system pulls fine dust into the return ducts and distributes it through the whole house, and some of it settles in the ductwork and sheds for weeks afterward. We shut the system down during sanding hours, seal the vents, and run heat around the work windows — the house stays comfortable and the ducts stay clean.
What does the $250 dust containment option include?
Floor-to-ceiling plastic barrier walls sealing the work zone off from the rest of the house — across open doorways, stair openings, and pass-throughs — with zippered doors for crew access. Combined with sealed vents and vacuum-assisted machines, it keeps the living side of the house essentially untouched.
Is dustless sanding actually dust-free?
No, and we'd rather say so than surprise you. Vacuum-assisted equipment captures the large majority of dust at the source, but no sanding process is literally dust-free — expect a light, fine film near the work area, cleaned up in normal post-job wiping. The horror-story version, dust through the whole house, is what the HVAC protocol and containment prevent.
Do I need to replace my furnace filter after refinishing?
Yes — it's the single best follow-up you can do. The filter catches whatever fine dust reached the return side during the project, and a loaded filter keeps shedding into the airstream. Swap it when the job wraps, run the fan for an afternoon, and the air is done clearing.
Can we live in the house during sanding?
Most of our clients do. Sanding is concentrated in the first day or two, and with vents sealed, containment up where the layout calls for it, and low-VOC waterborne finishes on the coating days, the house stays livable throughout. Households with significant asthma or allergies sometimes choose to be out for sanding day specifically — that's the cautious version, not the required one.
Which part of refinishing makes the most dust?
Edge and corner work with the edger, followed by the coarse first cuts with the big machine. Both run with vacuum extraction, but it's why the crew vacuums progressively rather than once at the end, and why the fine final sanding is done with the enclosed multi-disc TRIO.
Will dust in the air ruin the finish itself?
It can — dust settling into wet finish leaves visible nibs, which is a redo. That's why the floor is vacuumed and tack-wiped before every coat and why containment helps the finish quality, not just your bookshelves. Our incentive to control dust is the same as yours.
Do you cover the floor vents even if the heat is staying off?
Yes. An open floor vent in a sanding area is a funnel into the duct boot — debris that lands there blows back out for months. Every floor register in the work area gets sealed regardless of what the system is doing.
Does containment change the project timeline?
Barely — setup and teardown happen inside the normal 3-to-5-day window. The plastic goes up before the first cut and comes down after the final coat is dry enough, and the finish schedule runs exactly as it would without it.
