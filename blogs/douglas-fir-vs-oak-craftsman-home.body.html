Ten-Second Identification: Which Wood Is This?

You don’t need a lab. Look at the grain. Oak has open pores you can see (sometimes feel), flecks and rays in the surface, and often a flame-shaped “cathedral” pattern down the board. Douglas fir is the opposite: smooth, pore-less surface with straight, tightly spaced parallel grain lines — old vertical-grain fir looks like the edge of a closed book. Color helps too, once finish is off: red oak leans pink-tan, white oak grayish-brown, fir a warm honey that deepens to rose-orange with sun and years. And location is the strongest hint of all in a Craftsman: formal rooms up front are usually oak, everything behind the swinging door is usually fir.

If your oak-vs-oak question is red versus white, that’s a separate comparison we’ve already written — red oak vs white oak. In Craftsman-era Seattle homes, the front-room oak is most often red oak, sometimes quarter-sawn white oak in the grander houses.

What the Hardness Gap Actually Means

The Janka scale puts numbers on what your floor already knows: red oak sits at 1290, white oak around 1360, and Douglas fir at 660 — half as resistant to denting. In practice, oak shrugs off most of daily life and records only the greatest hits: dropped cast iron, stiletto heels, the piano move. Fir records more of the everyday — chair legs, dog nails, gravel in a boot tread — and a century-old fir floor wears those marks by the thousand.

Here’s the reframe we offer at estimates, honestly: on a hundred-year-old floor, that softness reads as patina, not damage. Old-growth fir has growth rings far tighter than anything milled today, which makes it tougher than its Janka number suggests and gives the wear an even, burnished character — closer to a farmhouse table than a scratched countertop. If you want a floor that hides evidence, oak is genuinely the better tool. If you want the house’s history visible underfoot, fir was never a downgrade.

Under the Sander: Two Different Animals

Oak is the wood sanding equipment was effectively designed around: dense enough to cut evenly, forgiving of grit choices, hard to ruin. Fir is the opposite temperament — so soft that an aggressive cut or a moment’s hesitation with a drum sander digs a trench, and its dramatic earlywood/latewood bands can wash out into ripples under careless abrasion. Sanding fir well means lighter passes, finer grit progressions, and machines that flatten rather than gouge — our Lägler TRIO’s three-disc final pass earns its keep on fir more than anywhere else. This, bluntly, is why rental-sander weekends end badly on fir floors more than any other wood in Seattle.

The payoff for care is outsized: freshly sanded vertical-grain fir, with its tight stripes running room to room, is one of the best-looking floors in the Pacific Northwest, full stop. We wrote a whole field guide on it — refinishing fir floors in old Seattle homes — because the wood deserves the specialization.

Color and Finish: Where the Two Woods Part Ways Completely

Oak accepts stain the way it accepts everything else — evenly and on command. Natural, honey, mid-brown, espresso: the front rooms of a Craftsman can be any of them, and our stain color guide walks that decision. Fir mostly refuses. Its density flips between growth bands, so pigment grabs unevenly and the result blotches — the classic failed experiment is a fir hallway someone tried to stain to match the oak dining room. Our standing advice: stain the oak if you like, keep the fir natural, and let each wood be excellent at what it is.

Finish chemistry matters double on fir. Oil-modified poly ambering on red oak reads traditional; the same amber stacked on fir’s already-warm tone lands at pumpkin, and it keeps going for years. A clear waterborne — the Bona and Pallmann systems we run, 2–3 coats — holds fir at its true honey and keeps oak’s stain the color you chose. The full argument is in water-based vs oil-based, but for fir it isn’t close.

Which Wood Wins? Depends on the Question

Fir rewards you when…

  • You love visible grain and a floor with a century of character
  • The finish plan is natural + clear waterborne — no stain
  • Patina reads as charm to your household, not damage
  • You want the authentic Northwest Craftsman material
  • The boards are original old-growth — tighter than anything sold today

Oak is the easier floor when…

  • Big dogs, kids, and heavy traffic run the household
  • You want a stain color — light, gray, or dark
  • You’d rather not baby the sanding or the furniture pads
  • Future repairs matter: new oak boards match, new fir doesn’t
  • You prefer wear invisible for as long as possible

Fir vs Oak: What Seattle Craftsman Owners Ask

How do I tell if my floors are Douglas fir or oak?
Check the grain: oak has visible pores, flecks, and often a flame-shaped cathedral pattern; fir is smooth with straight, tightly spaced parallel lines. In a Craftsman the layout usually answers it too — oak in the formal front rooms, fir in bedrooms, halls, and the kitchen. Many Seattle houses have both.
Is Douglas fir too soft to be a good floor?
No — it's proven otherwise for a century. At Janka 660 it dents far more easily than oak, but old-growth fir's tight rings make it tougher than the number implies, and its wear pattern reads as even patina rather than damage. It asks for felt pads and trimmed dog nails; in exchange you get a floor nobody can buy new.
Can you stain fir to match our oak floors?
Not convincingly, and we'll say so rather than sell it. Fir's alternating hard and soft growth bands absorb stain unevenly, so it blotches — and a stained fir hallway next to stained oak looks worse than two honest woods side by side. Keep fir natural, stain the oak if you like, and unify with one finish and sheen.
Do fir and oak cost the same to refinish?
Yes — $3.99/sq ft for a full sand with natural finish on either, $6.50/sq ft with stain (which in practice means the oak, since we rarely stain fir). Fir demands more careful sanding technique, but that's our problem to manage, not a line on your invoice.
Which handles dogs better?
Oak, clearly — roughly twice the dent resistance, and dog nails that mark fir within months take years to read on oak. If you have large dogs and are choosing which rooms they live in, put them on the oak. On fir, a matte or satin sheen hides nail marks considerably better than anything glossier.
Can damaged boards be replaced in each wood?
Oak, easily — red oak strip is still milled today and blends after finishing. Original fir is the hard one: modern fir is fast-growth with wide, soft rings that won't match century-old vertical grain, so good repairs come from salvage yards. It's a solvable problem, but it's why we tell fir owners to protect what they have.
Do the two woods need different finishes?
Same system, different stakes. We run 2–3 coats of clear waterborne on both — but on fir the choice is critical, because ambering oil-based finish pushes fir's warm tone to orange within a few years. On oak, oil's amber is a defensible taste; on fir it's the most common mistake we're hired to undo.
We're refinishing the whole main floor — one job or two?
One job, always. Sanding both woods in the same project with the same finish and sheen is exactly how a mixed-wood Craftsman ends up looking coherent. Splitting the work across seasons or contractors is how the doorway seam starts announcing itself.
