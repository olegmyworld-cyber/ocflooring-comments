Why Nobody Sands Under Cabinets (Including Us)

Three reasons, in order of importance. First, there’s no wear to fix: finish fails from foot traffic, grit, water, and sunlight, and the wood under a cabinet box has seen none of those since the day the kitchen was installed. Second, the machines physically can’t get there — a drum sander needs open floor, an edger needs a few inches of clearance, and a toe kick offers neither. Third, and this is the one that matters financially: getting access would mean pulling cabinets, which means disconnecting plumbing and possibly disturbing countertops — real remodel money and real remodel risk — to refinish wood that will be re-covered by the same cabinets a week later.

What we do instead is sand and finish to the visible edge: up to the toe kicks, around the island, into the corners with edgers and hand work. Done properly, the transition is invisible — the finish line lives in the shadow of the toe kick where no eye and no light reaches. This is standard practice on every kitchen we do, and it’s one reason the kitchen doesn’t carry a premium over any other room in our pricing.

Appliances Are the Exception — They Move

Cabinets stay; appliances don’t. The floor under a stove, refrigerator, or washer and dryer is different in kind from the floor under cabinets, because someday that appliance will be pulled out — for repair, replacement, or a remodel — and whatever the floor looks like under there will suddenly be on display, possibly in the middle of your next kitchen’s new open layout. So we move them and sand under them: $80 per appliance, $160 for a washer/dryer pair, which covers the labor of disconnecting, walking them out on protection, and reinstalling after the final coat cures.

Homeowners sometimes ask to skip this to save the fee. We’ll do the job either way, but we make the case once: an unsanded appliance bay means old finish, old color, and often old vinyl or grime lines sitting a quarter-inch from your freshly refinished floor, and refrigerators have a way of migrating three inches during cleaning day. For the cost of a dinner out, the whole kitchen matches. The details of how furniture and appliances are handled during a job are in our guide to storing furniture during refinishing.

The Real Problem: When the Cabinet Footprint Changes

Here’s where the hidden wood stops being a curiosity and becomes a line item. If you remodel a kitchen and the new cabinet layout doesn’t match the old one — an island moves, a peninsula disappears, the run gets shorter — you expose wood that was covered for forty years next to wood that’s been walked on for forty years. Two problems appear at once. If the flooring was installed around the old cabinets rather than under them, there are gaps with no wood at all, which need lace-in repairs: new boards woven into the old floor, matched for species and width. And even where the wood is continuous, the covered boards are lighter — no sun, no wear — so the floor has a ghost footprint of the old kitchen.

The fix for both is the same and it works: repair first, then a full sand and refinish of the entire connected area, which erases the color difference because everything is cut back to fresh wood and finished as one surface. What doesn’t work is spot-treating — new finish feathered into an old floor never disappears, a problem we cover honestly in our scratch-repair guide. Budget-wise: lace-in repair is priced by the scope of the patchwork, then the refinish itself is the normal $3.99/sq ft over the whole area. On red oak — which is what’s under most local kitchens — matching boards are easy to source and the repairs vanish completely after sanding.

Remodeling? Get the Order of Operations Right

If a kitchen remodel is anywhere on your horizon, the sequencing rule is simple: cabinets first, floors second. Set the new cabinets, let the trades finish their damage, then have us repair, sand, and finish the floor up to the new toe kicks. Refinishing before a remodel is paying to finish wood that’s about to be covered, scratched by installers, or cut open for plumbing. The one nuance is flooring extent: if the new layout exposes areas the old floor never covered, the lace-in work happens between cabinet set and sanding. We coordinate this with kitchen contractors regularly — the floor is the last trade in, every time. The same logic applies to baseboards and shoe molding, which should go on after the finish cures, not before the sander shows up.

Hardwood Under Kitchen Cabinets: What Homeowners Ask Us

Do you refinish the wood under our kitchen cabinets?
No, and neither does anyone else — the machines can't reach it, the wood isn't worn, and getting access would mean removing the cabinets themselves. We sand and finish up to the toe kicks, and the finish line hides in their shadow. The result reads as one seamless floor.
Will we see a line where you stopped at the cabinets?
Not on a properly done job. The cut line sits under the toe kick overhang, out of sight and out of light. Where floors meet appliances — which do get moved and sanded under — there's no line at all because the finish is continuous.
Do you move the stove and refrigerator?
Yes. Appliances get disconnected, moved out on floor protection, and reinstalled after the finish cures — $80 per appliance, $160 for a washer and dryer pair. We recommend it every time: appliance bays are visible whenever the appliance shifts, and someday that appliance is coming out for good.
Our kitchen has vinyl over hardwood. Can you restore the wood?
Usually, yes — this is one of the most common situations in Lynnwood-era houses. We remove the vinyl and adhesive, assess the oak underneath, and refinish it with the rest of the floor. The wood under vinyl is often in excellent condition. The catch is at the cabinet line, where the vinyl-era floor may have been cut around the boxes.
We're planning a remodel that moves the island. What happens to the floor?
Wherever cabinetry used to stand, you'll have either bare gaps (if the floor was installed around the cabinets) or unweathered lighter wood. We lace in matching boards where wood is missing, then sand and refinish the entire connected area so old, new, and hidden wood all read as one color. Done in that order, the old footprint disappears completely.
Should we refinish before or after a kitchen remodel?
After, always. New cabinets go in first, then we repair, sand, and finish up to the new toe kicks as the final trade. Refinishing first means paying to finish wood the remodel will cover or damage.
Does refinishing a kitchen cost more per square foot than other rooms?
No — the rate is the same $3.99/sq ft for a natural refinish. Kitchens add cost only through extras that are priced transparently: appliance moves, vinyl removal if there's a layer over the wood, and any lace-in repairs. Hidden square footage under cabinets isn't measured or billed.
Is it safe to run the kitchen during the refinish?
Plan on a few days without it. Once we sand, the floor can't take foot traffic until the finish goes down, and appliances are reconnected after the final coat can bear weight — with the waterborne finishes we use, that's typically 48 to 72 hours after the last coat. Most families set up a temporary kitchen in the garage or dining room.
How do we find out what's under our kitchen floor without tearing it up?
Pull the kick plate under the dishwasher or look at the floor inside the fridge bay — remodel-era layers usually show their whole history there. Failing that, a floor register or a closet corner tells the same story. We check this during every free estimate.
