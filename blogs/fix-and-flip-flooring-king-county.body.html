Step One: Look Under the Carpet Before You Budget

King County’s mid-century housing stock — precisely the vintage that dominates flip inventory — was built when oak strip flooring was the default and wall-to-wall carpet was the upgrade that came later. In Renton’s 1950s–70s ramblers, in Burien, Skyway, Kent’s older plats, the odds that a carpeted living room has red oak underneath are genuinely good, and the oak has usually spent decades protected by the very carpet that hides it. Pull a corner back at a closet, check a floor vent, look at the stair nosing — five minutes with a pry bar before you write the scope can move four figures.

What you find dictates the budget line. Clean oak under carpet: refinish at $3.99/sq ft and take the win. Oak with pet stains, water marks, or a few bad boards: refinish plus per-board repairs — still usually far ahead of replacement, and our guide on refinish vs replace runs that math in detail. Painted, tiled-over, or missing sections: now you’re comparing patch-and-refinish against new material honestly. The only wrong move is scoping new flooring without looking.

The Flip Math: Refinish vs New, Line by Line

Take a representative 1,400 sq ft rambler with oak throughout. Refinishing: 1,400 × $3.99 ≈ $5,600, one trade, 3–5 days, and the listing gets “refinished original hardwoods” — language that photographs well and appraises as hardwood, because it is. Replacing the same footprint with new hardwood: $3–$4.25/sq ft labor plus $4–$10+ materials lands somewhere between $10,000 and $20,000 before demolition and disposal of what’s there now. LVP splits the difference on cost but not on buyer reaction — at entry-level price points buyers accept it as normal; in move-up neighborhoods, listing photos full of vinyl plank read as “flip grade,” and buyers have learned the term.

The speed comparison is just as lopsided in refinishing’s favor, with one caveat. The sand-and-finish itself fits inside any renovation schedule, but it must come nearly last — after paint, after cabinets, after every other trade has carried tools across the floor. Finish cures for 7 days before rugs and staging; plan the sequence so floor day is the second-to-last thing that happens before photos. Investors who slot us in mid-renovation pay for it in touch-ups.

Where Refinishing Isn’t the Answer

An honest decision tree has exit ramps. Refinishing loses when the hardwood itself is past saving: long water damage runs with cupped, delaminated, or rotted boards; floors sanded so many times the tongue is exposed — the signs a floor can’t be refinished are checkable in one walkthrough. It loses in kitchens and baths that are getting gutted anyway, where subfloor surgery makes patching-in new oak or switching materials more sensible. And it’s unavailable where the house simply has no hardwood — plywood under carpet in every room means you’re choosing among new materials, and the choice should track the neighborhood’s price point, not the supplier’s closeout aisle. The honest pros and cons of LVP apply doubly when a buyer’s inspector will walk every board.

One more exit ramp that investors under-use: partial scope. Refinish the oak in the main living areas where photos are made, put quality LVP in the basement rec room, and don’t pretend the two need to match. Buyers accept different flooring on different levels; they notice cheap flooring everywhere.

Working a Refinish into a Flip Schedule

An empty flip is the best-case refinishing site, and the schedule shows it: no furniture, no occupants, no phasing — the full 3–5 day timeline with zero of the usual friction. A few scheduling facts to build around. Sanding is the loud, dusty part (we run dust containment for +$250 when the paint’s already done and you want it kept clean). Coats go down the same day sanding ends with waterborne finish. Light foot traffic is fine the next day, other trades’ boots and ladders are not — full cure takes about 7 days, and staging should respect it. If the property still has appliances to shuffle, that’s $80 each, $160 for a washer/dryer pair.

The finish choice for flips is mercifully simple: natural, clear, satin or matte, done with a commercial waterborne system. Stain adds $2.51/sq ft, a day, and a taste opinion the next owner may not share — the same logic that applies to pre-listing refinishes applies harder to flips, where nobody upstairs has a favorite color. Natural red oak under a clear coat is the most market-proof floor in the region.

Flip Flooring: What Investors Ask Us

Is it cheaper to refinish or replace hardwood in a flip?
Refinishing, by a wide margin, whenever the existing floor is sound. A natural refinish is $3.99 per square foot all-in; new hardwood runs $3–$4.25 in labor plus $4–$10 or more in materials, before demo and disposal. On a typical 1,400 sq ft rambler that's roughly $5,600 versus $10,000–$20,000.
How do I know if there's hardwood under the carpet?
Pull back a corner in a closet, lift a floor vent register, or check the stair nosing — five minutes tells you. In King County homes built from the 1940s through the early 1970s, oak strip under carpet is the norm rather than the exception, especially in Renton's Boeing-era neighborhoods.
How long does refinishing add to a flip schedule?
The work itself is 3–5 days for a full refinish, or one day for a screen and recoat. The scheduling constraint is sequence: floors come after paint and cabinets, and the finish wants about 7 days of cure before staging furniture and rugs. Slot it second-to-last before photos and it costs you nothing extra.
Should we refinish or just install LVP everywhere?
If real hardwood exists, refinish it — 'original hardwoods' outperforms vinyl plank in listing response at almost every price point, and it's usually cheaper than a quality LVP install anyway. LVP earns its place where there's no hardwood to save, in basements, and in wet rooms. Covering sound oak with vinyl is paying to remove value.
What finish should a flip use — stain or natural?
Natural, satin or matte, with a clear commercial waterborne. It photographs light and current, offends no buyer, and skips the extra $2.51 per square foot and added day that staining costs. Stain makes sense only when matching existing floors or chasing a specific high-end comp look.
Can you fix pet stains and damaged boards during a flip refinish?
Yes — individual board replacement is routine and quoted per repair. Light stains often sand out; black urine-stained boards get cut out and replaced with matching stock before finishing. Budget a contingency for a few boards on any carpeted floor you haven't seen yet, since some damage only shows once carpet and pad come up.
Do empty houses refinish faster or better?
Both. No furniture moves, no phasing around occupants, no compromises on scheduling coats — the crew works the whole footprint straight through. An empty flip is the ideal refinishing site, which is one reason the numbers favor investors more than almost any other client type.
What's the minimum job size you take on?
500 square feet. Below that, per-foot pricing stops reflecting our fixed costs and we'd rather say so upfront. Most whole-house flip scopes clear the minimum several times over; a single small room might not, which is worth knowing when scoping partial work.
Does refinished hardwood actually appraise better than LVP?
Appraisers list hardwood as hardwood and vinyl as vinyl, and comps in most King County neighborhoods reward the former. More decisively, buyers do: refinished original oak is a headline feature in listing copy, and vinyl plank never is. The appraisal gap is real but the buyer-reaction gap is the one that moves your sale price.
