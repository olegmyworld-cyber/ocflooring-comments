The Rate Is Fixed. The Total Isn’t.

We publish three numbers and we charge them: $1.99/sq ft to screen and recoat, $3.99/sq ft to sand and finish natural, $6.50/sq ft to sand and finish with stain. We don’t quote a higher rate for a tricky room or a lower one for an easy one. That’s partly a fairness thing and partly a practical one — a variable rate is impossible for a homeowner to check, and the whole point of publishing a rate is that you can.

Which means the interesting question isn’t “what’s your rate,” it’s “what’s in scope.” The total is the rate times an area, plus stairs counted individually, plus a short list of named add-ons, plus whatever repairs the floor needs. Every large gap we’ve ever investigated between two quotes came out of one of those four buckets. If you want the anatomy of a single estimate line by line, we did that separately in what you’re actually paying for in a line-item estimate; this piece is about why two of them differ.

Variable One: Which Service Your Floor Actually Qualifies For

This is the biggest swing by a distance, and it is not a choice so much as a diagnosis. A screen and recoat at $1.99 abrades the existing finish and lays a fresh coat over it — one day, half the price, and it only works if the finish film is still continuous everywhere. A full sand at $3.99 takes the floor to raw wood and is the only option once traffic has worn through to bare fiber, or once there are stains, deep scratches or a color change involved. Adding stain puts it at $6.50, because it inserts water-popping, application and a full dry cycle before any finish coat goes on.

On a 1,000 sq ft floor those are $1,990, $3,990 and $6,500 — the same room, three totals, and a homeowner comparing a recoat quote against a sand quote will conclude one contractor is gouging them when the two bids simply aren’t for the same work. The test is quick: flick water on the worst traffic lane. If it beads, you may still be a recoat. If it darkens the wood in seconds, the film is gone there and you’re a sand. Variable Two: The Square Footage Number Itself

Ask three people the size of a floor and you’ll get three answers, because they’re measuring different things. The number on a listing sheet is the house’s gross area — it includes bathrooms, the garage, the unfinished basement and the wall thicknesses. The number a contractor should be quoting is the wood-floor area they will actually sand and coat, which in a typical house is meaningfully smaller.

Our rule is simple: we measure what gets finished. Closets count if they’re in scope. The floor under a refrigerator, range or washer counts, because we finish under there rather than stopping at the appliance. The footprint of fixed cabinetry doesn’t, because we don’t sand under permanent cabinets. Hallways, thresholds and the landing at the top of the stairs all count. If a quote arrives with a square footage noticeably larger than your wood area, ask where the number came from — a bid built on a listing figure is either padded now or getting revised later. Variable Three: Everything That Isn’t Floor

Stairs are the biggest and most frequently omitted. They’re priced per tread — $55 to $75 — because there’s almost no machine work in them: each tread is hand-sanded, the nosing is shaped by hand, risers and stringers are detailed separately, and the coating schedule has to leave the household a way upstairs. A missing stair line is the single most common reason a quote looks cheap; the full breakdown is in stair refinishing cost per step.

Appliance moves run $160 for a washer/dryer pair and $80 each for others — a disconnect-and-reset charge, removable if you move them yourself. Dust containment is $250: barriers at openings, sealed returns, and containment maintained through sanding, which we recommend whenever the household stays in the house. Repairs are quoted per situation, because a single split board and a water-stained section under a dishwasher are not the same job. Black staining in particular can change a project’s shape entirely — sometimes it sands out, sometimes it doesn’t, and the honest limits are in can black stains be sanded out. Most of the surprises people report are on this list, and nearly all of them are visible at the estimate if somebody looks; we catalogued them in the hidden costs in refinishing quotes.

What Seattle Houses Do to the Job (and What They Don’t)

Here’s a distinction worth making, because it’s where we differ from some contractors. Seattle’s older housing stock is genuinely harder to work in than a 2005 tract house. The Craftsman and bungalow inventory across Wallingford, Ballard, Phinney and Mount Baker is divided into small rooms, which means a much higher ratio of edge and corner work — the slow, hand-guided part of sanding — to open drum passes. Third-floor walk-ups in Capitol Hill mean carrying machines up two flights. Narrow streets and permit-only parking around Fremont and the U District mean the truck can’t always sit where we need it, and built-ins, radiators and floor registers all add hand time.

None of that changes our rate. It changes how many days we’re on site, which is our problem to absorb, not a surcharge to pass on. What we will do is tell you honestly at the estimate that a chopped-up 1920s upper floor is a four-day job rather than a three-day one, because your calendar deserves the real number even when your invoice doesn’t change.

The Seattle variable that does move money is history. Houses built in the 1900s–1930s have often been sanded two or three times already, and a floor with limited thickness left may not be a candidate for a full sand at all — which pushes it toward a $1.99 recoat, or occasionally toward replacement. That measurement is the first thing we take in an old house, and the reasoning is in how many times a floor can be refinished and in our walkthrough of refinishing a 1920s Seattle bungalow. Small floors are the other one: below the 500 sq ft minimum the arithmetic stops scaling, and we explain how we group those jobs in minimum job sizes. Refinishing Price Questions We Get in Seattle

Why did my neighbor pay less for the same square footage?
Almost always because it wasn't the same job. The most common explanations, in order: they got a recoat and you're getting a full sand, their quote didn't include stairs, or the two square footages were measured differently. Ask what service level their quote named and whether stairs were on it.
Do you charge more for houses with lots of small rooms?
No. Our rate is flat regardless of layout. Small rooms mean much more edge and hand work, which costs us time rather than costing you money — it typically adds a day to the schedule. We'd rather tell you the honest number of days than build a complexity surcharge nobody can verify.
How do you measure square footage?
We measure the floor we're going to finish. Closets count if they're in scope, the area under a refrigerator or range counts because we finish under there, and fixed cabinetry doesn't. That number is usually meaningfully smaller than the square footage on a listing sheet, which includes bathrooms, garage and unfinished space.
Is a per-square-foot price even the right way to quote this?
For the sanding and coating, yes — that work scales almost linearly with area. What doesn't scale gets its own line: stairs by the tread, appliance moves, containment, repairs. Blending all of it into one rate would make the number impossible to check, which is the opposite of what a rate is for.
What's the cheapest my floor could possibly be?
If the finish is still continuous, a screen and recoat at $1.99 per square foot, done in a day, subject to the 500 square foot minimum. That's the whole reason we test the film before quoting — we'd rather sell the cheap service to a floor that qualifies than the expensive one to a floor that didn't need it.
Does the wood species change the price?
Not our rate. Oak, maple, fir, hickory and jatoba all get the same per-square-foot number even though they behave differently under the machines. Species does affect what we recommend — soft old fir at 660 on the Janka scale gets a gentler approach than hickory at 1820 — but that's a method conversation, not a pricing one.
Why is there a 500 square foot minimum?
Because the fixed costs of a job don't shrink with the room. Mobilization, masking, containment, machine setup and the final clean cost roughly the same on 300 square feet as on 1,300. Pricing a tiny job at the per-foot rate would mean losing money on it, so we quote a minimum and are upfront about why.
Can I lower the total without lowering the quality?
Yes, and we'll tell you how. Move your own appliances and the line comes off. Skip containment if the house will be empty. Take the stairs in a later year. Refinish the main floor now and the bedrooms later. What we won't do is cut coats, skip grits, or drop the flattening pass, because those savings show up on the floor in about two years.
