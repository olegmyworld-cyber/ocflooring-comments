Where the “Summer Only” Rule Came From — and Why It Retired

The old advice was built on two facts that were once true everywhere. First, oil-modified polyurethane dries by solvent evaporation and oxidation, both of which crawl in cool, damp air — an oil job that cures on schedule in a dry July can stall badly in a Puget Sound November, with coats staying tacky for days. Second, oil fumes are brutal, so you wanted every window open, which is a summer activity. Under those constraints, summer really was the season.

Waterborne finishes dismantled both constraints. The Bona and Pallmann systems we run cure predictably indoors at normal room temperature and humidity — the house’s furnace, not the sky, sets the conditions — and at roughly 65 g/L VOC versus 450–550 g/L for oil poly, they don’t require the open-window ventilation strategy. We put down finish in every month of the year, and if you toured the results with the dates hidden, you couldn’t sort January floors from July floors. The one honest exception: if you specifically want oil-modified poly — a legitimate choice in some situations — the calendar still applies to you, and late spring through early fall is genuinely better for it here.

What Western Washington Weather Actually Does to Your Floor

The climate story that matters isn’t about the finish — it’s about the wood. Hardwood breathes with humidity: it takes on moisture and swells in our damp season, gives it back and shrinks when the heating season dries the indoor air. That’s why boards show hairline gaps in February that close up by August. Perfectly normal, and a refinish doesn’t stop it — finish slows moisture exchange; it doesn’t seal wood in amber.

The scheduling consequence is modest but real: a floor sanded and finished at a seasonal extreme locks its flatness in at that extreme. Sand a floor in the swollen height of a damp spring and the boards will shrink apart slightly come winter; sand it bone-dry in January and summer swelling pushes edges together. In practice, indoor HVAC flattens most of this out — a heated, ventilated home stays in a fairly narrow humidity band year-round — and the effect on a finished job is cosmetic hairlines, not failure. We mention it because it’s the one grain of truth in the seasonal folklore, not because it should drive your calendar. What should: whether your house can hold roughly 60–80°F with normal indoor humidity for the week of the job. If yes — and with a functioning furnace, it’s yes — you’re in season.

The Case for Booking in the Off-Season

Since the finish no longer cares about the month, the differences that remain are logistical, and they mostly favor the off-season. Fall and winter calendars are simply more open: you can pick your week instead of taking what’s left, reschedule without drama, and the crew on your floor isn’t racing to the next job. Summer is our compressed season — everyone wants floors done while they’re at the cabin, sellers want them done yesterday — and lead times stretch accordingly. (There’s a reason the industry writes posts like why spring is the best time for new flooring — demand seasons are real; quality seasons, with modern finishes, mostly aren’t.)

Winter jobs come with two practical notes. Keep the furnace running normally through the work and cure — the finish wants a lived-in house, not a cold one. And plan around the re-entry timeline the same as any season: socks the same evening, furniture at 48–72 hours, rugs at 7 days. Holiday hosting is the real winter constraint we schedule around — nobody wants furniture off the floors on December 24th.

The Everett Angle: Old Wood, Damp Air, Warm Houses

Everett is a useful stress test for all of this, because it combines some of the region’s oldest floors with some of its dampest air. The mill-era housing stock near downtown, Riverside, and the Delta neighborhood carries fir and oak that’s crossed a century of Puget Sound winters; the postwar and newer homes toward Silver Lake and the south end mostly run oak over conventional framing. We refinish both all year. The century-old floors actually argue hardest for heating-season work: those houses breathe more than modern construction, and winter is when their indoor conditions are most stable — the furnace is on, the windows are shut, and the floor sits at a steady moisture level for weeks on end.

The damp-air caveat applies here more than most places: unheated spaces are off the menu from October to May. A vacant Everett rental with the power off in January is not a refinishing candidate until the heat runs for a few days first — we’ve pushed jobs a week for exactly this, and it’s cheaper than redoing a finish that cured badly at 48°F. So When Should You Book?

Work backward from your life, not the weather. Hosting Thanksgiving? Finish by early November — furniture needs 48–72 hours and rugs a week, per the standard timeline. Listing in spring? Book in late winter, before the pre-market scramble. Want the family gone during the work? Summer vacation weeks are genuinely convenient — just reserve them well ahead. Empty house between tenants? Whichever month it’s empty, with the heat left on.

The pricing footnote, because people ask: our rates don’t float with the season. A natural refinish is $3.99/sq ft and a screen & recoat is $1.99/sq ft in January and in July. The thing that varies is how long you wait for a start date — and that’s the strongest argument for the months everyone else avoids.

Refinishing Season in Western Washington: What Homeowners Ask

Can hardwood floors be refinished in winter in Western Washington?
Yes, without compromise — provided the house is heated. Waterborne finishes cure on indoor conditions, and a furnace-warmed home in January sits comfortably in the 60–80°F range they want. Winter is actually our most flexible booking season, and the results are indistinguishable from summer work.
Does rain affect floor refinishing?
Not directly — the work happens indoors and we're not opening windows to cure a waterborne finish. Rain matters indirectly: months of damp air raise indoor humidity in poorly heated spaces, which slows curing, and it genuinely drags out oil-based polyurethane jobs. In a normally heated home, a rainy week outside changes nothing.
Is there a best month to refinish hardwood floors?
For finish quality, no — any month works with modern waterborne systems in a heated home. For scheduling, October through February offers the best availability and the least competition for start dates. Summer is the most convenient for families who travel, and the busiest, so it requires the most lead time.
Why do floors have gaps in winter?
Heating season dries indoor air, wood gives up moisture, and boards shrink slightly across their width — hairline gaps appear, then close again in the damp months. It's normal seasonal movement, not damage, and refinishing neither causes nor cures it. A floor finished in winter simply locks in at its drier, slightly smaller state.
Should we wait for summer if we want an oil-based finish?
It genuinely helps. Oil-modified poly dries by evaporation and oxidation, both of which slow badly in cool, damp air — one coat a day becomes the optimistic case between October and May here. If a project calls for oil-based, late spring through early fall is the honest window. Waterborne removes the constraint entirely.
Do you charge more in busy season?
No. A natural refinish is $3.99 per square foot and a screen and recoat is $1.99 per square foot year-round, with the same 500 sq ft minimum. What changes with the season is lead time — summer start dates book out furthest, winter dates are often available within a week or two.
Does the house need to be heated for a winter refinish?
Yes — that's the one hard requirement. The finish wants normal living conditions, roughly 60–80°F, during application and for the cure. A vacant house with the power off in January needs the heat run for several days before we sand, so the wood and the air both settle at normal levels first.
Can we still open windows during a winter refinish?
You don't need to. The waterborne finishes we use run around 65 g/L VOC — low-odor enough that most families sleep home the same night. We ventilate as the finish schedule allows, but the open-every-window regime belongs to oil-based finishes and their 450–550 g/L fumes, not to modern waterborne work.
