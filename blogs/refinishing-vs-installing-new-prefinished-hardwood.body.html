The Math on a Typical Everett Main Floor

Take 1,000 sq ft of oak — a common main-floor footprint in Everett’s post-war neighborhoods. A full sand and natural refinish is $3,990. New prefinished hardwood on the same footprint starts around $7,000 at the bottom of the material range and passes $14,000 with mid-grade product, and that’s before demolition: the existing floor has to be pried up, hauled out, and disposed of, and the subfloor made ready — line items that don’t exist on a refinish. On most projects we quote, replacement lands at two to three times the cost of refinishing the same square footage, for a floor that is not two to three times better. The complete refinishing price breakdown is in our refinishing cost guide, and we’ve run the refinish-or-replace numbers in more detail before — the gap has only widened as material prices have climbed.

The one cost argument that genuinely favors replacement: if your existing floor needs extensive board repairs, has been sanded down to the nails, or has water damage across large areas, the repair line items stack up and the gap narrows. That’s an inspection question, not a guess — which is why we look before we quote.

What New Prefinished Actually Buys You — the Honest Version

Prefinished flooring earns its popularity for real reasons, and pretending otherwise would be salesmanship. The finish is applied in a factory — usually an aluminum-oxide-infused coating cured under controlled conditions — and it’s hard-wearing. There’s no sanding dust in your house and no finish drying time: the crew nails the last board, and you walk on it that afternoon. If you want a species, width, or color your current floor can’t become — wide-plank hickory where you have narrow fir, for instance — replacement is the only way there. And if part of the project is adding hardwood to rooms that never had it, new material is obviously in the picture anyway.

Now the part the showroom doesn’t lead with. Each prefinished board is finished individually, so every edge carries a micro-bevel — a tiny V-groove between boards. Those grooves are there to disguise minor height differences between planks, and they collect dirt in a way a flat floor doesn’t. A site-finished floor is sanded flat as one surface after installation and then coated as a single monolithic film, seams sealed under the finish. It’s the difference between a floor made of boards and a floor that reads as one plane — most people can spot it from the doorway once they know to look. It’s also why prefinished floors are harder to refinish later: those bevels have to be sanded completely away first, which costs extra thickness. We’ve covered that problem in whether prefinished floors can be refinished and the broader trade-offs in prefinished vs site-finished.

What a Refinish Buys You

A full refinish takes the floor you own down to bare wood — flattening cups, erasing scratches, gray traffic paths, and most stains — and rebuilds the finish with 2–3 coats of commercial waterborne (we run Bona and Pallmann systems), with a Lägler TRIO multi-disc pass at the end for a flat, even surface. You choose the color at that point: natural, or any stain, on the wood that’s already there. Solid hardwood holds enough material for multiple refinishes over its life, which is why 80-year-old floors in this area are still going strong.

What refinishing can’t do: change the species, change the board width or direction, or save a floor with rot, extensive pet-stain penetration, or too little wood left above the tongue. When we see those, we say so — a floor at the end of its sandable life is the legitimate case for new material, and we wrote about when floors can’t be refinished so you can pre-check yours.

Refinish or Replace with Prefinished: What Everett Homeowners Ask

Is it cheaper to refinish hardwood or install new prefinished flooring?
Refinishing, by a wide margin, whenever the existing floor is sound. A full sand and natural refinish runs $3.99 per square foot. New prefinished hardwood costs $3 to $4.25 per square foot in installation labor plus $4 to $10 or more per square foot for the material, before tear-out and disposal of the old floor. On the same footprint, replacement typically costs two to three times as much.
How do I know if my existing floor can be refinished?
Solid hardwood with wood remaining above the tongue-and-groove can be refinished, usually several times over its life. The disqualifiers are widespread rot or water damage, deep pet-stain penetration across large areas, or a floor already sanded to the nail heads. A quick in-person look, or a photo of a floor vent edge showing board thickness, usually settles it.
Is prefinished hardwood more durable than a site-applied finish?
Factory aluminum-oxide coatings are very hard, but commercial two-component waterborne finishes like Bona Traffic HD are specified for restaurants and gyms and hold up excellently in homes. In practice both outlast the average time between refinishes. The bigger practical difference is the surface: site finishing seals the seams under one continuous film, while prefinished boards keep open micro-bevels at every edge.
What are micro-bevels and why do they matter?
Prefinished boards are coated individually at the factory, so each edge is eased into a small V-groove that hides height differences between planks. Those grooves collect dirt, telegraph a board-by-board look, and have to be sanded completely away before the floor can ever be refinished flat — which spends extra wood thickness.
How long does each option take?
A typical full refinish runs 3 to 5 days from first sanding pass to final coat, and with waterborne finish you can walk on it in socks the same evening the last coat goes down. A replacement runs longer: demolition, subfloor prep, material acclimation, then installation — days to a few weeks depending on scope.
Does refinishing add as much home value as new floors?
Usually more, per dollar spent. Buyers respond to the condition and color of the floor, not to whether the boards are new, and original old-growth wood in older Everett homes is a selling point new material can't replicate. Spending $4,000 to make existing hardwood look new typically returns better than spending $10,000-plus replacing it.
Can you match new prefinished flooring to my existing floor instead?
Sometimes, but it's the hardest way to do it. Prefinished colors rarely match an existing sanded floor exactly, and the bevel difference shows at the seam between old and new. Where floors must flow together, the cleaner approach is weaving in unfinished boards and refinishing the whole area as one surface.
My floor is engineered wood. Does this change the answer?
It can. Engineered floors refinish only if the wear layer above the plywood core is thick enough — roughly 2 mm or more for a careful single refinish, 3 mm or more for repeat sandings. Thin-veneer engineered products are effectively replace-only. We check the wear layer before quoting either path.
