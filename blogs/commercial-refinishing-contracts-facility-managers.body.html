Quick answer

The four questions that decide a commercial refinishing contract are: exactly what happens to the floor (grit sequence, product name, coat count, sheen), exactly when the building loses the space (phasing, after-hours access, reopening times), exactly what the contractor carries (license, workers’ comp, a certificate naming the building as additional insured), and exactly what happens afterward (warranty terms and a recoat cycle). Get all four in writing and two bids become comparable. Miss the third and a slip-and-fall on your lobby floor becomes your problem. For Bellevue buildings the schedule question is usually the expensive one — a waterborne system recoats in 2–3 hours and lets the space reopen the same evening in socks, while oil-modified poly takes a day per coat and roughly 30 days to cure.

What Makes a Commercial Floor Different From a Big Residential One

Mostly three things: the traffic, the clock, and the paperwork. Traffic means the finish spec has to be a commercial grade rather than a homeowner-grade product — the two-component waterbornes that get specified for gyms and restaurants exist because a lobby sees more foot traffic in a month than a hallway sees in a year. The clock means the work is phased around a building’s operating hours instead of a family’s patience, and phasing costs money that a residential square-foot rate doesn’t contain. The paperwork means insurance certificates, W-9s, badge access, lien releases, and a punch-list process, none of which show up on a kitchen-table estimate.

What doesn’t change is the physical work. Sanding is sanding: a coarse cut, finer grits to erase the previous scratch pattern, an edger at the perimeter, and a Lägler TRIO pass to blend the two so the floor reads flat under the unforgiving downlighting most commercial spaces have. If a bid doesn’t describe that sequence, it isn’t a scope — it’s a number.

Anchor pricing: our residential rates are $1.99/sq ft for a screen & recoat, $3.99 for a full sand with natural waterborne finish, $6.50 with stain, stairs at $55–$75 per tread, 500 sq ft minimum. Commercial jobs get quoted per project because phasing, after-hours labor, protection of adjacent finishes, and containment all move the number — but those rates are the floor the estimate is built up from, and we’ll show you the build-up rather than handing you a lump sum.

The Scope Questions: What Physically Happens to the Floor

Ask for the grit sequence in writing. A real sand names grits and a perimeter method. A bid that says “sand and finish” could be a full sand or a buffer with a screen, and those are different jobs at different prices with different results.

Ask for the product by brand and name, plus coat count and sheen. “Two coats of commercial-grade polyurethane” means nothing; “Bona Traffic HD, sealer plus two coats, satin” is a specification you can hold someone to and verify from the empty pails at the end. Ask specifically whether it’s a two-component (catalyzed) product, because that’s where the durability difference lives — we made the full case in why we run Bona Traffic HD.

Ask what’s excluded. Base and shoe removal and reinstallation, transitions and thresholds, stair treads and nosings, floor outlets and vent covers, moving fixtures or furniture, protecting adjacent carpet and tile, dust containment barriers, disposal. Every one of those is a line item somebody either priced or didn’t, and the difference between two bids is usually hiding in that list — which is exactly the pattern we described in hidden costs in refinishing quotes and what a line-item estimate is actually paying for.

Finally, ask how the floor gets measured and verified. Field-measured square footage beats a takeoff from a lease exhibit, and a written measurement means the invoice can’t drift.

The Access and Schedule Questions

This is where commercial jobs actually succeed or fail, and it’s the part with the least to do with flooring. Before signing, settle: which hours the crew has access, whether the work is nights and weekends or normal business hours in a phased section, who reserves the freight elevator and loading dock, who issues badges or escorts, where materials stage, where waste goes, and who notifies tenants. In a multi-tenant Bellevue building, a notification email that goes out two days late costs more goodwill than the floor is worth.

Then settle reoccupancy explicitly, because it’s the number your operations depend on. With the waterborne systems we use: coats go down 2–3 hours apart, the space takes sock traffic the same evening, furniture and rolling loads come back at 48–72 hours, and full cure is about seven days — which matters for when mats, rugs, and chair casters return. With oil-modified polyurethane it’s one coat per day, strong solvent odor throughout, and roughly a 30-day cure. For an occupied building that difference isn’t a preference, it’s the whole project plan. The occupied-space logic is the same one we walk through for households in low-VOC refinishing with people in the building.

If the space is a restaurant, a gym, or a worship hall, the phasing questions get sharper still, and we’ve written the specifics for each: refinishing around service hours in restaurants and bars, gymnasium floor refinishing, and church and community hall restoration.

The Paperwork: Insurance, Licensing, Warranty

Three documents before anyone mobilizes. A Washington contractor’s license in the company’s legal name — verifiable, current, bonded. A certificate of insurance showing general liability and workers’ compensation, with your ownership entity and property manager named as additional insured, sent directly from the carrier or agent rather than forwarded as a PDF by the contractor. And a written warranty with a term on it; ours is one year on workmanship, alongside whatever the finish manufacturer warrants on the product. We’ve been doing this since 2013 and past a thousand floors, and we’d rather send all three unprompted than have a manager chasing them the week before a start date.

Then the commercial terms: payment schedule and retainage, a change-order process that requires written approval before extra work happens, lien releases at each payment, and a punch-list walk with a defined window for correction. None of this is exotic. All of it is the difference between a dispute and a conversation.

Green flags in a commercial bid

Red flags

Commercial Refinishing Contracts: What Facility Managers Ask

Can the building stay open during refinishing?
With a low-VOC waterborne system, usually yes, in phased sections. Bona Traffic HD is around 65 g/L and GREENGUARD Gold certified, coats go down 2 to 3 hours apart, and a finished area takes sock traffic the same evening. With oil-modified polyurethane at 450 to 550 g/L and a coat per day, planning to stay open is not realistic.
How is commercial work priced compared to residential?
It's built up from the same rates — $1.99 per square foot for a screen and recoat, $3.99 for a full sand with natural waterborne finish, $6.50 with stain, stairs at $55 to $75 per tread — then adjusted for phasing, after-hours labor, protection of adjacent finishes, containment, and access constraints. We show the build-up rather than quoting a lump sum.
What insurance documentation should we require?
A current Washington contractor's license in the company's legal name, a certificate of insurance showing general liability and workers' compensation, and an additional insured endorsement naming your ownership entity and property manager. Ask for the certificate to come from the agent or carrier, and require it before mobilization rather than after.
How long before furniture and equipment can go back?
On a waterborne system, sock traffic the same evening, furniture and rolling loads at 48 to 72 hours, and full cure at about seven days. Chair mats, area rugs, and anything that traps moisture against the finish should wait for full cure. Those windows are what the phasing plan has to be built around.
Do you work nights and weekends?
Yes, and for most occupied commercial spaces that's the plan. After-hours work costs more in labor and needs access arrangements settled in advance — badges or escorts, freight elevator and dock reservations, staging space, and waste removal. All of it belongs in the contract, not in a phone call on day one.
How often should a commercial wood floor be recoated?
It depends on traffic, but the principle is to recoat on a schedule instead of sanding on a crisis. A screen and recoat at $1.99 per square foot adds finish without removing wood, and a floor kept on a recoat cycle can go decades without another full sand. Once wear reaches the wood, recoating no longer works and a full sand is the only option.
What if the floor has already been sanded several times?
Then remaining thickness becomes the governing constraint, and we measure before recommending anything. A floor near the end of its sanding life should be recoated, not sanded, and if it can't be recoated the honest conversation is about replacement. We'd rather tell you that at the walkthrough than after the machines are in the building.
What warranty do you provide on commercial work?
A one-year workmanship warranty in writing, alongside the finish manufacturer's own product warranty. We're licensed and insured, we've been doing this since 2013, and we're happy to put the term and what it covers directly in the contract so it isn't a matter of interpretation later.
