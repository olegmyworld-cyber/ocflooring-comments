“How long will this last?” is the question we get most often at the end of an estimate, and the honest answer is a range wide enough to be unsatisfying: somewhere between four years and thirty, depending on the room, the household, the product, and whether anyone puts a mat by the back door. What follows is what we tell people, including the parts that aren’t flattering to our own industry. Chief among them: a finish reaching the end of its life is not a failure and doesn’t mean you were sold something cheap. Finish is a consumable. It is supposed to wear out, the way brake pads are supposed to wear out, and the whole design of a well-maintained wood floor is that the film gets replaced periodically so the boards never have to be.

What Actually Lasts: The Film, Not the Floor

Almost every confused conversation about finish life comes from mixing up two different things. The wood is a structural material with a hundred-plus-year life; it doesn’t wear out so much as get sanded away a fraction of a millimeter at a time. The finish is a plastic film a few thousandths of an inch thick sitting on top of it, and it is the only thing between the wood and your shoes. Everything you can see happening to your floor — dulling, scuffing, traffic lanes, that grayish cast in front of the sink — is happening to the film.

The film wears from the top down and from the middle of the room outward along the routes people walk. Because it thins gradually rather than failing at once, there is a long window during which a floor looks tired but is still fully protected. That window is the whole ballgame. Inside it, a screen and recoat abrades the surface of the old film, bonds a fresh coat to it, and you are back to a full-thickness wear layer for one day of work and half the price of a sand. Outside it — once traffic has cut through to raw wood — a recoat can’t bond over exposed fiber, and the job becomes a full sand whether the rest of the floor needed one or not.

That’s why we push people to think in terms of the recoat clock instead of the failure date, and why the maintenance plan we lay out for the ten years after a refinish is mostly about catching the film before it opens up.

What Burns Through a Finish Early

Finish rarely dies of old age — it gets abraded to death, and almost always by the same short list. Grit is the whole game. Sand and small stone tracked in on shoes act like loose sandpaper underfoot, and in a Pacific Northwest winter the grit arrives wet and stays. Two good mats — one outside, one inside — and a habit of shoes-off at the door will genuinely add years to the lanes that wear first. Nothing else on this list comes close to that in leverage.

Dogs are second, and it’s claws rather than weight: an untrimmed nail concentrates a lot of force into a point that is harder than the film. Sun doesn’t abrade but it degrades, especially on south and west exposures, where the finish gets brittle and the wood beneath ambers or bleaches unevenly. The wrong cleaner is a slower killer than people expect — oil soaps, vinegar solutions, and anything marketed as a “shine restorer” leave a residue that dulls the surface and, more importantly, ruins adhesion when it comes time to recoat, which we go into in detail in our rundown of the best and worst floor cleaners. Steam mops drive moisture and heat into seams and shorten the film’s life at the joints. And rubber-backed or vinyl-backed rugs trap moisture and can leave a permanent print, which is why rug pad choice is not a trivial decision — we cover the safe ones in our guide to rugs and pads that don’t damage a finish.

Then there is the one nobody plans for: a slow leak. A dishwasher weeping at the door gasket or a fridge line dripping behind the cabinet doesn’t just kill the finish, it stains the wood black and can cup the boards, at which point you are past finish maintenance entirely.

Still in recoat territory when…

  • Water flicked onto the worst lane still beads for a minute or two
  • The floor looks dull and scuffed but the color is uniform end to end
  • Scratches are visible in raking light but a fingernail doesn’t catch
  • No gray, no black, no pale bare patches anywhere
  • It’s been under about ten years since the last sand or coat

Past a recoat — it needs a sand when…

  • Bare, pale, or fuzzy wood shows in a traffic lane or on stair noses
  • Water darkens the wood within seconds instead of beading
  • There are black stains, deep gouges, or cupped boards
  • The old finish is peeling, flaking, or has been waxed
  • You want a different color, which no recoat can deliver

What the Finish Choice Buys You

Product matters less than traffic and habits, but it isn’t nothing. The commercial two-component waterbornes we run — Bona Traffic HD and Pallmann’s pro systems — cross-link with a hardener and are the same products specified for gym and restaurant floors, which is exactly why we put Traffic HD on residential floors. Against a bargain single-component waterborne, you are buying real additional years in the lanes that wear first, for a difference in material cost that disappears into the total.

Oil-modified polyurethane at roughly 450–550 g/L VOC is durable too, and its old reputation as “the tough one” is out of date — modern two-component waterbornes lead current abrasion testing. What oil actually costs you is schedule and color: one coat a day instead of two or three, a roughly 30-day cure instead of about seven, and a film that keeps ambering for years. Penetrating hardwax oils are a different proposition again — they wear rather than fail, and they’re maintained by spot-refreshing instead of recoating, which suits some households beautifully and annoys others.

Factory-finished floors are the outlier. Their aluminum-oxide coatings are genuinely harder than anything applied on site and can go fifteen or twenty years in light use, but when they do wear, the microbevels between boards make a clean recoat difficult and the whole floor usually has to be sanded flat first — a trade-off we lay out in our comparison of site-finished versus factory-finished floors over the long run.

The Cadence That Makes a Floor Last a Century

Here is the arithmetic that should drive the decision. Say a 1,200 sq ft main floor. A recoat costs $2,388 and takes one day. A full sand and natural refinish costs $4,788 and takes 3–5 days, plus 48–72 hours before furniture returns and about seven days to full cure. Recoat every seven or eight years and you’ll spend less over thirty years than a single owner who ignores the floor and sands it three times — and at the end of those thirty years you still have a floor with all of its remaining sands intact. Skip the recoats and you burn through the finite resource, which is the point we make in our guide to how many times a floor can be refinished.

There’s a middle path worth knowing about, too. If only the hallway and kitchen have gone while the rest of the house is fine, a targeted repair can sometimes buy years without a whole-floor project — the honest limits of that approach are in our piece on spot-fixing worn traffic paths. It works best when the affected area ends at a natural boundary like a doorway, and it does not work at all when the wear fades gradually into a room, because a recoat boundary in open floor is visible forever.

One regional footnote, since most of the floors we maintain are here. Puget Sound is hard on entries — nine months of wet shoes and grit — and easy on everything else, since we don’t get the extreme indoor humidity swings that torture floors in other climates. In practice that means the front six feet of the house often needs attention years before the rest, and the fix is usually a mat and a recoat rather than a project. How Long a Floor Finish Lasts: The Questions We Get

How long does a hardwood floor finish last, in one number?
If you need one number for a family home, call it eight years in the busy parts of the house. Bedrooms and formal rooms will go far longer, entries and kitchen paths noticeably shorter, and habits move the number more than the product does. The right way to use that number is as a reminder to look at the floor around year six, not as an expiration date.
Is a dull floor the same as a worn-out floor?
No, and confusing the two costs people money in both directions. Dullness is usually the top of the film scuffed by grit, which a recoat fully reverses. Worn-out means the film is gone and the wood is exposed, which only sanding fixes. Test with water: if it beads, you are dull, not worn.
Does a more expensive finish actually last longer?
Between a bargain single-component waterborne and a commercial two-component like Bona Traffic HD, yes — meaningfully, in the traffic lanes where it matters. Between two premium products, the difference is smaller than the difference between two households. Grit control, pet nails, and cleaner choice all outrank the label on the can.
Can I just recoat one room instead of the whole floor?
Yes, if the room ends at a doorway or another natural boundary. Recoat lines in the middle of open floor are visible in raking light forever, so we won't put one there. In open-plan houses that often means the practical unit is the whole main floor, which is worth knowing before you budget by the room.
How do I know a recoat will bond to what's already there?
We test before we quote. A small area gets screened and coated, and we check adhesion the next day. Wax, oil soap residue, silicone polishes, and some prefinished coatings all cause failures, and finding that out on a test patch costs nothing while finding it out on a whole floor costs a full sand.
Does refinishing shorten the life of my floor?
A full sand does, slightly and permanently — it removes wood, and a typical solid floor has three to five sands in it total. A recoat does not: it only touches the finish. That's the entire argument for staying on a recoat cycle, and it's why we'd rather sell you a $1.99 service than a $3.99 one when the floor still qualifies.
What about engineered floors — same answer?
Same finish life, different limits underneath. The film wears at the same rate, so the recoat cadence is identical, but an engineered floor has only its wear layer to sand and thin ones may have no full sand available at all. That makes staying ahead of the recoat clock more important on engineered floors, not less.
We're about to refinish. What can we do to make it last?
Mats at every exterior door and shoes off inside; trim the dog's nails; sweep or dry-mop the grit weekly; clean with a pH-neutral hardwood cleaner and nothing else; felt pads under every furniture leg, replaced when they load up with grit; blinds or film on the sunniest exposure; and a recoat booked at the first sign of a lane rather than at the first sign of bare wood.
