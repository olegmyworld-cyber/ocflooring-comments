What the Warranty Actually Covers — and What It Can’t

A workmanship warranty covers things that are the contractor’s fault. If a coat peels because of an adhesion failure, if sanding marks telegraph through the finish under afternoon light, if the film didn’t level — that’s on us, and we come fix it. What no contractor covers, and no finish manufacturer covers either, is damage done to the finish after we leave: moisture forced into the seams by a steam mop, a soft acrylic film laid on top by a “restorer” product, a sheen chemically dulled by months of vinegar. This isn’t fine print looking for an exit. The two kinds of damage look completely different under inspection — a workmanship defect follows the pattern of our work; product damage follows the pattern of a mop. We’ve honored plenty of warranty calls, and the distinction has never been hard to make.

The First Week Is Its Own Rulebook

The waterborne systems we coat with — Bona and Pallmann, two to three coats — are dry enough for socks the same evening, but the film keeps hardening for about seven days. During that window: furniture back at 48–72 hours, lifted into place rather than dragged; rugs wait the full week so the film can finish curing evenly; and cleaning means a dry dust mop only. No liquid touches the floor in week one — not even the good cleaner. The full schedule, hour by hour, is in our guide to walking on refinished floors. Most cure-window damage we see isn’t dramatic; it’s a rug pad texture faintly printed into a finish that was five days old.

The Only Routine Your Floor Needs

After the cure, maintenance is genuinely simple. A dry microfiber mop through the traffic areas every day or two picks up the grit that does the actual damage — dirt underfoot works like sandpaper, and it scratches finish faster than any dog. Once a week or so, a pH-neutral hardwood cleaner: mist it onto the mop pad, not onto the floor, and the floor should be dry within a minute of the pad passing. That’s the entire program. Add felt pads under furniture legs, walk-off mats at exterior doors, and trimmed pet claws, and you’ve done everything our floor-protection checklist asks of anyone. The Blacklist, and What Each Product Actually Does

Steam mops are the worst offender because they feel virtuous — no chemicals, just water. But that water is vapor under pressure, and it drives moisture through the seams between boards and into the finish film itself, clouding it white and swelling the wood below. We wrote a whole post on why steam and hardwood don’t mix; the short version is that no finish manufacturer permits it. Vinegar is an acid, and a diluted acid mopped over a floor weekly dulls the sheen slowly enough that most people blame the finish. Oil soaps leave a residue that smears underfoot, grabs dirt, and builds up with every wash. None of these ruin a floor in a day — that’s what makes them dangerous. They ruin it over a year, and by then the receipt is long gone.

Why “Polish” and “Restorer” Products Are the Expensive Mistake

The acrylic “hardwood polish” bottles promise to bring back the shine, and for a weekend they do — by laying a thin coat of soft plastic over your actual finish. That layer scuffs faster than real finish, goes cloudy in traffic lanes, and here’s the part the label never mentions: nothing bonds to it. The normal maintenance move for a dulling floor is a screen and recoat — abrade the old coat, lay a fresh one, $1.99/sq ft, one day. A floor with acrylic polish on it can’t be recoated until the residue is stripped, because new finish peels off contamination — and if stripping doesn’t get it all, the fix becomes a full $3.99/sq ft resand. A $12 bottle quietly doubles the cost of your floor’s next maintenance visit. If a product’s label says “restores shine,” put it back on the shelf.

Cleaning Refinished Floors: What Redmond Homeowners Ask Us

Will using a steam mop actually void my warranty?
It voids coverage for the damage it causes, yes. Steam damage — clouded finish, swollen seams — is distinct from a workmanship defect, and neither we nor the finish manufacturers cover it. One or two careful passes probably won't destroy a floor, but there is no safe version of pressurized moisture on wood, so we ask clients not to use one at all.
What cleaner do you actually recommend?
A pH-neutral cleaner made specifically for polyurethane-finished hardwood — Bona's spray cleaner is the one we hand out, and Pallmann makes an equivalent. They cost about $10 a bottle and a bottle lasts months. The brand matters less than the chemistry: neutral pH, no oils, no wax, no acrylics.
Can I use a vinegar-and-water mix like my parents did?
We'd rather you didn't. Vinegar is a mild acid, and mopped weekly it slowly etches the sheen off a waterborne finish — the floor reads dull and worn years before it should. The advice dates from the wax-finish era, when floors were maintained completely differently. A pH-neutral cleaner costs almost nothing and doesn't fight your finish.
How soon after refinishing can I mop?
First liquid cleaning at about a week, once the finish is fully cured. Before that, dry dust mopping only. After the first week, damp is fine — the rule is that the floor should be dry within about a minute of the mop passing. Standing water is never okay on hardwood, cured or not.
Are wet Swiffer-style pads safe?
The dry sweeping pads are great. The pre-moistened wet pads are a mixed bag — some formulas leave a residue that builds up and dulls the floor over time. If you like the format, use a dry pad misted with a pH-neutral hardwood cleaner instead. You get the convenience without gambling on the solution.
How do I get scuff marks off without damaging the finish?
Most scuffs are rubber transfer sitting on top of the finish, not damage in it. A microfiber cloth with a little hardwood cleaner takes them off; a clean tennis ball rubbed over the mark works surprisingly well on stubborn ones. If a mark won't budge with either, stop — aggressive scrubbing or magic erasers will dull the spot worse than the scuff.
My floor went dull and sticky after years of an oil soap. Can it be fixed?
Usually, but it takes a professional assessment. If a deep clean strips the residue completely, a $1.99/sq ft screen and recoat restores the wear layer. If the contamination has soaked in too deep for new finish to bond, the honest fix is a full resand at $3.99/sq ft. We test a small area before quoting either way.
Do rugs need special pads on a refinished floor?
Yes — breathable felt or natural rubber pads, not the cheap PVC waffle pads, which can react with finish and imprint a grid pattern into it. And after a refinish, no rugs at all for the first seven days while the finish cures. After that, lift and rotate rugs occasionally so the floor ages evenly.
