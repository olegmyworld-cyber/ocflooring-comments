Why Refinishing Deposits Exist at All

A refinishing company sells calendar time. When we book your floor for the first week of February, we’re turning down other work for those days, and if you cancel the night before, those days mostly can’t be re-sold. A deposit makes that commitment mutual: you have skin in the game, we hold the dates. That’s the legitimate core of every deposit, and it’s why “no deposit ever” isn’t automatically a virtue — a contractor with no committed schedule may also feel free to bump your job when a bigger one appears.

The second legitimate purpose is job-specific materials. A custom stain color mixed for your floor, treads ordered for a stair project, finish bought in quantity for an unusually large job — those are real costs a contractor incurs before day one, and it’s fair for a deposit to cover them. Note what’s not on this list: payroll, the sander, the van, the finish a company keeps in stock anyway. Those are the contractor’s costs of being in business, not your costs of being a customer.

What a Fair Deposit Covers

Run the math on a typical Kirkland job and the healthy range becomes obvious. Say 800 sq ft at our $3.99/sq ft natural-refinish rate — about $3,200. A deposit of 10–30% is $320 to $960: real money, clearly tied to holding a week of calendar and any special materials, and small enough that the contractor still has every financial reason to show up, finish, and pass your walk-through to collect the rest. That incentive structure is the entire point. A company holding 15% of your money works hard for the other 85%; a company holding 60% has already been mostly paid for work it hasn’t done.

Scale matters too. A one-day $1.99/sq ft screen and recoat has almost no pre-job costs — no stain to mix, minimal calendar risk — so a token deposit or none at all is common and reasonable. A three-week whole-house project with custom-stained stairs sits at the other end. If the deposit percentage doesn’t track the contractor’s actual up-front exposure, ask why. And whatever the number, it should be a line against a written quote with per-square-foot rates — the kind of quote transparency we’ve argued for in our breakdown of $2 vs $6 per square foot quotes and our list of hidden costs to watch for.

What a Deposit Should Never Cover — the Red Flags

Half up front “for materials” is the classic tell, because refinishing barely has materials in the deposit sense — finish, abrasives, and filler for an average job are a small fraction of the price, and established companies stock them. A deposit sized way past materials-plus-scheduling is usually funding something else: the last job’s payroll, a cash-flow hole, or in the ugliest cases, a contractor who won’t be back. The pattern repeats often enough in complaint boards and court records across every trade that we treat it as a rule: the deposit percentage rises as the contractor’s stability falls.

The companion red flags travel in a pack. Cash-only pricing — you lose the paper trail that protects you. A deposit requested before you’ve received a written scope — you’re paying for a promise no one has defined. Pressure to “lock in a discount today” — urgency is doing the work the reputation should be doing. And a contractor who can’t show you an active Washington contractor registration and proof of insurance — the deposit is the least of your risks at that point. None of these alone proves bad faith. Two or more together is your cue to keep shopping, and our five questions to ask before refinishing will surface most of them in one phone call.

The Sniff Test, Compressed

A deposit is reasonable when…

  • It’s 10–30% of a written, itemized, per-square-foot quote
  • It’s clearly tied to calendar commitment and job-specific materials
  • The balance is due only after you’ve walked the finished floor
  • Any payment method leaves a paper trail — card, check, invoice
  • The contractor is registered, insured, and happy to prove both

Walk away when…

  • It’s 50% or more, or the full job, before work starts
  • It’s cash-only, or discounted for cash
  • It’s requested before a written scope of work exists
  • The quote is a lump sum with no per-foot rates to check it against
  • Today-only pricing pressure is attached to the deposit

The Paperwork That Makes Any Deposit Safe

The deposit itself matters less than what surrounds it. Before money moves, you should be holding a written scope that says what’s being sanded, what finish system is going down and how many coats, the per-square-foot rate, what’s excluded, and the schedule. In Washington you can verify any contractor’s registration and insurance status through the state Department of Labor & Industries lookup in about a minute — do it, every time, including for us. A company that’s licensed, insured, and publishing its prices has structurally fewer reasons to lean on your deposit as a lifeline.

Then hold your leverage in the right place: the final walk-through. Whatever the deposit was, the balance should change hands only after you’ve seen the floor in daylight and raised anything that looks off. Reputable contractors want that walk-through — it’s where a good job gets signed off and small punch-list items get caught while the crew’s still mobilized. Our 1-year workmanship warranty picks up from there. A payment structure that skips the walk-through, with the balance auto-due on the last coat, quietly deletes your best quality-control moment.

Refinishing Deposits: What Kirkland Homeowners Ask

How much should a hardwood refinishing deposit be?
Around 10 to 30 percent of a written quote is the reasonable range in the Kirkland area. Small one-day jobs like a screen and recoat often need no deposit at all; jobs with custom stain or stair materials sit at the higher end. Anything at or above 50 percent is a red flag regardless of the story attached.
Is it normal for a contractor to ask for half up front?
It happens, but it isn’t healthy. Refinishing has modest true up-front costs — a stable company doesn’t need half your money to start. Oversized deposits usually signal cash-flow problems, and in the worst cases they precede a contractor who never returns.
Should I ever pay for a refinishing job entirely in advance?
No. Full prepayment removes the contractor’s financial incentive to finish and pass your walk-through, and it removes your leverage if the work falls short. No discount is worth that trade.
What should the deposit actually pay for?
Two things: holding your dates on the contractor’s calendar, and materials ordered specifically for your job — custom-mixed stain, stair treads, special-order supplies. General overhead, standard finish stock, and labor belong in the balance, paid when the work is done.
When should the final payment be due?
After the final walk-through — you and the contractor looking at the finished floor in good light, with anything questionable addressed or scheduled. A payment structure that makes the balance due automatically on the last coat skips your best quality-control step.
Is a cash discount a red flag?
Yes. Cash-only or cash-discounted pricing erases the paper trail that protects you if anything goes wrong, and it often signals a business run informally in other ways too — insurance and registration included. Pay in a way that leaves a record.
How do I check a contractor before handing over a deposit?
Verify their Washington contractor registration and insurance through the state Labor & Industries lookup, read recent reviews, and insist on a written scope with per-square-foot rates. Those three steps take fifteen minutes and screen out the majority of deposit horror stories.
Does OC Flooring require a deposit?
It depends on the job — size, materials, and schedule — and whatever we propose goes in writing alongside our published rates ($1.99/sq ft screen and recoat, $3.99 natural refinish, $6.50 with stain) before we ask for anything. We also offer financing, which for many projects replaces the deposit conversation entirely.
I’m refinishing to sell my house — does that change anything?
It raises the stakes on scheduling. Get your dates written into the same document as the deposit, so the money you put down is buying a committed timeline, not just a place in line. And ask about financing — moving the cost past closing often beats stretching for a deposit mid-sale.
