Why Width Changes the Physics

Wood swells and shrinks across the grain as indoor humidity changes — every floor does it, every winter and summer. The movement is proportional to board width, which is the entire story of wide plank in one sentence. A 2¼-inch strip that opens a hairline seasonal gap becomes, at 9 inches, a board that opens a gap you can see from standing height. The same math applies to cupping: when the bottom of a board picks up more moisture than the top, the edges rise — and the wider the board, the more visible the curl. Our post on cupped floors and whether refinishing fixes them covers the diagnosis; the wide-plank version of the rule is simply that everything happens sooner and shows more. This is also why wide solid plank is usually face-fastened or glued in addition to nailed — the forces at each board are genuinely larger.

Flatness Is the Whole Game When Sanding Wide Plank

On a strip floor, a slightly uneven sanding pass hides in the visual noise of narrow boards and many seams. On wide plank there’s nowhere to hide: each board is a broad, continuous surface that catches window light like a tabletop, and any dish, chatter mark, or drum stop-line reads instantly. Sanding a cupped wide plank floor dead flat also removes real thickness — you’re cutting the raised edges down to the level of the centers, and on a badly cupped floor we’ll tell you honestly whether the wood budget is there, because a floor only has so many sandings in it. This is exactly why we run the Lägler TRIO as a final pass on wide plank: it’s a planetary sander that cuts flat by design instead of relying on operator touch, and on a 7-inch board under a west-facing window, that difference is the difference.

Solid vs Engineered Wide Plank: Different Answers

A lot of modern wide plank is engineered — a real hardwood wear layer over a plywood core — and at width, engineered construction is genuinely the more stable choice: the core resists the cross-grain movement that makes wide solid boards restless. The refinishing question, though, comes down to one number: wear layer thickness. A 4mm wear layer takes a careful full sand; a 2mm layer takes a light one, once; a 1mm veneer takes none. We measure at a floor vent before quoting anything, and our engineered hardwood wear layer guide walks through the numbers in detail. The practical Mercer Island version: if your wide plank went in during a 2010s remodel, it’s probably engineered, probably European white oak, and probably refinishable exactly once — so that one refinish is worth doing well.

Gaps, Filler, and Honest Expectations

Here’s a place we’ll talk you out of spending money: trowel-filling the gaps in a wide plank floor. Filler works on strip floors with stable hairline gaps. On wide plank, seasonal movement is strong enough to crack and eject filler within a year or two — the board simply moves more than the filler can flex, and you end up with crumbling lines that look worse than the gaps did. Our approach is to fill genuine defects — knots, checks, board-end chips — and leave seasonal gaps alone, then manage them with humidity instead: keep the house in a sane winter range and the gaps close on their own each spring. A floor that gaps identically every winter and closes every summer isn’t failing; it’s breathing. A gap that grows every year regardless of season is a different conversation, and worth a look from us.

Finish Choices: Why Wide Plank Usually Goes Natural and Matte

People buy wide plank for the wood itself — the long grain, the mineral streaks, the plank-to-plank variation — so the finishes that flatter it are the ones that disappear. On white oak wide plank we do more natural, matte, waterborne finishes than everything else combined: two to three coats of Bona or Pallmann, no stain, no amber shift, at our standard $3.99/sq ft. An oil-modified poly would push that pale European-oak look yellow within a few years, which is precisely what nobody paying for wide plank wants — the full chemistry argument is in our water-based vs oil-based comparison. Stains are possible and sometimes right ($6.50/sq ft), but on wide boards stain also highlights every sanding imperfection, which loops back to the flatness section above: on wide plank, the prep is the finish.

Wide Plank Refinishing: What Mercer Island Homeowners Ask Us

Can wide plank floors be sanded and refinished like normal hardwood?
Yes — solid wide plank sands the same way strip does, and engineered wide plank can usually take one careful sand if the wear layer is 2mm or more. The difference isn't whether it can be done but how much precision it demands: flatness errors that vanish on strip flooring are obvious on wide boards, so machine choice and technique matter more.
Why does my wide plank floor have gaps every winter?
Because wood shrinks across its width as indoor air dries out, and wide boards have more width to shrink. A 7- or 9-inch plank opens a visibly wider winter gap than a narrow strip would — that's geometry, not a defect. If the gaps close each summer, the floor is behaving normally. Gaps that grow year over year regardless of season are worth a professional look.
Should the gaps be filled during refinishing?
Usually not. Filler in wide plank gaps cracks and pops out within a season or two because the boards move more than the filler can tolerate. We fill true defects — knots, checks, chips — and leave seasonal gaps to close on their own. Managing winter humidity does more for wide plank gaps than any filler ever will.
Is engineered wide plank better than solid?
At widths of 7 inches and up, engineered is genuinely more stable — the plywood core resists the cross-grain movement that makes wide solid boards gap and cup. The trade-off is refinishing life: solid can be sanded several times, engineered usually once. Which matters more depends on how long you plan to own the floor.
Will refinishing fix the cupping in my wide plank floor?
Only if the moisture cause is fixed first and the boards have mostly relaxed. Sanding a cupped floor flat while it's still wet locks in the wrong shape — when the boards dry and flatten, the sanded profile turns into crowning. We measure moisture content before sanding any cupped floor and will tell you if it needs a season to settle first.
What finish do you recommend on white oak wide plank?
A natural, matte or satin, waterborne finish — two to three coats of Bona or Pallmann at $3.99/sq ft. It keeps white oak's pale, neutral color instead of yellowing it the way oil-modified poly would, and it holds up to real traffic. Most wide plank is bought for its natural look; the finish's job is to protect that look, not change it.
Does radiant heat under wide plank change the refinishing process?
It changes the operating advice more than the sanding. The refinish itself proceeds normally, but radiant heat dries boards from below, which amplifies seasonal movement in wide planks. We ask radiant-heat clients to ramp temperatures gradually in fall rather than jumping the slab warm, and to watch winter humidity — the floor will thank you in narrower gaps.
How much does refinishing wide plank cost compared to regular flooring?
The rates are the same — $3.99/sq ft for a natural refinish, $6.50/sq ft with stain, $1.99/sq ft for a screen and recoat — with a 500 sq ft minimum. Wide plank doesn't carry a surcharge; it carries a precision requirement. The place the numbers can differ is repairs, since replacement wide boards cost more per piece and sometimes need sourcing.
