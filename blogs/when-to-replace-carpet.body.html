The Five Signs, and Why These Five

Carpet rarely fails all at once; it fades out of usefulness while everyone in the house slowly stops noticing. The five signs below are the ones that mean the carpet itself is finished — as opposed to dirty, loose or unlucky, which are cheaper problems wearing a convincing costume. Matting vs Dirt: The Test That Saves Floors

The most common carpet misdiagnosis is calling a dirty carpet dead. Traffic lanes collect ground-in soil that dulls the color and flattens the look, and a carpet with years of it can appear utterly finished while being halfway through its life. So before you price replacement, run the two tests we run. First, the fluff test: rub a matted patch briskly with your fingers or drag a coin across it against the pile. If the fibers stand back up and hold, the twist is alive — that is dirt, and a professional hot-water extraction will shock you with how much floor comes back. If the fibers stay flat, or stand up and slump within the hour, the twist is gone and no cleaning will resurrect it. Second, the closet comparison: look at the same carpet inside a closet, where feet never go. If the closet carpet looks great and the lanes look terrible, the gap between them tells you whether the problem is wear or washing. A deep clean costs a small fraction of a new floor — when it can work, it should always go first, and we say so even though we sell the floor.

When the Smell Is Not in the Carpet

Odor is the sign people fight the longest, because it responds to cleaning — briefly. A carpet that smells fine for a week after extraction and then blooms again on the first humid day is telling you the source is underneath: urine, spills or moisture that soaked through years ago and lives in the pad, sometimes in the subfloor itself. Surface cleaning cannot reach it, ozone treatments and enzyme sprays only negotiate with it, and the honest fix is mechanical: old carpet and pad out, the subfloor inspected and sealed where it needs it, new pad and carpet in. This is also the moment to fix the cause — a pet-accident zone gets a moisture-barrier pad this time, and a musty room gets its moisture question answered before any new carpet goes down. If we find a damp slab or an active leak at the estimate, the carpet conversation pauses until the water conversation is done. New carpet over an unsolved problem is a purchase with an expiration date.

Now the good news list. Ripples, waves and wrinkles are not a worn-out carpet — they are a loose one. Carpet that was knee-kicked instead of power-stretched at installation, or that has relaxed over years of sun and traffic, lifts into waves that look alarming and fix completely. A restretch pulls the carpet taut, trims and re-secures it, and the floor is flat again by lunch — at a fraction of the cost of replacement, which is why is restretching worth it is usually a short read with a happy answer, and what stretching costs puts numbers on it. If wrinkles return months after a proper restretch, the pad has collapsed, and that is sign four wearing sign-of-nothing's clothing. Replacing Carpet: What Homeowners Ask Us

How do I know when carpet needs to be replaced?
Five signs: matting that will not fluff back up after cleaning, odor that returns after professional extraction, visible backing or failing seams, a pad that has gone crunchy or flat underfoot, and age — around fifteen-plus years — where deep cleans no longer hold. If your complaint is not on that list, there is a decent chance the fix is cheaper than a new floor.
How long does carpet usually last?
Quality carpet over a good pad runs ten to fifteen years in normal family use; builder-grade over cheap pad can be visibly done in five to seven. The room matters as much as the product — bedroom carpet lives far longer than family-room carpet — and the pad quietly decides more of the lifespan than the carpet label does.
My carpet looks flat and dirty in the traffic lanes. Is it worn out?
Run the fluff test: rub the matted area briskly against the pile. Fibers that stand back up and stay up are dirty, not dead — book a hot-water extraction and be pleasantly surprised. Fibers that stay flat or slump back within the hour have lost their twist, and no cleaning brings that back. That is the difference between a cleaning bill and a new floor.
Why does my carpet still smell after professional cleaning?
Because the smell is not in the carpet. Odor that survives extraction lives in the pad or subfloor, where surface cleaning cannot reach — classic with old pet accidents and moisture. The fix is replacement: old carpet and pad out, subfloor inspected and sealed if needed, and a moisture-barrier pad under the new carpet so the story does not repeat.
Should I restretch my carpet or replace it?
If the problem is ripples, waves or a wrinkle at the doorway and the carpet itself is healthy, restretch — it costs a fraction of replacement and the floor is flat by lunch. Replace when the fibers are matted flat, the backing shows, or wrinkles return after a proper restretch, which means the pad underneath has collapsed. We quote whichever one your floor actually needs.
Is it worth replacing carpet before selling a house?
Usually, yes — old carpet photographs badly and invites lowball mental math from every buyer who walks in, while fresh neutral carpet in bedrooms is one of the cheapest per-dollar upgrades in a listing.
