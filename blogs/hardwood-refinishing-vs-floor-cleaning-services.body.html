What a Floor Cleaning Service Actually Does

A professional hardwood cleaning crew brings machine scrubbers, specialty cleaners, and sometimes a buffer, and removes what years of mopping never quite gets: bonded grime in the finish texture, cleaner residue, kitchen film, the gray cast that makes a floor look older than it is. On a floor whose finish is intact underneath, the results can genuinely surprise you — that’s the honest case for the service, and it’s real.

What it can’t do is add anything. Cleaning is subtraction: dirt off the top of a plastic wear layer. The finish underneath is exactly as thin, scratched, and worn as it was before the crew arrived — just cleaner. Which is why the same service that transforms one floor does nothing for its neighbor: the difference was never the cleaning, it was what was left to uncover. (One caution while we’re here: skip steam cleaners entirely — steam forces moisture into wood and finish seams and does real damage. Our maintenance guide covers what routine care should look like instead.)

What Cleaning Can’t Fix — and Why the Water Drop Test Settles It

Once the finish itself is gone or failing, cleaning is washing bare wood. The telltales: boards going gray or dark in the traffic paths (that’s exposed wood absorbing moisture and grime), scratches that catch a fingernail (through the finish, into wood), whitening around the dishwasher and sink, color faded in front of the sliding door — sun damage is its own beast — and finish visibly flaking or peeling anywhere. Cleaning makes these floors briefly shinier and slightly sadder, because now the damage is clean and well-lit.

The sixty-second diagnostic anyone can run: put a single drop of water on the floor’s worst traffic area. If it beads and sits, finish is present — the floor’s problems are cosmetic-and-dirt problems, and cleaning or recoating are your lanes. If it soaks in and darkens the wood within a couple of minutes, the finish is gone there, and you’re in refinishing territory no matter what any brochure says. Run it in a corner too; the difference between the two spots is your floor telling you exactly how much finish the traffic has eaten.

The “Revitalizer” Trap

Between cleaning and recoating sits a product category the mop aisle loves: polishes, restorers, revitalizers — liquids that promise a “like new” shine you mop on yourself, and that some cleaning services apply as a finishing flourish. Here’s what they are: acrylic coatings that sit on top of your finish. Here’s what they do over time: build up in cloudy layers, hold dirt, go sticky in humidity, and streak. And here’s the expensive part — the residue interferes with adhesion, so when the floor eventually needs a real recoat, the finish won’t bond over it. The buildup has to be chemically stripped or the whole floor sanded, turning what could have been a $1.99/sq ft recoat into a full $3.99/sq ft refinish.

We see the aftermath regularly: a floor “maintained” with polish for five years costs more to restore than a floor that was simply ignored. If a cleaning quote includes a polish step, ask exactly what product goes down — and if the answer is acrylic, decline it. A well-cleaned floor with intact finish doesn’t need fake shine on top.

The Middle Rung Nobody Sells You: Screen and Recoat

The gap between a $300 cleaning and a $3,000 refinish is exactly where most tired-but-not-dead floors live, and the service that fills it is a screen and recoat: abrade the existing finish so a new coat can bond, then lay down fresh commercial waterborne. It erases finish-level scratches and dullness, adds genuine new protection — the thing cleaning can’t do — and resets the wear clock for a fraction of a refinish. One day of work, socks that evening, $1.99/sq ft.

The catch is the qualification: a recoat needs intact finish to bond to and a floor free of that acrylic contamination. Bare-wood traffic paths, deep scratches, and gray boards disqualify it — new finish over exposed wood looks blotchy and wears unevenly, which is why we check with the water test before quoting one. Cleaning services don’t offer recoats (they don’t carry finish), and some refinishers don’t bother mentioning them (the ticket is smaller). We lead with it whenever a floor qualifies, because a floor recoated on time can skip its next full sanding entirely.

The Line, Drawn Plainly

Cleaning is enough when…

  • Water beads on every part of the floor, traffic paths included
  • The complaint is grime, film, or residue — not scratches
  • The color under the dirt is even, with no gray boards
  • The floor was refinished or recoated in recent memory
  • You’re maintaining, not rescuing

It’s past cleaning when…

  • Water soaks in and darkens the wood anywhere
  • Traffic paths are gray, bare, or rough underfoot
  • Scratches catch your fingernail — they’re in the wood
  • The finish is peeling, flaking, or cloudy from polish buildup
  • You want the color or sheen changed — that’s finish work by definition

Refinishing vs Cleaning: What Everett Homeowners Ask

Can a cleaning service make my hardwood look new again?
Only if the finish underneath the dirt is intact. Cleaning removes grime from the wear layer; it can't repair, thicken, or replace that layer. Run the water drop test: if water beads on your worst traffic path, cleaning can help. If it soaks in and darkens the wood, the finish is gone and cleaning is washing bare wood.
What's the difference between buffing, recoating, and refinishing?
Buffing or polishing shines what's there. A screen and recoat abrades the existing finish and adds one new coat of real polyurethane — $1.99/sq ft with us, one day. Refinishing sands to bare wood and rebuilds the entire finish system — $3.99/sq ft natural, $6.50 with stain, three to five days. Each rung fixes what the one below can't.
Is professional floor cleaning worth the money?
On the right floor, yes — machine scrubbing removes bonded grime that home mopping never gets, and an intact-finish floor can look dramatically better. On the wrong floor it's wasted: no cleaning fixes gray boards, bare paths, or deep scratches. The water drop test tells you which floor you have before you spend.
Should I let a cleaning service apply polish or restorer?
We'd decline it. Those products are acrylic coatings that build up cloudy, streaky, and sometimes sticky — and they block adhesion, so a future recoat won't bond until the buildup is stripped or sanded off. They routinely turn a would-be $1.99/sq ft recoat into a full refinish. Clean finish doesn't need artificial shine.
My floor is sticky after a cleaning. What happened?
Usually residue — from too much cleaning product, the wrong product, or a polish applied on top. Try a proper rinse-mop with a hardwood-specific cleaner first. If stickiness persists, there's likely acrylic buildup involved, and we can assess whether it strips off or needs abrasion at an estimate.
How much cheaper is cleaning than refinishing?
Meaningfully: professional cleaning typically runs a few hundred dollars, a screen and recoat at $1.99/sq ft lands around $1,600 on an 800 sq ft floor, and a full natural refinish at $3.99 about $3,200. Which is exactly why paying for cleaning when the floor needs refinishing is the worst outcome — you pay the small bill and still owe the big one.
Do you offer floor cleaning?
No — we're a refinishing company: recoats, full sands, stains, and repairs. Which is partly why our diagnosis is useful: when a floor just needs cleaning, we have nothing to sell you, and we'll say so at the free estimate. When it needs more, we'll show you why on the floor itself.
How often should hardwood be professionally cleaned or recoated?
Routine cleaning at home — dry dusting plus an occasional damp mop with hardwood cleaner — is most of the battle. A deep clean makes sense when film builds up despite good habits. The recoat rhythm matters more: a fresh coat every three to seven years, depending on traffic, keeps the wear layer ahead of the wear and can postpone full sanding almost indefinitely.
