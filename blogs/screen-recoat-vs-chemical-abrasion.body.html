Quick answer

A screen and recoat preps your old finish mechanically — a buffer and abrasive screen scuff the surface so a fresh coat bonds — while chemical abrasion systems do the same job with a deep clean and a bonding agent instead of scratches. For most site-finished floors, screening is simpler, cheaper, and proven; chemical systems earn their keep on factory-finished floors whose aluminum-oxide coatings are too hard for a screen to bite. Both refresh the finish only — neither fixes scratches through the color, gray wood, or dents. Here’s how we decide between them.

You can rough it up physically, which is the classic screen and recoat. What Screening Actually Does

A screen and recoat starts with a heavy floor buffer driving an abrasive mesh screen or conditioning pad across every foot of the floor. The screen doesn’t touch wood — it cuts thousands of fine, uniform scratches into the top of the existing finish. Under a raking light the floor goes evenly dull, which is exactly what you want: dull means scratched, and scratched means the next coat of finish has something to key into mechanically. Vacuum, tack, coat, done. It’s the same logic as sanding glossy trim before repainting it, scaled up to seven hundred square feet.

The method’s virtues are boring and real. It’s visible — you can see whether the old finish dulled evenly, so you know before coating whether the bond will hold. It removes a whisker of the old surface, taking light scuffing and cloudiness with it. And it’s cheap, because the consumable is an abrasive screen, not a proprietary chemistry kit. The limits are equally real: a screen can’t reach into the beveled edges between boards on prefinished floors, and on the hardest factory coatings it skates instead of cutting — more on both below.

What a Chemical Abrasion System Does Instead

A chemical adhesion system replaces the screen with chemistry. The floor gets an aggressive deep clean with the system’s prep solution — this step is not optional and not quick — followed by a bonding agent that’s applied like a thin coat of finish and left to set. That bonding layer is formulated to grip the old finish on one side and the new finish on the other, so the topcoat that follows has something to hold without a single scratch being cut. “Chemical abrasion” is honestly a marketing name; nothing is abraded. It’s a primer, and thinking of it that way keeps expectations realistic.

The honest appeal: no screening dust, no risk of a screen cutting through thin finish on edges, and — the big one — it works on coatings a screen can’t scratch. The honest cost: you’re trusting chemistry you can’t see. A screened floor shows you its prep; a chemically prepped floor looks shiny and clean right up until the topcoat either bonds or doesn’t. The systems work when the instructions are followed exactly — the cleaning step especially — and fail memorably when they aren’t.

Where Chemical Systems Genuinely Earn Their Keep

Factory-finished floors are the reason these systems exist. Prefinished hardwood ships with aluminum-oxide-infused coatings that are dramatically harder than anything applied in a house — great for wear, miserable for recoating, because an abrasive screen slides across the surface without cutting the uniform scratch pattern a bond needs. Screen a floor like that, coat it, and you can end up peeling finish off in sheets a few months later. We’ve written before about refinishing prefinished floors; the short version is that their bevels and coatings complicate everything, and recoating is no exception.

Those beveled edges are the second half of the argument. A buffer screen is flat; a prefinished floor isn’t. Every V-groove between boards goes untouched by the screen, which means every groove is a line where the new coat is bonded to nothing. A chemical system coats into the bevels the same as everywhere else. So our rule of thumb is simple: site-finished floor, we screen it. Factory-finished floor that a homeowner wants recoated rather than fully resanded, a chemical adhesion system — used precisely to the letter of its instructions — is the right tool, and a contractor who refuses to ever use one is limiting your options.

Where Both Fail the Same Way: Contamination

Here’s what the marketing for both methods underplays: the most common recoat failure has nothing to do with screens versus chemistry. It’s contamination. Years of grocery-store floor polish, oil soaps, wax, or the silicone in furniture spray build an invisible film that neither a screen nor a bonding agent can reliably defeat. Screening smears it around; chemical cleaners remove some products and not others. New finish applied over that film beads, crawls, or — worst case — looks perfect for a few weeks and then peels wherever a sock rubs it.

That’s why the step that actually predicts success is the adhesion test. Before we commit to any recoat, we prep and coat a small patch in a closet or behind a door, let it dry, and try to scrape and peel it. If it grips, the floor is a recoat candidate. If it lifts, no prep method is going to save it, and the honest answer becomes a full resand — which cuts below the contamination entirely. A contractor who quotes a recoat over the phone without asking what’s been used on your floor is skipping the only step that matters. It’s the same reason we’re skeptical of “sandless refinishing” as a cure-all — the chemistry is legitimate; the promises made for it often aren’t.

What Neither Method Fixes

A recoat — screened or chemical — replaces the sacrificial wear layer on top of your floor. That’s all. Scratches that have gone through the finish into stained or bare wood keep their color and simply get sealed under the new coat. Gray, oxidized boards stay gray. Dents stay dented. UV fading stays faded. If you kneel down and the damage you’re bothered by has depth or color to it, you’re shopping for the wrong service, and our guide to deep versus surface scratches will sort you out faster than any prep-method debate.

The flip side: catch a floor while its damage is still confined to the finish, and a recoat is the best money in flooring — it resets the wear clock for a fraction of a refinish, indefinitely, every few years. Wait too long and you’ve bought the full sand anyway, plus years of living with a worn floor. We’ve made the recoat-timing argument at length; the one-line version is that recoats reward the punctual.

The Decision, Compressed

We screen when…

  • The floor was sanded and finished on site — ordinary poly, no factory coating
  • The surface has light scuffing or cloudiness a screen can cut away
  • Board edges are square, so the screen reaches the whole surface
  • The adhesion test patch grips after mechanical prep
  • You want prep you can verify by eye before the coat goes down

Chemistry earns it when…

  • The floor is factory-finished with an aluminum-oxide coating a screen skates over
  • Beveled edges leave grooves a flat screen can’t touch
  • A test screening won’t dull the surface evenly no matter the grit
  • Dust is a hard constraint even at screening’s low levels
  • The system’s full cleaning protocol will actually be followed — no shortcuts

Screen and Recoat vs Chemical Systems: What Homeowners Ask

What is a screen and recoat, in plain terms?
A buffer fitted with an abrasive screen scuffs the top of your existing finish with thousands of fine scratches, the dust gets vacuumed and tacked off, and a fresh coat of finish goes over it. The scratches are what the new coat bonds to. It takes about a day and costs $1.99 per square foot with our waterborne systems.
What is a chemical abrasion or chemical adhesion system?
It’s a two-part product — an aggressive prep cleaner followed by a bonding agent — that lets new finish stick to old finish without any sanding or screening. Basic Coatings’ TyKote is the best-known example. Despite the name, nothing is abraded; it works like a primer between the old coat and the new one.
Which one is better?
For site-finished floors, screening: it’s cheaper, the prep is visible, and it lightly cuts away surface wear at the same time. For factory-finished floors with aluminum-oxide coatings and beveled edges, chemical systems are genuinely better, because a screen can’t scratch the coating evenly or reach into the bevels.
Why can’t you just screen a prefinished floor?
Two reasons. Aluminum-oxide factory coatings are hard enough that a screen slides over them without cutting a proper scratch pattern, so the new coat has little to grip. And the beveled grooves between boards sit below the flat screen, so they get no prep at all — every groove becomes a future peel line.
Will either method remove scratches from my floor?
Only marks that live in the finish itself. Scratches that reach the wood, dents, gray oxidized boards, and sun fading all remain — a recoat seals them under new finish rather than removing them. Damage with depth or color change needs a full sand and refinish.
What makes a recoat fail and peel?
Contamination, overwhelmingly. Polish, wax, oil soaps, and silicone furniture sprays leave films that neither screens nor bonding agents reliably overcome, and the new coat peels wherever the film blocked the bond. This is why we do a small adhesion test patch before committing to any recoat, whatever the prep method.
How long does a recoat take, and when is the floor usable?
One day on site for most homes. With the waterborne finishes we use you can walk in socks the same evening, put furniture back at 48 to 72 hours, and lay rugs after about 7 days when the finish reaches full cure.
How often should a floor be recoated?
When the finish looks dull in traffic lanes but no wear spots have broken through to wood — for busy households that’s roughly every 3 to 7 years. Recoat on that schedule and the floor may never need a full resand; wait until wood shows and the recoat window has closed.
Do you use chemical systems yourselves?
When the floor calls for it, yes. Our default is mechanical screening because most floors we recoat were finished on site, but for sound factory-finished floors a properly executed chemical adhesion system is the right tool, and we’d rather use it than screen a floor we know the screen can’t prep.
