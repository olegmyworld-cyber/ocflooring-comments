“How many coats do you do?” is one of the most common questions we get at estimates, and it’s a good instinct — more coats sounds like more protection, and homeowners have learned to use it to compare bids. The trouble is that it’s the easiest number in the trade to inflate. Coats aren’t standardized, product grades vary enormously, and a thin, badly applied coat counts on paper exactly the same as a proper one.

What a “Coat” Actually Is

A coat is one application of liquid finish, spread at a rate the manufacturer specifies — typically somewhere around 350 to 500 square feet per gallon — which then dries down to a film a fraction of a millimeter thick. Stack the coats and you get total dry film build, and film build is the thing that actually resists your dog’s nails. The number of coats is just a proxy for it, and a sloppy proxy: a coat spread too thin covers less than half as much film as a properly loaded one, and a coat spread too thick doesn’t cure right and can cloud or stay soft.

Not all coats do the same job, either. The first coat down is usually a sealer. It soaks partly into the wood, locks the surface, evens out how absorbent different boards are, and raises the grain slightly so it can be abraded flat before the topcoats go on. Sealers are formulated to bond to wood; topcoats are formulated to be tough. Using a topcoat as the first coat works with some systems and not others, which is one of the many reasons we stay inside a single manufacturer’s system rather than mixing products.

The other invisible ingredient is abrasion between coats. Waterborne finishes bond to the coat below mechanically as much as chemically, so a light screening between coats is what keeps the layers from separating years later. Skipping it saves an hour and is a leading cause of the peeling and flaking we get called out to diagnose. A crew that promises four coats and skips abrasion has built a worse floor than a crew that did three properly.

Why Three Is the Number

Three coats — sealer plus two topcoats — is the residential standard for good reasons that are practical rather than magical. The sealer handles adhesion and absorbency. The first topcoat builds the bulk of the protective film. The second topcoat covers any thin spots in the first, evens the sheen, and gives you a wear surface that can lose some thickness over the years without exposing wood.

That last point is the whole argument. A finish is sacrificial: shoes and grit take microns off the top every year. The extra topcoat is not there to be tougher on day one; it’s there so that in year eight there’s still film between feet and wood, which is what keeps you eligible for a $1.99/sq ft recoat instead of a $3.99/sq ft full sand. We make that case in more detail in is recoating worth it, and it’s the strongest reason not to save money by dropping to two.

Where two coats is genuinely fine: a guest bedroom, a formal dining room, a room that will see socks and a rug and not much else. Where we’d push for a fourth: a commercial floor, a gym, a restaurant, or occasionally the entry-and-hall run of a large family home where everything funnels through six feet of oak. Commercial work is a different animal entirely — see gymnasium floor refinishing for what an actual high-build system looks like.

Why the Product Matters More Than the Count

Here’s the part that makes coat-counting a bad way to compare bids. Finishes are not interchangeable. A two-component commercial waterborne — Bona Traffic HD, Pallmann’s pro systems — cross-links with a hardener and produces a film specified for restaurant and gymnasium floors. A bargain single-component waterborne from a big-box store costs a third as much per gallon and wears accordingly. Two coats of the former outperforms four coats of the latter, comfortably.

Which means the useful question at an estimate isn’t “how many coats” but “which product, and how many coats of it.” If a quote says four coats and won’t name the finish, you’re being sold a number rather than a floor. This is one of the concrete differences hiding inside a suspiciously low bid, and we break the rest of them down in what separates a $2 quote from a $6 quote. How to Tell If Your Floor Got Enough

Signs the floor got enough finish

  • Even sheen across the whole room, including near walls
  • No dull, thirsty-looking patches where the grain drank the coat
  • Pores on oak look filled and smooth rather than open and dry
  • The contractor can tell you the product name and the coat count
  • Coats were abraded between — ask; a good crew says yes immediately

Signs it didn’t

  • Dull patches that show up as the floor dries, especially on oak
  • Grain that still feels rough underfoot after the final coat
  • Visible roller or applicator lines in raking light
  • Wear showing in traffic lanes within two or three years
  • A quote that lists a coat count but names no product

When More Coats Actively Hurt

More is not always better, and this is the part contractors rarely volunteer. Piling on coats — especially thick ones, or coats applied before the previous one has properly dried — traps solvent and moisture in the film. The result can be a finish that stays soft, prints under furniture legs, or dries with a milky haze. Waterborne finishes have a maximum recommended build for a reason.

Excess film also changes the look. Each coat adds a little depth and a little sheen; a floor with four coats of semi-gloss reads noticeably plastic-ier than the same floor with three, which is worth knowing if you chose your sheen off a sample. Our sheen guide covers how that decision interacts with everything else. And on very old, thin, or previously over-sanded floors, more finish doesn’t compensate for less wood — that’s a structural question, not a coating one.

The practical rule: follow the manufacturer’s system. They tested it. A crew improvising extra coats is not being generous, they’re being untested.

Stain Doesn’t Count as a Coat

Worth stating plainly, because it comes up in nearly every comparison of two quotes: stain is color, not protection. It soaks into the top fraction of the wood and adds no meaningful film. A stained floor still needs its full sealer-plus-topcoats system on top — that’s why our stain refinish is $6.50/sq ft against $3.99/sq ft natural, and why a stained job runs a day longer.

Two related details. First, some sealers don’t play well over certain stains, and the manufacturer’s compatibility chart is not optional reading — a mismatch shows up as adhesion failure months later. Second, on a stained floor the abrasion between the first and second topcoats has to be gentle, because cutting through into the color is a repair that means sanding the room again. This is one of the places where experience is the actual product you’re buying, and it’s the same reason refinishing isn’t a good DIY project.

What the Coat Schedule Means for Your Week

Coat count drives your calendar more than it drives your budget. With waterborne, coats go 2–3 hours apart, so a three-coat system finishes in a day and a whole refinish lands in the usual 3–5 days including sanding and dry time — the full sequence is in our refinishing timeline. With oil-modified polyurethane it’s one coat per day, and in a Puget Sound winter that “day” can stretch when the air is cool and damp, which is a real scheduling factor for hardwood floor refinishing in Seattle and anywhere else around here between October and May.

After the last coat, the waiting is about cure, not coats: socks the same evening, furniture at 48–72 hours, area rugs at seven days, full cure around a week with our waterborne systems. Adding a fourth coat adds a few hours to the job, not days — the reason we don’t do it by default is film-build limits and cost, not schedule. Coats of Finish: What Homeowners Ask Us

How many coats of polyurethane does a hardwood floor need?
Three is the residential standard: a sealer coat plus two topcoats. Two is acceptable in genuinely low-traffic rooms, and four is worth it in commercial spaces or an entry-and-hall run that takes the whole household's traffic. Beyond four you're past the manufacturer's recommended film build and buying nothing.
Is four coats better than three?
Only in high-traffic situations, and only if each coat is applied properly with abrasion in between. Past a certain film thickness the extra finish doesn't add durability, can trap moisture and cure soft, and makes the floor look glassier than the sheen you picked. Product quality is a much bigger lever than the fourth coat.
Does the first coat count as a coat?
It counts, but it does a different job. The sealer soaks into the wood, evens out absorbency between boards, and gives the topcoats something consistent to bond to. Some contractors count it, some don't, which is exactly why comparing coat counts between quotes is unreliable unless you also ask what product and what sequence.
How long between coats?
Two to three hours with a waterborne finish, which is why a full system can go down in a single day. Oil-modified polyurethane needs 8 to 24 hours between coats, so the same three-coat job takes most of a week and the floor is unusable throughout. Cool, damp weather stretches both, oil far more than waterborne.
Do you sand between coats?
We abrade — a light screening, not a sanding — between coats, and it matters more than most homeowners realize. Waterborne finishes bond to the layer below mechanically as well as chemically, and skipping the abrasion is a leading cause of finish peeling years later. Ask any contractor whether they do it; the answer is informative.
Does a stained floor need more coats?
It needs the same number of protective coats, because stain adds color and essentially no film. That's why a stain refinish is $6.50 per square foot against $3.99 natural — the stain step is additional work and an extra day, not a substitute for a coat. It also demands a compatible sealer and a gentler abrasion between coats.
Can I add another coat later?
Yes, and that's exactly what a screen and recoat is: we abrade the existing finish and lay one fresh coat, for $1.99 per square foot. It only works while the old finish is intact — once the traffic lanes have worn through to bare wood, there's nothing to bond to and you're into a full sand. Timing is the whole game.
What do you actually put on a typical house?
Two to three coats of a Bona or Pallmann waterborne system, abraded between, with the product named on the estimate. On a high-traffic entry or a commercial space we'll spec more; in a quiet bedroom we'll tell you two is enough rather than sell you a third. The choice is on the estimate in writing either way.
