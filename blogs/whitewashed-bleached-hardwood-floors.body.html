Quick answer

“Whitewashing” and “bleaching” are two different jobs that get sold as one look. Whitewashing adds white pigment — a white stain or tinted sealer — that sits in the grain and lets the wood’s own color show through. Bleaching removes color chemically with a two-part peroxide before any pigment goes on, and it’s what you need when the wood underneath is too pink, too yellow, or too red to whiten any other way. On our price list a pigmented white refinish runs the stained rate of $6.50/sq ft; bleaching adds a day or two of chemistry, drying, and a second light sanding, and gets quoted per project. Both only stay white under a non-yellowing waterborne finish — put oil-based poly over a white floor and you’ve bought a cream floor.

Three Processes, One Word

Pigmented white stain (often called pickling). A white or off-white stain goes on the sanded floor, gets wiped back, and settles into the open grain — heaviest in the pores, lightest on the flat of the board, which is exactly the look people picture. The wood’s own color still shows through, so the result is wood-toned-but-pale, not paint. This is the most common version and the one that lands at our $6.50/sq ft stained refinish rate.

White-tinted sealer or a whitewash coat under clear finish. Instead of pigmenting the wood, we pigment the first coat. The effect is a bit more uniform and slightly more veiled — it can look almost misty on white oak — and it’s more forgiving on species that blotch, because the pigment is sitting in a film rather than being absorbed unevenly by the wood. It also means the color lives in the finish layer, which changes how repairs work later.

Two-part wood bleach. Not a color at all: an A/B peroxide system that strips pigment out of the wood, then gets neutralized, dried, and lightly re-sanded because the water raises the grain. It doesn’t make the floor white on its own — it makes the floor neutral, so that a whitewash or a pale stain lands where you wanted instead of drifting pink or orange. On red oak it’s often the difference between the sample you approved and the floor you actually get. It’s also the slowest and least forgiving step in the whole process, and it’s where a rushed crew makes streaks that no amount of finish will hide.

Which Floors Take White, and Which Fight It

Species decides most of this before anyone opens a can. White oak is the friendly one — open grain that holds pigment, a naturally neutral-to-brown tone, and no red to argue with. It’s the wood in essentially every whitewashed floor you’ve saved to a moodboard. Red oak can be done and is done constantly around here, because it’s what’s under half the houses on the Eastside, but its pink undertone reads through white as a faint rose blush; bleaching first is usually what separates “pale and gray-brown” from “pale and pink.” If you want the full comparison of the two woods, we wrote red oak vs white oak some time ago and it holds up.

Maple at 1450 Janka is dense and closed-grained, so pigment has almost nowhere to sit — white stain tends to blotch and look patchy, and a tinted sealer is the safer route. Douglas fir, at 660 Janka, is soft, resinous, and drinks stain unevenly; it can be whitened, and on the right 1920s house it looks wonderful, but expect variation between boards and expect us to insist on sample areas. Hickory at 1820 has such dramatic natural color range between heartwood and sapwood that white pigment exaggerates the contrast rather than calming it — we get into that in refinishing hickory. And Brazilian cherry (jatoba) at 2350 Janka is the one we’ll talk you out of: it’s deeply red, it photo-darkens with light exposure, and it will keep pushing back toward red no matter what you put on it.

The Finish Decision Is Not Optional Here

On a natural or mid-brown floor, the water-based vs oil-based argument has two legitimate sides. On a white floor it has one. Oil-modified polyurethane ambers on day one and keeps ambering for years, which turns white to cream, cream to butter, and a cool gray-white to a slightly nauseating green-tinted beige. Every pale floor we do gets a non-yellowing waterborne system — Bona or Pallmann, sealer plus two to three coats — for exactly this reason, and we’d refuse the job before we’d put oil over a whitewash. The longer version of the argument is in water-based vs oil-based finish.

Sheen matters more than usual too. Gloss on a pale floor reads plasticky and shows every footprint; matte and satin both suit the look, with matte leaning chalky and Scandinavian and satin a little more forgiving of dust. Our sheen breakdown is in matte, satin, or semi-gloss. One quiet advantage of waterborne here: because it never ambers, the sheen and color you sign off on in the sample is the sheen and color in year ten.

Living With a White Floor: The Honest Part

Pale floors hide dust and pet hair remarkably well — better than any dark floor, which is the single most under-appreciated argument for them in a house with a golden retriever. They also make north-facing Northwest rooms feel lit in February in a way we genuinely notice on site. That’s the good side, and it’s real.

The trade-off is repairs. On a natural floor, a scratch exposes wood that looks like the rest of the wood. On a whitewashed floor, a scratch that goes through the pigment exposes the raw color underneath — on red oak, a pink line across a white floor. Spot-fixing a pigmented floor is genuinely hard because you’re matching a color, not just a sheen, which is the same reason refinished rooms can read differently from each other and the reason correcting uneven color from a previous refinish usually means redoing the whole floor rather than a patch. A screen & recoat still works fine on a pale floor — it refreshes the clear coats and doesn’t touch the color — but any damage that reaches the wood means recolor, not touch-up. Plan on felt pads, entry mats, and keeping the dog’s nails short, same as any floor, just with the knowledge that mistakes are more visible.

The other honest note is subjectivity. Pale is a strong style position. It looks current now; so did dark espresso floors in 2010, and we sanded a lot of those back out. If resale in three years is the driver rather than how you want to live, natural with no stain is the safer bet and it’s what most of our clients land on — we said so in that article and we’ll say it here.

Whitewash is a good fit when…

  • The floor is white oak, or red oak you’re willing to bleach first
  • You want a north-facing Northwest room to read brighter year-round
  • Dust and pet hair are a daily annoyance and dark floors are out
  • You’re staying in the house long enough to enjoy a style choice
  • You’re fine with a non-yellowing waterborne finish — the only option here

Reconsider when…

  • The floor is Brazilian cherry, or hickory you want to look calm
  • You’re refinishing purely to sell and want the broadest appeal
  • Big dogs, big furniture, and a low tolerance for visible scratches
  • You want a floor you can spot-repair board by board later

Whitewashed and Bleached Floors: What Homeowners Ask

What's the difference between whitewashing and bleaching?
Whitewashing adds white pigment to the wood, usually as a stain wiped into the grain or as a tinted sealer. Bleaching removes the wood's own color with a two-part peroxide system, which is a chemical process, not a color. Many pale floors use both: bleach first to neutralize the wood, then pigment to set the final tone.
Can you whitewash red oak?
Yes, and we do it often because red oak is what most Eastside homes have. The catch is that red oak's pink undertone reads through white pigment as a faint rose blush. If you want a truly neutral or gray-white result, the floor needs bleaching first, which adds a day or two and additional cost.
Will a white floor turn yellow over time?
Only if it's finished with the wrong product. Oil-modified polyurethane ambers immediately and keeps yellowing for years, which ruins any pale color. A non-yellowing waterborne system like Bona or Pallmann stays clear, so the floor holds the color you approved. This is the one situation where the finish choice isn't a matter of taste.
How much does a whitewashed floor cost?
A pigmented white refinish is priced at our stained rate of $6.50 per square foot, against $3.99 for a natural refinish, with a 500 square foot minimum. Bleaching is quoted separately because it adds chemical materials, a drying day, and a second light sanding pass to knock down raised grain.
Do whitewashed floors show scratches more?
Differently rather than more. A scratch that only reaches the finish looks the same as on any floor. A scratch that goes through the pigment exposes the wood's natural color underneath, which on red oak means a pink line across a pale floor. They do hide dust and pet hair better than any dark floor, which is a real daily advantage.
Can a whitewashed floor be screened and recoated later?
Yes. A screen and recoat abrades the clear coats and adds a fresh one without touching the color underneath, so it refreshes a pale floor the same way it refreshes any other, at $1.99 per square foot. What it can't do is repair damage that has reached the wood, since that requires recoloring.
How long does the whole process take?
A pigmented whitewash follows the normal stained-refinish schedule: 3 to 5 days on site, socks the same evening as the last coat, furniture at 48 to 72 hours, rugs at about a week. Add a day or two if the floor is being bleached first, since the bleach has to neutralize, dry fully, and get lightly sanded before any color goes on.
Can you whitewash over an existing stained floor?
Not over it — the floor has to be sanded to bare wood first, because pigment can't be applied on top of an old finish or an old stain. Once it's bare, whether whitewash will work depends on the species and on how much color is left in the wood, which is what bleaching is for. We test a section before quoting the approach.
