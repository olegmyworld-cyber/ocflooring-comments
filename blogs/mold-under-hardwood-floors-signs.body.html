What Mold Under a Floor Actually Announces Itself With

The first sign is almost always smell, not sight. A persistent musty, earthy odor — strongest at floor level, worse on humid days, worse in a closed-up room, and unchanged by cleaning — is the classic report. Mold under a floor grows on the underside of boards, on the subfloor, and on any paper underlayment, all of which you can’t see, so the nose finds it before the eyes do.

Second: shape change. Boards that have cupped — edges higher than the centers — mean the underside is wetter than the top, which is exactly the condition growth needs. Boards that feel spongy underfoot, or that flex when you rock your weight, suggest the subfloor beneath has softened. We wrote up cupping and its causes in cupped hardwood floors, and the more advanced version, where boards lift entirely, in buckled hardwood after a leak.

Third: staining with a direction to it. Water damage spreads. If discoloration fans out from a wall, a doorway to a bathroom, a dishwasher, or an exterior corner — darkening as it approaches the source — that’s a moisture map. Add a finish that’s peeling or clouding in that same area, or a floor that has visibly darkened along one exterior wall, and you have a strong case for opening a small section and looking underneath rather than guessing.

The fourth sign is the building, not the floor: standing water or damp soil in the crawlspace, a missing or torn vapor barrier, condensation on ducts, downspouts discharging next to the foundation, or a bathroom with no working exhaust fan. In our experience the crawlspace tells the truth faster than the floor does, and it takes ten minutes to check.

What It Usually Isn’t

Plenty of alarming-looking floors have nothing growing under them, and it’s worth being honest about that before anybody panics or tears up a room.

Black stains are the biggest false alarm. Dark, almost inky marks on old floors are frequently a tannin-and-iron reaction — water plus a nail, a metal furniture foot, or the fasteners themselves — or old pet urine that reacted with the wood decades ago. Those look terrible and often can’t be sanded out, but they’re chemistry, not biology; the full explanation is in can black stains be sanded out. White or cloudy patches in the finish are usually moisture trapped in the film rather than growth beneath the wood, covered in white and cloudy spots on hardwood. And seasonal gapping and mild cupping in a Puget Sound winter is often just wood doing what wood does with humidity swings — see how humidity affects hardwood.

The distinguishing question is always the same: is there an active source of moisture? Chemistry-based staining is old, static, and doesn’t smell. Growth needs water now, and it comes with the odor and usually with movement in the boards.

The Order of Operations That Saves You Money

1. Stop the water. Nothing else matters until the source is found and fixed — a supply line, a failed wax ring, a roof or window leak, grading that sends rain at the foundation, a crawlspace with no vapor barrier, a bathroom fan venting into the attic. Flooring work over an unresolved source is money burned; the floor will do the same thing again.

2. Dry it, and measure the drying. Drying isn’t “it feels dry now.” It’s moisture readings in the wood and the subfloor returning to a normal range and staying there. Professional water-damage restoration exists for this and does it properly, with equipment and documentation. We keep meters in the truck and are glad to take readings, but if there’s been a real event, get a restoration firm in — our general notes on that are in what to do after water damage.

3. Assess the growth. Here’s where we stay in our lane: we install and refinish floors; we are not a mold remediation company and we don’t do air testing. If there’s visible growth beyond a small area, if the smell is strong, or if anyone in the house has respiratory issues or a compromised immune system, bring in a licensed remediation contractor and, if you want independent numbers, an indoor air quality professional. That work happens before flooring, not alongside it.

4. Then the floor. Once the assembly is dry and clean, the flooring decision is finally a flooring decision: sand and refinish if the boards are sound, replace affected boards and blend, or replace the floor in that area if the subfloor had to come out. We can lace new boards into an existing floor and refinish the whole room so it reads as one floor — that’s ordinary work — but it has to follow the drying, not precede it.   • Get moisture readings, not impressions, before and after drying
  • Bring in licensed remediation for anything beyond a small area
  • Document everything with photos and dates for your insurer

Don’t do this

  • Refinish over a floor that’s still reading wet — the finish will fail
  • Sand aggressively to chase black stains out of soft old fir
  • Seal or paint over the smell; coatings don’t stop growth
  • Run a dehumidifier and call it dry without measuring
  • Assume a floor that looks fine on top is fine underneath

Why This Comes Up So Often in Edmonds

Edmonds gives moisture more paths into a house than most of the cities we work in, and it’s not the homeowners’ fault — it’s geography and vintage. The bowl and the older streets above it are full of pre-war and early-postwar houses with post-and-pier or partial crawlspace foundations, and a fair number of those crawlspaces still have bare soil or a vapor barrier that tore twenty years ago. Damp ground under an unvented crawlspace puts moisture into the underside of a floor every day of the winter, which is the textbook cause of cupping and the reason a floor can smell musty with no leak anywhere.

Add the marine air and the terrain. Houses on the slope above the waterfront collect groundwater moving downhill, and a foundation that sits into that flow will stay damp regardless of how well the roof performs. Downspouts that end at the foundation instead of running out to the street are the single most common contributing detail we notice — a five-dollar extension has saved a lot of floors. The 1960s–1980s split-levels further inland bring a different version: a lower level partly below grade, with hardwood or engineered flooring installed over a slab or a low crawl where nobody ever thought about vapor.

The practical upside is that Edmonds problems are usually diagnosable in one visit, because the causes repeat. We’ll look at the floor, pull a register, take readings, and open the crawlspace hatch. If the answer is “your floor is fine, your crawlspace needs a vapor barrier,” we’ll say that and leave — the estimate is free either way. Mold Under Hardwood: What Edmonds Homeowners Ask

How do I know if it's mold and not just old stains?
Smell and movement are the tells. Growth produces a persistent musty odor at floor level and usually comes with cupped, soft, or spongy boards, because it needs active moisture. Old black stains from pet accidents or metal-and-water reactions look dramatic but are static, odorless, and decades old.
Can you refinish over a floor that had mold underneath?
Only after the moisture source is fixed, the assembly is dried to measured normal levels, and any growth has been properly remediated. Finish applied over damp wood doesn't bond or cure correctly and will cloud, peel, or cup within months. The order isn't negotiable, and we'll decline jobs that skip it.
Do you do mold remediation?
No. We're a flooring company — we sand, finish, repair, and install. We'll take moisture readings, look in the crawlspace, and tell you honestly what we see, but anything beyond a small area belongs to a licensed remediation contractor. If health concerns are involved, an indoor air quality professional should be part of it too.
Does the whole floor have to come out?
Usually not. If the damage is localized and the subfloor is sound, we replace affected boards, lace the new wood into the existing field, and refinish the room so it reads as one floor. Full replacement is for cases where the subfloor itself had to be cut out or the damage is widespread.
Why does my floor cup every winter in Edmonds?
Most often a crawlspace issue: bare soil, a torn vapor barrier, or poor ventilation putting moisture into the underside of the boards while your heated house dries the top side. That imbalance is exactly what cups a floor. Fixing the crawlspace usually does more for the floor than any flooring work would.
Will sanding remove mold?
No, and it's the wrong tool for it. Growth lives on the underside of boards, on the subfloor, and in the cavity — places a sander never reaches. Sanding the top surface removes discoloration you can see while leaving the cause untouched, which is how the problem comes back looking like a workmanship failure.
Is a musty smell always mold?
Not always, but it's always worth investigating. Crawlspace soil odor, a dry P-trap, a failing sump, or a damp subfloor without active growth can all produce it. The point of checking isn't to assume the worst; it's that the same investigation answers the question either way, and it's a short one.
What should I check before calling anyone?
Four things: whether the smell is stronger at floor level, whether any boards feel soft or look cupped, whether your downspouts discharge away from the foundation, and whether the crawlspace has an intact vapor barrier over dry soil. Photos of all four make the first conversation far more useful.
