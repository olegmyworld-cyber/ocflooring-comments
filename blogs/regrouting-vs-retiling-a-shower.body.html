Grout Is Not What Keeps the Water Out

Start with the fact that reframes the whole decision: cement grout is porous. Water passes through it — slowly through sound grout, quickly through cracked grout — and that is by design. In a properly built shower, what stops the water is a membrane behind the tile: a bonded sheet or liquid-applied layer that catches everything the grout lets through and sends it back to the drain. We walk through that whole assembly in our guide to shower waterproofing, and it is the single most useful thing to understand before you spend a dollar on grout.

The consequence cuts both ways. If the membrane behind your tile is intact, cosmetic grout failure is genuinely cosmetic — ugly, mold-prone, worth fixing, but not a leak in progress. And if the membrane has failed, or was never there, no amount of fresh grout will save the shower, because grout cannot do a membrane's job. Every 1990s and 2000s subdivision around Lake Stevens has showers built in the era when tile was routinely set over bare cement board with no membrane at all, and those are the ones where a regrout quote deserves real skepticism.

The Signs That Say Regrout

Some grout failure is just grout being grout. Cement grout shrinks slightly as it cures, wears under cleaning, and picks up soap and mineral staining that no scrubbing fully reverses. Here is the pattern that says the problem stops at the surface: the failure is spread evenly rather than concentrated in one spot; the tiles are all solid when you tap them — a sharp click, not a dull thud; the walls feel firm, with no flex when you press a palm against the center of a wall; and nothing downstairs has ever shown a stain. Hairline cracks where wall meets wall or wall meets tub are usually not even a grout failure — they are grout installed where silicone belongs. Every change of plane moves, and rigid grout in a moving joint cracks no matter how well it was mixed. The fix there is caulk, not tile.

A shower that checks those boxes is a good regrout candidate, and the honest version of the work is more than a skim coat. The old grout gets raked out to at least half the joint depth — ideally two-thirds — because new grout bonded to a thin smear of old grout fails in months. Then the joints are regrouted, the plane changes get silicone, and the whole thing gets sealed. Done that way, a regrout is a one-day job on most showers and the result looks close to new, especially if you use the opportunity to fix a grout color you have regretted since the day it went in.

The Signs That Say the Problem Is Behind the Tile

Now the other column. Recurring failure is the loudest signal there is. If this is your second or third round of grout repair in the same shower, the grout is not failing — something is moving or staying wet behind it, and rigid cement will keep cracking over that spot forever. Hollow-sounding tile means the bond between tile and substrate has let go, which is what happens when the board behind stays damp or the structure flexes. Mold you cannot kill — scrubbed away, back in three weeks — is being fed moisture from the wall cavity, not the shower side. And anything visible from below or beside the bathroom — a ceiling stain, bubbled paint, baseboard that swells in the next room — means the water has already won and the conversation is about how much of the surrounding structure comes out with the tile.

This is the point where homeowners understandably ask whether the whole thing can be avoided by tiling over what is there. In a dry area, sometimes. In a shower, no — and we wrote out exactly why in our guide to tiling over existing tile. A wet-area failure has to be opened, dried, and rebuilt from the studs out, because the thing that failed is the layer you cannot reach any other way. When we rebuild, that means a pre-slope under the pan, a bonded membrane over everything, and a photographed 24-hour flood test before a single tile goes on — the assembly that makes the next thirty years boring.

What an Honest Regrout Actually Involves

If your shower passes the inspection, insist the regrout is done properly, because the lazy version is everywhere. Grout smeared over old grout without raking the joints looks fine for a season and then delaminates in sheets. The real sequence: rake or grind the joints out to depth, vacuum the dust, regrout, tool the joints, then cut out every corner and plane-change joint and replace it with color-matched silicone. On a regrout we will also talk you through epoxy versus cement grout — epoxy costs more and is fussier to install, but in a shower it does not need sealing and shrugs off the staining that sent most people down this road in the first place.

One thing a regrout cannot do, no matter how well it is executed: add waterproofing that was never there. If your shower was built without a membrane and is somehow still dry, a regrout extends the run of good luck — it does not convert luck into engineering. We will tell you plainly which one you are buying.

The economics are lopsided, and that is exactly why the diagnosis matters. A regrout costs a few hundred dollars on most showers; a full rebuild is real money — we priced one honestly in what it costs to tile a shower, and installed tile work runs $14–$26 per square foot with labor from $11. But a regrout on a shower with failed waterproofing is the worst: you pay for the cosmetic work, the leak continues behind the fresh grout — often looking better than ever while it does — and every month it runs, the rot line moves further into framing and subfloor. Regrouting vs Retiling: What Homeowners Ask Us

How do I know if my shower needs regrouting or retiling?
Tap the tiles and read the pattern of failure. Evenly aged grout over solid-sounding tile, with no repeat failures and no staining below, points to a regrout. Hollow tiles, joints that keep failing in the same spots, mold that always returns, or any moisture showing outside the shower point to a failed assembly behind the tile — and that is a rebuild, because the layer that failed cannot be reached any other way.
Is regrouting a shower worth the money?
When the structure behind the tile is sound, absolutely — it is one of the best value repairs in a house. A proper regrout costs a small fraction of a rebuild and can add five to ten years to a shower. It is only wasted money when the waterproofing behind the tile has already failed, because then the leak continues behind the fresh grout.
Why does my shower grout keep cracking in the same place?
Because something behind that spot is moving or staying wet. Rigid cement grout over a moving joint or a damp, delaminating substrate will crack every time it is replaced. One round of cracking at corners usually just means grout was used where silicone belongs; the same field joints failing repeatedly means the problem is behind the tile.
Does cracked grout mean my shower is leaking?
Not by itself. Grout is porous even when intact, and a properly built shower has a membrane behind the tile that catches what passes through. Cracked grout speeds the water up but the membrane still does the stopping. The real question is whether that membrane exists and still works — which is what tapping tiles, checking for repeat failures, and looking below the bathroom tells you.
Can you just regrout over old grout?
No — and a contractor who proposes it is selling you a short season of cosmetics. New grout needs joint depth to bond, which means raking the old grout out to at least half its depth first. Grout skimmed over old grout delaminates in months. The corners should also be cut out and re-siliconed, since flexible joints are what stop the corner cracking from coming straight back.
How long does regrouting a shower take?
Most showers are a one-day job: raking the joints out, regrouting, tooling, and re-siliconing the plane changes. Epoxy grout or heavy rake-out can push it into a second day, and the shower should then rest before its first use while everything cures. A rebuild, for comparison, runs the better part of a week — waterproofing, a 24-hour flood test, tile, and grout each need their own time.
What does it cost to retile a shower if regrouting will not fix it?
Installed tile work runs $14 to $26 per square foot with labor from $11, and a shower sits at the top of that range because the waterproofing assembly — pre-slope, bonded membrane, curb, and a documented 24-hour flood test — is most of the labor. The number depends heavily on what the water has done to the framing behind, which is exactly why catching it early matters.
My shower was built in the 1990s with no membrane. Should I regrout it?
If it is dry, solid, and has never leaked, a regrout extends its run — honestly, its run of good luck, since grout cannot add waterproofing that was never installed. We will do that work and tell you plainly what it does and does not buy. What we will not do is call it a fix: when that shower does fail, the answer is a rebuild with a real membrane behind the tile.
