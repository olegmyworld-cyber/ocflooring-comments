Quick answer

The real risk when you refinish a reclaimed floor is that a standard job erases exactly what you paid for — the saw marks, nail holes, checks and century-old patina that made the wood worth salvaging in the first place. A full flatten at $3.99/sq ft turns a 1910 mill floor into a very expensive new floor. On most reclaimed installations in Seattle homes, the right move is the lightest one that solves the actual problem: a screen and recoat at $1.99/sq ft that renews the finish and touches nothing else, or a hand-controlled skip sand that takes the high spots and leaves the story.

What You’re Actually Buying With Reclaimed

“Character” is a vague word doing a lot of work, so it’s worth breaking into its parts, because each part has a different survival rate under a sander. There is surface texture: circular-saw arcs, straight-blade mill marks, hand-hewn facets, and the fine ripple of wood that dried in place. There is hole and void history: square cut-nail holes, bolt holes, mortises from where a joist once landed, worm track. There is checking and split — the fine end-grain cracks that come from a century of seasonal movement. And there is color, which is the subtlest and most fragile of the four: the deep oxidized tone of a surface that has been exposed to air, smoke, and sunlight since before your grandparents were born.

Here is the part that surprises people. Texture, holes, and checks live in the top few millimeters and can partly survive a light pass. Color does not live anywhere — it is the top few tenths of a millimeter. Sand a reclaimed board even lightly and the exposed wood is fresh, pale, and completely uncharacterful; that gray-brown patina cannot be sanded back into existence, and no stain reproduces it convincingly because stain sits in the grain rather than everywhere. Anyone selling you “refinishing that keeps the patina” is describing a recoat, whether or not they say so.

Metal, Grit, and the Things That Live in Salvaged Lumber

Reclaimed lumber carries its previous life inside it, and some of that has to be dealt with before any machine runs. Fasteners come first. Even carefully de-nailed stock hides broken cut nails, staples, and the occasional screw driven flush and forgotten. We go over reclaimed floors with a metal detector and a nail set before sanding — a missed head destroys a belt instantly, and if it catches the drum it can throw a gouge you’ll be looking at for twenty years. On floors where fastener holes are part of the look, we’re also careful about which ones get filled and which stay open, a judgment call we get into in our piece on nail holes, gouges, and what sanding can’t hide.

Embedded grit is the second surprise. Barn and industrial flooring spent decades absorbing sand, gravel dust, and mineral debris into its softer earlywood. That grit blunts abrasive at a rate that has nothing to do with the wood’s hardness, and it is a real reason reclaimed jobs consume more paper and more hours than their square footage suggests. Third: old coatings. Whitewash, milk paint, shellac, and industrial enamel all show up on salvage, and while we can usually work with them, anything that might be lead-based gets tested before sanding rather than after. And fourth, occasionally, is stock we decline — creosote- or penta-treated timber has no business being sanded indoors, and we’ll say so.

None of this is exotic; it’s just why a reclaimed quote isn’t a per-square-foot number off a rate card. We want to walk the floor, run a magnet and a moisture meter, and look at the underside of a board if there’s any way to get at one.

Choosing a Finish That Flatters Texture

Sheen matters more on reclaimed than on any other floor. A gloss or semi-gloss film reflects in a continuous sheet, and on a textured surface that reads as plastic wrap over interesting wood — it flattens the very relief you kept. Matte and satin scatter light instead, so saw marks and hollows keep their shadows. On reclaimed we specify matte or satin nearly every time; if you want the full reasoning across floor types, our guide to choosing a sheen covers it.

Base chemistry is the next call, and here our usual advice inverts slightly. We run waterborne on most floors because it dries clear and stays clear — which on reclaimed means it preserves whatever tone the wood already has rather than pushing it further. Bona and Pallmann waterborne systems go down in two to three coats with abrasion between them, socks the same evening, furniture at 48–72 hours, rugs at seven days, full cure at about a week. That fast, non-yellowing, low-odor package is why it’s the default.

The genuine alternative on character floors is a penetrating hardwax oil, which soaks in rather than building a film. It leaves the texture completely unmasked, it can be spot-repaired without recoating a whole room, and on hand-hewn or heavily saw-marked stock it simply looks better than any film — at the cost of more frequent maintenance and a lower tolerance for standing water. We lay out the honest trade-offs in our piece on penetrating oil finishes. What we steer people away from on reclaimed is oil-modified polyurethane: at roughly 450–550 g/L VOC, a coat a day, and about a 30-day cure, it also ambers relentlessly, which on already-warm fir pushes the whole room toward orange.

Seattle’s Reclaimed Stock, Specifically

Almost all the salvaged wood under Seattle floors is local, and it comes from three places. First and most common is old-growth Douglas fir pulled out of demolished houses, warehouses, and mill buildings — the same wood that’s still in service as original flooring in Ballard, Wallingford, and Queen Anne. It is remarkable material: tight vertical grain, twenty-plus rings to the inch, nothing like the fast-grown fir sold today. It is also soft by Janka’s reckoning at 660, so it dents, and we say that out loud at every estimate — the point we make at length in our piece on old-growth fir in Ballard and Queen Anne and in our guide to refinishing fir in old Seattle homes.

Second is industrial and warehouse stock from the Georgetown, SODO and Interbay building fabric — heavy fir and occasionally maple decking, often thick, often wide, with bolt holes and machine-oil staining that some clients love and some want gone. Third is agricultural salvage trucked in from eastern Washington and the Midwest: barn siding and threshing-floor plank, usually wide, usually the most texturally dramatic material and the least flat. Wide reclaimed plank brings its own set of problems — seasonal gapping, edge cupping, more movement than strip flooring — which we cover in our guide to refinishing wide-plank floors.

Where these floors end up is fairly predictable: Capitol Hill and Central District remodels, converted Georgetown and Fremont live-work spaces, and new builds in Ballard and Phinney where a reclaimed floor is doing the work of making a 2020s house feel rooted. Whichever it is, we quote it after seeing it — the details are on our Seattle hardwood floor refinishing page, and the same crews handle it across the Eastside and north into Snohomish County. Reclaimed Floors: What Owners Ask Before We Start

Can you refinish a reclaimed floor without losing the patina?
Only with a screen and recoat, which abrades the finish film and never reaches the wood. Any sanding, however light, exposes fresh wood at whatever it touches, and the oxidized color is only a few tenths of a millimeter deep. That's why we always ask what problem you're solving before we pick a service.
Our reclaimed floor is uneven and people trip. What now?
That's the one case where flattening is genuinely the right answer, and it means accepting the loss of patina at the high points. A skip sand is a middle path: we take the proud edges and leave the low areas untouched, which fixes the trip hazard while keeping much of the texture. It looks intentional because it is.
Does reclaimed wood cost more to refinish than regular hardwood?
Often, yes — not because of the rate but because of the hours. Embedded grit eats abrasive, fasteners have to be found and set, and much more of the work is done by hand rather than by machine. We quote reclaimed after seeing it in person rather than off a square-footage number.
Will you fill the nail holes and cracks?
That's your call, and we ask it before we start rather than deciding for you. Filling gives a smoother, more sanitary surface and a floor that's easier to clean; leaving them open keeps the honesty of the material. A common compromise is filling only what's deep enough to catch a heel or a vacuum and leaving the rest.
What finish looks best on textured reclaimed wood?
Matte or satin, and either a waterborne film or a penetrating hardwax oil. Gloss fights texture by reflecting in a continuous sheet. Hardwax oil is the purist's choice because it leaves the surface completely unmasked and repairs in patches; a waterborne film is the practical choice for busy households because it resists water and cleans easily.
Is reclaimed fir too soft for a kitchen or a house with dogs?
It dents, and it will keep denting. Douglas fir sits at 660 on the Janka scale against 1290 for red oak. Plenty of our clients put it in kitchens anyway and love it, because dents on a character floor read as more character. If a pristine surface matters to you, reclaimed fir is the wrong material and we'd rather say so early.
Can reclaimed engineered plank be refinished?
Only if the reclaimed face layer is thick enough, and many are not. The wear layer above the tongue is all you have, and on some reclaimed-face products it's thin enough that a single sand would go through. We measure before promising anything, and if it's marginal the honest answer is recoat only.
Do you install reclaimed flooring as well as refinish it?
Yes. New-install labor runs $3–$4.25 per square foot on top of materials, and reclaimed material itself ranges widely — often well above the $4–$10+ per square foot band that covers most new hardwood. Reclaimed also needs longer acclimation and more careful racking, since board thickness and width vary more than milled stock.
