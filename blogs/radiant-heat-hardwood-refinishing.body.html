Yes, You Can Refinish a Floor Over Radiant Heat

Sanding doesn’t care what’s under the subfloor. A drum or belt machine takes off finish and a thin layer of wood; an edger handles the perimeter; the Lägler TRIO blends the two so the room reads flat. None of that reaches anywhere near tubing in a slab or a gypcrete pour, and none of it reaches PEX stapled to the underside of a subfloor. The finish system is the same too — waterborne sealer plus two or three coats, socks the same evening, furniture at 48 to 72 hours, rugs at about a week.

So when someone in Redmond asks whether their radiant floors “can even be refinished,” the answer is almost always yes — the difficulty lives in the planning, not the sanding. Three things need to be true: the wood is thick enough to sand, the tubing’s location is known before anybody drives a fastener, and the heat is managed on a schedule around the job. The rest is a normal 3–5 day refinish.

The Heat Has to Come Down — and Come Back Up Slowly

Two separate reasons, and they’re both worth understanding rather than just following.

Finish behaves badly on a warm floor. Waterborne finish needs to flow out and level before it skins over. Heat coming up through the boards accelerates surface drying, so the wet edge closes faster than a coat can be laid into it — the result is lap marks where passes meet, visible stipple, and in bad cases adhesion problems between coats. This is not a subtle effect; it’s the single most common way a radiant refinish goes visibly wrong. We ask owners to bring the system down to a normal, non-heating setpoint about three or four days before we start so the slab and boards can equalize, not just the air.

Wood hates sudden temperature swings. Bringing a radiant system from off to full output the day after the last coat drives moisture out of the boards fast, which is how you get gaps opening, edges cupping the wrong way, or filler cracking loose. Most flooring manufacturers cap the floor surface temperature somewhere in the low 80s Fahrenheit, and every one of them asks for gradual ramping — a few degrees a day rather than a jump. The One Thing That Can Actually Go Wrong: Fasteners and Tubing

Sanding is safe. Repairs are where the risk lives. If three boards near the dishwasher need replacing, or a transition needs re-securing, somebody is about to put a fastener into a floor with water lines under it — and a punctured PEX loop in a slab is not a flooring repair anymore, it’s a plumbing excavation.

So we ask, before quoting any repair work: is the tubing in the concrete slab, in a gypcrete or lightweight-concrete pour over a wood subfloor, in aluminum transfer plates in the joist bays under the subfloor, or in a grooved subfloor panel system? Each answer changes what’s safe. Owners often have the as-built drawings from the builder or the installing contractor; on newer Redmond construction they’re frequently in the closing packet, and the HVAC contractor who commissioned the system will usually have them. Where there’s no documentation, the tubing gets located with a thermal camera while the system is running warm — a genuinely satisfying trick, since the loops light up like a diagram — before anyone picks up a nailer.

If the answer comes back “nobody knows,” we plan the job with no fasteners: glue-assisted repairs, face-nailing avoided entirely, and any board replacement handled with adhesive methods. It’s slightly slower and completely worth it.

Radiant Floors Move Differently, and Timing Matters

A floor with heat under it lives at a lower moisture content all winter than the same floor on forced air — the heat is coming up through the wood, all season. So radiant floors show their widest winter gaps and their tightest summer fit, and the swing is usually bigger than homeowners expect. The mechanism is the one in why hardwood floors develop gaps and humidity and hardwood, just amplified.

Two consequences for refinishing. First, don’t fill gaps at their widest. Sanding and filling a radiant floor in late January locks filler into joints that are going to close in July, and closing joints push filler up and out — a floor that looked perfect in February shows crumbs and ridges by summer. We’d rather sand a radiant floor in the shoulder seasons, or fill conservatively and accept that some seasonal gapping is the honest, permanent character of the floor. That scheduling logic is the same one we lay out for the region generally in how Puget Sound humidity changes your refinishing schedule.

Second, cupping and crowning read differently here. On a normal floor, cupping usually means moisture from below. On a radiant floor, it can also mean the system was ramped too aggressively or a loop is running hotter than its neighbors. Sanding a cupped floor flat before the cause is fixed produces a floor that crowns when it dries out — the whole argument we made in cupped hardwood floors and whether refinishing fixes it. Diagnose first, sand second. Always.

Is It Even Solid Wood? Check Before You Plan a Sand

This is where radiant-heat projects most often stall. Engineered flooring is the standard recommendation over radiant, because a plywood-core board moves less across its width than a solid one, and a lot of the wood over radiant in Redmond homes is engineered — often with a wear layer between 0.6 mm and 2 mm, sometimes 3 mm or more on better products. A full sand takes roughly a millimeter, so a 2 mm or thicker wear layer can usually be refinished at least once, a 3 mm layer more than once, and anything under about 2 mm generally cannot be sanded safely at all.

You can check without guessing: pull a floor vent or a threshold and look at the plank’s cut edge, where the layers are visible as clean lines. That, plus how many times the floor has already been refinished, decides the whole conversation. Our full guide is can engineered hardwood be refinished — check the wear layer, and it’s the first thing we look at on radiant jobs. When there isn’t enough wear layer, the honest alternative is a buff and coat at $1.99/sq ft, which restores the finish without spending wood the floor doesn’t have.

Green lights for a full refinish

  • Solid hardwood, or engineered with 2 mm+ of wear layer left
  • As-built drawings or a thermal scan showing where the loops run
  • A system that can be ramped down and back up on a schedule
  • A floor that lies flat now — no active cupping or crowning
  • Shoulder-season timing, so gaps aren’t at their winter widest

Stop and check first when…

  • Nobody knows whether the tubing is in the slab or in the joist bays
  • The wear layer is thin, or the floor has already been sanded twice
  • Boards are cupped and the cause hasn’t been found yet
  • The system can’t be turned down (shared boiler, tenant, no thermostat access)
  • It’s January and the gaps are at their annual maximum

Refinishing Over Radiant Heat: What Redmond Homeowners Ask

Can hardwood over radiant heat be sanded and refinished?
Yes, in almost every case. Sanding removes finish and a thin layer of wood and never gets close to tubing in a slab, a gypcrete pour, or the joist bays. The two things that can stop a refinish are a floor that has already been sanded down to very little thickness, and engineered flooring with a wear layer too thin to survive a pass.
Does refinishing over radiant heat cost more?
No. Our rates are the same: $3.99 per square foot for a natural refinish, $6.50 with stain, $1.99 for a screen and recoat, with a 500 square foot minimum. What radiant changes is the schedule around the work, not the price of the work itself.
Do I have to turn the heat off?
Down first, then off during the coating days and the cure window. We ask for a ramp down to a normal non-heating setpoint three or four days before we start so the slab and the boards equalize, off through the finish coats and roughly the first seven days of cure, then a gradual ramp back up of a few degrees per day.
What happens if the heat stays on while you're finishing?
The finish skins over before it can flow out and level. That shows up as lap marks where passes meet, visible stipple, and sometimes adhesion problems between coats. It's the most common way a radiant refinish goes visibly wrong, and it's entirely avoidable with a few days of planning.
How do you know where the tubing is?
Ideally from the as-built drawings, which builders often include in the closing packet and which the HVAC contractor who commissioned the system usually keeps. Where there's no documentation, we locate the loops with a thermal camera while the system is running warm. If it still can't be confirmed, we plan any repairs with adhesive methods and no fasteners at all.
Should I fill the gaps in a radiant floor?
Conservatively, and not in the middle of winter. Radiant floors run drier in the heating season, so gaps are at their widest in January and close again in summer. Filler packed into a joint at its widest gets pushed out when the joint closes, so we'd rather sand in the shoulder seasons and accept some seasonal gapping as the honest character of the floor.
My radiant floor is cupped. Will sanding fix it?
Only after the cause is fixed. Sanding a cupped floor flat while it's still holding extra moisture produces a floor that crowns once it dries out, which is a worse problem than the one you started with. On radiant specifically, cupping can also mean the system was ramped too fast or a loop is running hot, so the diagnosis comes before the machines.
Is my floor solid or engineered, and does it matter?
It matters more than anything else on this list. A lot of wood over radiant is engineered, because it moves less than solid across its width. Pull a floor vent or a threshold and look at the plank's cut edge: if you can see thin layers, it's engineered, and the top layer's thickness decides whether it can be sanded. Under about 2 mm, a screen and recoat is the safe option instead.
