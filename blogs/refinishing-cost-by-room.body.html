Quick answer

Refinishing is priced by the square foot — $1.99 for a screen and recoat, $3.99 for a full sand with natural finish, $6.50 with stain — but rooms don’t cost the same per foot, because rooms aren’t equally full of edges, appliances, and stairs. A 300 sq ft Bellevue bedroom is nearly all open field and prices exactly as the math says. A kitchen adds $80 per appliance we pull. A hallway is mostly perimeter. And a staircase isn’t priced by area at all: it’s $55–$75 per tread, which makes fourteen steps cost about as much as a bedroom.

Why Square Footage Alone Doesn’t Price a Floor

Sanding a floor is two different jobs happening in the same room. The field — the open middle — is machine work, and it moves fast: the big machine covers ground, the Lägler TRIO makes the final flattening pass, and productivity is high. The perimeter is hand and edger work, done on your knees, slower per foot by a wide margin, and it doesn’t care how big the room is. It cares how long the walls are.

That’s the whole reason room costs diverge. A 400 sq ft living room might have 80 linear feet of edge. Four 100 sq ft rooms with the same total area have roughly twice that, plus four doorways, four thresholds, and four closets. Same square footage on paper, meaningfully more labor in reality. It’s also why an open-plan Bellevue great room is the best value in the house and a warren of small 1980s bedrooms is the worst — and why we quote from a walkthrough rather than from a number you read off a listing.

The second driver is everything that isn’t floor: appliances, cabinet toe kicks, radiator covers, built-ins, closet doors on tracks, and transitions to tile or carpet. Each one is a stop, a hand tool, and a decision. We itemize them so nothing appears as a surprise on the invoice — the general version of that argument is in the hidden costs in refinishing quotes.

The Kitchen: Appliances, Toe Kicks, and Water

Kitchens are the most complicated room to refinish and the most valuable one to get right, because they take more abuse than any other floor in the house. Three things drive the number here. First, appliances: anything sitting on the wood has to come out and go back, and we price that honestly at $80 per appliance, or $160 for a washer-and-dryer pair in a laundry area. A typical kitchen means the refrigerator and usually the dishwasher and range — call it $240 in most Bellevue kitchens. It isn’t a padded line item; it’s two people, a dolly, appliance sliders, and the disconnect-and-reconnect on water and gas that we do carefully because the alternative is a leak behind the fridge you find in April.

Second, cabinets. We refinish up to the toe kick, not under the cabinets, and that boundary is a permanent one — the finish stops at the same line the original floor stops at. That matters enormously if you’re also planning a kitchen remodel, because the order of operations changes what’s possible and what it costs. If new cabinets are going in, the floor should be done in coordination, and we wrote the full sequencing argument in refinishing hardwood under kitchen cabinets. Doing it in the wrong order is one of the more expensive mistakes available to a homeowner.

Third, condition. Kitchen floors are where we find the water damage: black staining at the dishwasher, cupping in front of the sink, a soft board by the fridge line. Some of that sands out and some of it doesn’t, and board replacement is quoted separately after we see it. On the plus side, kitchens are small — 120 to 200 sq ft in most Eastside houses — so even at $3.99/sq ft plus appliances, the room itself rarely exceeds a thousand dollars.

Stairs: The Most Expensive 40 Square Feet in the House

Stairs get their own pricing model because they are their own trade. There is no machine that sands a staircase; every tread is hand-sanded, every riser and nosing detailed, and the balusters have to be worked around one at a time without touching the paint. That’s why the number is $55–$75 per tread rather than a square-foot rate, and why a fourteen-step staircase — maybe 40 square feet of actual wood — runs $770 to $1,050, about what a good-sized bedroom costs.

Where a staircase lands in that range depends on the details: open treads with exposed sides cost more than closed boxed stairs, spindles at tight spacing take longer than a simple wall-side run, and a landing counts as its own area. Painted risers versus stained risers is another fork, and a curved or winder staircase — common in the 1990s Bellevue two-stories with the big entry — takes noticeably longer than a straight run. The full breakdown is in stair refinishing cost per step.

One practical note about sequencing: stairs are usually the last thing coated, because they’re also the only way to the second floor. On an occupied home we plan the schedule so nobody is stranded upstairs, which sometimes means doing the staircase in two halves on alternating days. That’s part of the room-by-room scheduling logic we lay out in refinishing an occupied home room by room.

Hallways, Landings, and the 500 sq ft Minimum

Small spaces are where homeowners are most surprised, and it’s worth explaining rather than defending. A 90 sq ft hallway at $3.99 is $359 of revenue for a job that still requires the truck, the machines, the dust setup, the finish system, and two or three trips over several days. That math doesn’t work, which is why we hold a 500 sq ft minimum. It isn’t a penalty for small jobs; it’s the honest floor beneath which a professional refinish can’t be done at professional standards.

The practical move is bundling. If the hallway is worn but the adjoining bedrooms aren’t, we’ll often suggest recoating the whole floor at $1.99/sq ft instead of sanding one strip — it clears the minimum, costs less per foot, and avoids the single worst outcome, which is a beautifully refinished hallway that no longer matches anything it connects to. Where that mismatch is unavoidable, a transition is sometimes the better design answer than pretending it isn’t there; we covered the options in flooring transitions between rooms, and the small-job economics in detail in minimum job sizes for small refinishing jobs.

Bedrooms and Open Areas: Where the Money Goes Furthest

Bedrooms are the easy part. Low traffic means the finish usually isn’t worn through, which frequently makes them recoat candidates at $1.99/sq ft rather than full-sand candidates at $3.99 — and on an upstairs level that has never seen a dog or a dining chair, that’s often the honest recommendation even though it earns us less. The main variables are closets (edge work, sliding door tracks) and whether the furniture is out before we arrive.

Open living and dining areas are the best value per dollar in any house: long uninterrupted runs, few obstacles, high machine productivity. In the open-plan Bellevue houses built from the late 1990s on, a single connected great room can be half the total square footage and less than half the total labor. This is also where the whole-house decision gets made — once you’re above roughly 800 square feet of contiguous floor, the per-foot economics are as good as they’re going to get, and the question shifts from cost to whether the wood has enough life left, which we worked through with real numbers in refinish vs. replace on 800 sq ft of oak.

A Real Bellevue House, Priced Out

Take a shape we see constantly: a two-story built between 1985 and 2005, red oak on the main floor, carpet upstairs, a staircase with a landing, and a kitchen with an island. Main floor is roughly 1,100 sq ft of wood — entry, great room, dining, hallway — plus a 160 sq ft kitchen and fourteen stairs.

Full sand with a natural waterborne finish: 1,260 sq ft × $3.99 = $5,027. Appliances in the kitchen — refrigerator, dishwasher, range — add $240. Fourteen treads at the middle of the range, $65, adds $910. If the family is staying in the house and we’re containing dust off the HVAC, add $250. That’s about $6,427 for a whole main floor plus stairs, in three to five days, with socks on the floor the evening of the final coat and furniture back at 48 to 72 hours. Choosing a stain instead of natural moves the field rate to $6.50 and takes the same job past $9,500 — which is why we ask hard questions about whether the color is worth it on Bellevue’s red oak, since a clear waterborne over red oak reads exactly the way most Eastside buyers want it to.

Two Bellevue-specific notes. First, this city has no shortage of 1980s and 1990s red oak in good structural shape but tired finish — a screen and recoat at $1.99/sq ft would take that same 1,260 sq ft to about $2,507, and on plenty of houses that’s genuinely the right call. Second, if you’re in a downtown condo rather than a house, the building’s rules matter more than the square footage does; noise windows, elevator reservations, and HOA approval are the real schedule. Refinishing Cost by Room: Bellevue Questions

Can you just refinish one room?
Only if it clears our 500 sq ft minimum, or if it can be bundled with adjacent areas. Below that, the fixed costs of a professional job — mobilization, machines, containment, a full finish system — don't fit inside the price. We'll usually suggest either expanding the scope or recoating a larger area at $1.99/sq ft instead.
Why do stairs cost so much compared to the floor?
Because there's no machine for stairs. Every tread, riser, and nosing is hand-sanded and hand-coated, worked around balusters one at a time. Fourteen steps is roughly 40 square feet of wood and a full day or more of skilled labor, which is why it's priced at $55 to $75 per tread rather than by area.
Do you move the refrigerator and other appliances?
Yes, and we charge $80 per appliance, or $160 for a washer-and-dryer pair, because it's real work: disconnecting, moving carefully across a floor mid-project, and reconnecting water and gas properly. You can move them yourself to save the line item, but we'd rather do the water connections than fix them later.
Is the kitchen more expensive per square foot than a bedroom?
The rate is the same — $3.99/sq ft for a full sand with natural finish — but kitchens carry extras that bedrooms don't: appliances at $80 each, more transitions, and a higher chance of water-damaged boards needing replacement. Bedrooms are usually the cheapest rooms in a house to refinish.
Should I refinish the whole floor or just the worn rooms?
Whole floor, almost always, if the rooms connect. A newly refinished room next to an old one never matches — different wear, different oxidation, different sheen — and the boundary is more noticeable than the wear you were fixing. Where rooms are genuinely separate, doing them in phases is reasonable.
What does refinishing a whole Bellevue main floor typically cost?
For a common two-story with roughly 1,260 sq ft of wood downstairs plus fourteen stairs: about $5,000 for the field at $3.99/sq ft, around $900 for the staircase, $240 for kitchen appliances, and $250 if dust containment is needed — call it $6,400. A screen and recoat on the same floor would be closer to $2,500.
Does a stain cost more in some rooms than others?
No — staining moves the rate to $6.50/sq ft everywhere it's applied. What it does change is the schedule: stain adds a day for the color to dry before sealing, and it makes matching between rooms harder if you ever refinish in phases. Natural finish is both cheaper and easier to match later.
Do I have to move all the furniture out?
Everything has to be off the floors we're coating. Most clients handle the small items and we quote labor for the heavy pieces if they'd rather we did it. Whichever way you go, it should be written into the estimate — an unclear furniture plan is the most common cause of a lost day on a residential schedule.
