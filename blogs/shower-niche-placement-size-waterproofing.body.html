Why a Box in the Wall Is a Bigger Deal Than It Looks

A niche looks like the simplest feature in a shower — a rectangle, some tile, done. Mechanically it is the opposite. A shower wall is one continuous waterproof plane, and a niche cuts a hole in it, then asks the installer to waterproof six new surfaces, a dozen new corners, and one shelf that gravity will hold water on every single day. Corners and horizontal surfaces are exactly where membranes are hardest to get right, which is why the niche is the single most common place we find the leak when we open up a failed DIY or budget-remodel shower.

None of this is a reason to skip the niche. Built into the membrane system properly, a niche is as durable as the rest of the shower and better than any alternative — wire baskets rust, corner shelves crowd the shower, and a teak stool is a puddle with legs. It is a reason to treat the niche as part of the waterproofing scope, priced and built by whoever is responsible for the whole assembly, rather than a decorative extra squeezed in after the important decisions are made.

Placement: Reach First, Spray Second, Studs Third

Height. For a standing niche, put the shelf you use most between 48 and 60 inches off the shower floor — roughly between the wrist and shoulder of the people who shower there. Taller households push toward the top of that range. If the shower serves someone seated, or you are planning to age in place, a second niche or a lower shelf at 30–36 inches earns its keep every day. A tub-side niche for bathing kids sits lower still. The test is not a chart; it is standing in the space and reaching.

Which wall. The ideal spot is a wall the showerhead does not blast directly — usually the same wall as the plumbing or the wall opposite the spray, where bottles are not standing in the firehose line all day. We also try to keep the niche out of the sightline from the bathroom door when the layout allows; a niche full of shampoo bottles is functional, not decorative. And in a Pacific Northwest house we avoid exterior walls when there is any alternative: a niche in an exterior wall displaces insulation, and the recess becomes a cold spot that grows condensation on the coldest mornings of the year. When the exterior wall is the only candidate, the bay gets rebuilt with rigid insulation behind the niche — not just emptied out.

Studs. Standard framing runs 16 inches on center, which leaves about 14½ inches of clear width in a bay — exactly why the classic prefab niche is 12 inches wide. Wider niches mean reframing: a header above, a sill below, sometimes a king stud. Entirely doable, priced as carpentry, and worth it for a double-wide niche in a primary shower — but it is framing work, and anyone who quotes a 24-inch-wide niche without mentioning framing has not thought about the wall they are cutting into. Plumbing and vents also live in walls; the niche goes where the wall is empty, which is a thing we verify, not assume.

Prefab Foam Niche vs Site-Built: The Honest Comparison

Prefab foam niches — the Schluter, Wedi and similar waterproof foam units — are what we use most. The box arrives already waterproof; the craft is in tying its flange into the wall membrane so the box and the wall become one continuous system. They come in the standard sizes above, they are consistent, and they take framing surprises out of the schedule. The limit is the catalog: if your design calls for a size or shape the manufacturers do not make, you are building it.

Site-built niches — framed in wood, skinned in backer board, then waterproofed with a bonded sheet or liquid membrane — can be any size, any shape, with any shelf arrangement. They are also entirely dependent on the installer’s membrane work in the hardest geometry a shower offers: inside corners, outside corners, and a horizontal shelf, all of it hand-detailed. Done well, indistinguishable in lifespan from prefab. Done by whoever caused the average DIY leak, it is the first thing to fail. Ask which approach your bid assumes — it tells you whether the niche was priced as waterproofing or as decoration.

Two details separate a niche that lasts from one that does not, regardless of method. The shelf must slope — a few degrees toward the shower, so water drains off instead of ponding against the back wall. Dead-level shelves look right on day one and grow black grout lines by year two. And the finished edges matter: mitered tile or a metal profile at the niche’s outside corners protects the most exposed edges in the whole shower. Bullnose that does not exist in your tile line is how niches end up with raw cut edges that read cheap forever.

Waterproofing: Where DIY Showers Actually Fail

Here is the uncomfortable truth behind the how-to videos: the niche is usually the hardest waterproofing in the shower, and it is handed to the least experienced person on the job. Cement board is not waterproof. Grout is not waterproof. A niche built from bare backer board and tiled over will pass water into the wall cavity from its first week in service — slowly, invisibly, into insulation and framing, until the drywall on the other side stains or the trim swells. By then the fix is demolition.

What right looks like: the niche’s six surfaces and every corner are covered by the same membrane system as the rest of the shower — bonded sheet with preformed corners and sealed seams, or a liquid membrane built up to the manufacturer’s measured thickness, flood-tested as part of the whole assembly. On our showers that means the niche is membraned when the pan is membraned, checked when the pan is checked, and standing behind the same photographed 24-hour flood test and 2-year warranty. The niche is not an accessory to the waterproofing; it is the stress test of it. The same principle decides bigger questions too — it is why we will not tile over existing tile in a wet area, where the membrane underneath can never be inspected.

Shower Niches: What Homeowners Ask Us

What height should a shower niche be?
For a standing shower, put the main shelf between 48 and 60 inches off the shower floor — roughly wrist-to-shoulder height for the people using it, so bottles are reachable without stooping. A tub-side or seated-user niche belongs lower, around 30 to 36 inches, and a small foot-prop niche for shaving sits at 12 to 18 inches. Stand in the space and reach before anyone cuts a hole.
What is the standard shower niche size?
The workhorse sizes are 12×12 and 12×24 inches, because standard framing leaves about 14½ inches of clear space between studs — a 12-inch-wide niche drops into one bay without touching structure. Wider niches, like a 24-inch horizontal or a full-width band, are absolutely buildable but require reframing with a header, which should show up in the quote as carpentry.
Are prefab foam shower niches good?
Yes — they are what we use most. Units from Schluter, Wedi and similar systems arrive already waterproof, and the craft is in tying their flanges into the wall membrane so the box and wall become one continuous system. Site-built niches make sense when you want a size or shape the catalogs do not offer; they just depend entirely on hand-detailed membrane work in the trickiest corners of the shower.
Why do shower niches leak?
Because a niche adds six surfaces, a shelf, and a dozen corners to a wall whose job was staying continuous — and because DIY builds often tile over bare cement board on the theory that grout keeps water out. It does not; grout is porous. Water migrates into the wall cavity slowly and invisibly until something stains or swells. The fix at that point is demolition, which is why the membrane has to be continuous through the niche from day one.
Should the niche go on an exterior wall?
Avoid it when you can. Recessing a niche into an exterior wall displaces insulation, creating a cold spot that condenses moisture in a Pacific Northwest winter. When the exterior wall is the only sensible location, the bay should be rebuilt with rigid insulation behind the niche rather than simply emptied — a detail worth asking your contractor about directly.
Does a shower niche need to be sloped?
The shelf does, yes — a slight pitch toward the shower so water drains off instead of ponding against the back corner. A dead-level shelf holds a permanent shallow puddle, and that puddle is where the black grout line and the first membrane test happen. It is a small detail that separates installers who have opened up failed showers from those who have not.
How much does adding a niche cost?
A standard single-bay niche is a modest line item inside the tile scope — the prefab unit or framing, membrane detailing, and extra cutting and edge finishing. It rides along with waterproofing work already underway, inside tile work that runs $14–$26 per square foot installed. Custom double-wide and full-width niches cost meaningfully more because they add real framing and membrane time, and they should be priced explicitly.
Can a niche be added to an existing tiled shower?
Not honestly, no. Cutting a niche into a finished shower means breaching the existing waterproofing and trying to tie a new membrane into an old one you cannot see or test — behind tile you would be destroying anyway. If the shower is due for a niche, it is due at rebuild time, when the membrane can be made continuous and flood-tested as one system.
