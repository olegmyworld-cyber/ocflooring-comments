The Honest Fork

This is the most common either/or question we get in Monroe, and it deserves a straight answer instead of a sales pitch for the pricier option. Both materials are good at being bathroom floors. Luxury vinyl plank is waterproof as a material, comfortable underfoot, quick to install, and genuinely attractive now — the wood-look printing stopped being embarrassing years ago. Tile costs several times more installed and pays it back in categories vinyl cannot enter: it is waterproof as an assembly, not just a material; it lasts as long as the house; and it is the only one of the two that can climb the walls of a shower.

So the fork is not “good versus cheap.” It is: what is this bathroom for, how long will you own it, and does the project include a shower? Answer those three and the material usually picks itself.

The Wet-Room Reality Nobody Prints on the Box

“100% waterproof” on a vinyl carton is true and misleading at the same time. The plank is waterproof — submerge one for a month and nothing happens. The floor is not, because a floor is hundreds of seams, four walls of perimeter gaps, a toilet flange, and a tub edge. Water that sits on a vinyl floor finds those openings eventually, and once it is underneath, the waterproof plank becomes a lid: the subfloor below stays wet, quietly, until the damage is structural. In a splash-and-mop bathroom this rarely matters. In a kids’-bathtub bathroom, or under a leak nobody notices for a week, it is the whole story. Tile handles the same event differently: set over a bonded membrane with sealed transitions, the assembly sheds water back into the room where you can see it — the same philosophy, turned vertical, that we detail in how a tile shower is waterproofed.

And the shower itself is not a debate. Vinyl plank has no legitimate role inside one — not on the walls, not on a pan. If your project includes rebuilding the shower, tile is in the project no matter what the floor gets, and the honest comparison becomes tile-everywhere versus tile-shower-plus-vinyl-floor. That second option is a genuinely good budget play, and we build it often.

What Your Subfloor Gets to Say About It

Monroe’s housing stock argues both directions. The newer subdivisions off Chain Lake Road and toward Fryelands stand on flat, sound OSB or plywood that accepts either material with minimal prep. The older farmhouse-era homes near downtown and out toward the valley are a different conversation: a century of settling means floors that dip and joists sized for lighter loads. Tile is unforgiving about both — it wants flatness and stiffness, and getting an old floor there is real prep money, part of what the long-lasting tile floor standard is built on. Vinyl is far more tolerant of a slightly imperfect floor — planks flex where tile would crack — which quietly makes LVP the pragmatic answer in some older Monroe bathrooms unless the owner wants to fund the structural work first. One thing vinyl does not tolerate: lumps and ridges telegraph through thin planks quickly, so “more tolerant” never means “no prep.”

Money, Over Ten Years Instead of One Invoice

On invoice day, vinyl wins by a landslide: labor at $3–$4.25 per square foot plus modest material, against tile at $14–$26 installed, means a typical 40 sq ft bathroom floor differs by four figures. Over a decade, the gap narrows. Vinyl in a busy bathroom is a 10–20 year floor — sooner if a failed seam or a long leak forces the issue — so a thirty-year horizon likely contains a replacement or two. Tile installed correctly is a one-time cost on any horizon a homeowner actually plans across. Neither answer is wrong; they are different loans. Vinyl borrows against the future at low interest; tile pays cash up front. Resale deserves one honest paragraph. In a powder room or a kids’ bath, good LVP reads as clean and current and costs you nothing with buyers. In the primary bathroom of a house you will sell, tile reads as finished in a way vinyl does not, and appraisers and buyers in the Seattle metro have been consistent about it. If the house is a keeper, please yourself; if a sale is in the plan, put the tile where buyers look hardest.

Tile vs Luxury Vinyl in the Bathroom: What Monroe Homeowners Ask

Is luxury vinyl or tile better for a bathroom?
For the floor of a budget-conscious, shower-stays-put bathroom, luxury vinyl is a genuinely good answer: waterproof as a material, warm underfoot, installed in about a day. For a bathroom with a shower rebuild, a heated floor, or resale weight — and for anything inside the shower itself — tile is the better tool. We install both and will say which your room is asking for.
Can luxury vinyl plank go inside a shower?
No. Not the walls, not the pan. Vinyl plank is a floating floor with seams; a shower is a waterproofing assembly that has to hold water under pressure at every joint. Showers get a pre-slope, a bonded membrane, a 24-hour flood test, and tile. A vinyl bathroom floor outside a properly built tile shower, though, is a combination we build all the time.
How much cheaper is vinyl than tile in a bathroom?
Several times cheaper installed. Our LVP install labor runs $3–$4.25 per square foot plus material; tile runs $14–$26 per square foot installed with labor from $11. On a typical 40 square foot bathroom floor that is a four-figure difference — real money, buying a shorter-lived floor. Which side of that trade is right depends on the room and your horizon.
Is LVP really waterproof if the box says 100% waterproof?
The plank is; the floor is not. Water that sits on a vinyl floor eventually finds seams, edges, and the toilet flange, and once it is under the planks it stays there, against the subfloor, where you cannot see it. For mop-and-splash life that is fine. For standing-water bathrooms, tile over a membrane handles the event; vinyl hides it.
Which lasts longer, tile or luxury vinyl?
Tile, and it is not close. A correctly installed tile floor is measured in decades and usually outlasts the ownership of the house. Quality LVP in a bathroom is a 10–20 year floor, less if a seam fails or a leak goes unnoticed. Over one invoice vinyl wins; over thirty years the totals converge more than people expect.
Does tile or vinyl add more to resale value?
In a primary bathroom, tile — it reads as permanent and finished to buyers and appraisers alike. In powder rooms, kids’ baths, and rentals, clean current LVP costs you nothing with buyers and keeps the budget for rooms that move the price. Spend the tile money where buyers look hardest.
Is tile too cold for a bathroom floor?
Bare tile is cooler than vinyl underfoot, which is either a complaint or an invitation: tile is the natural partner for electric floor heat, which turns the coldest floor in the house into the warmest room in it. If floor heat is out of the budget, a bath mat solves most of the issue — but warm-out-of-the-box is a legitimate point in vinyl’s column.
Can you put luxury vinyl over an old tile floor?
Often yes — sound, flat existing tile makes a workable base for floating LVP once grout lines are skimmed and height transitions are planned at the door. It is one of vinyl’s best budget tricks, since it skips demolition entirely. Cracked, hollow, or heaving tile is a different story: that gets diagnosed, not buried.
