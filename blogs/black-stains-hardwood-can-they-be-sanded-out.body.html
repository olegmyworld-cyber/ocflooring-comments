Every black stain call starts the same way: the carpet came up, or the rug moved, and there it is — a dark patch or a scatter of near-black rings that no cleaner touches. The homeowner’s question is always “will it sand out?”, and the honest answer is “sometimes,” which is unsatisfying enough that we wrote this article. Because whether a black mark sands out isn’t random — it follows from what made the mark and how long it had to work.

The chemistry is worth thirty seconds, because it drives every decision after it. Wood — oak especially — is full of tannins. When water carries dissolved iron into the board (from a nail, a radiator, a plant stand, a pet’s accident dissolving metal fasteners below), the iron and tannins react into iron tannate: the same blue-black compound in old-fashioned ink. Pet urine does something similar on its own schedule, with ammonia compounds darkening the wood as they concentrate. Either way, the color isn’t sitting on the wood. It has become part of the wood — and the only question that matters is how deep.

Reading the Stain: What Made It Tells You What Fixes It

Black marks come in recognizable signatures. Rings and crescents trace the footprint of something that sat and sweated — plant pots, vases, furniture legs — classic water-plus-iron marks, often shallow. Splotches with soft, cloudy edges, frequently in room corners or along walls, are the signature of pet urine, and they usually run deep because urine keeps soaking after the surface looks dry. Board-following darkness — staining that runs along the wood grain from a wall, a radiator, or an exterior door — points to a moisture source that worked over months, and the stain typically goes as deep as the water did. And a distinct gray-to-black in a moist area with a musty smell can be mold in or under the boards, which is a moisture problem first and a cosmetic problem second — if the floor is also deformed, read our piece on buckled hardwood after a leak before thinking about sanding anything.

The Depth Test: Why Some Stains Sand Out and Some Don’t

Sanding is subtraction — a full refinish takes off roughly 1/32 of an inch of wood, and a stain shallower than that leaves with the dust. Surface-gray water marks, light rings, and young stains often clear completely. But iron tannate and old urine stains routinely penetrate two, three, ten times that depth, and you cannot simply keep sanding until they’re gone: chasing a deep stain digs a visible dish into the floor, and every extra pass spends wear thickness the floor doesn’t get back — a floor only has so many sandings in it, and burning two of them on one stubborn stain is bad arithmetic. Our approach is sand-and-see: take the floor down as a normal refinish would, evaluate what remains, and then choose from the ladder below with the stain actually visible instead of guessing under old finish.

The Bleach Conversation, Without the Sales Pitch

Oxalic acid deserves its reputation on the right stain: it chemically reverses the iron-tannate reaction, and a black water ring can genuinely vanish under it. But it’s a targeted tool, not magic. It does little against urine staining, whose chemistry is different; it can leave a lightened halo where the treated wood ends up paler than its neighbors; and its stronger cousin — two-part A/B bleach — strips the wood’s own color along with the stain, which is why we treat it as the last stop before replacement. Bleached spots also take stain and finish differently than the surrounding floor, so the “fixed” area can announce itself in afternoon light. We use both chemistries, we’re glad they exist, and we quote them honestly: on iron stains, good odds; on urine, don’t pay for optimism.

Pet Stains Are Their Own Category

Urine is the hardest black stain because it isn’t one event — it’s a slow soak that keeps chemically working long after it dries, often reaching through the board and into the subfloor, and bringing an odor that sanding alone doesn’t remove. A floor with one or two pet spots is usually a board-replacement conversation; a floor with widespread staining becomes a judgment call between a dark-stain refinish at $6.50/sq ft that folds the damage into a deliberately deeper color, and selective replacement of the worst boards before a natural refinish. Black Stains on Hardwood: What Edmonds Homeowners Ask Us

What causes black stains on hardwood floors?
Almost always one of two chemical reactions: water carrying dissolved iron into the wood, where it reacts with tannins to form ink-black iron tannate — or pet urine, whose compounds darken wood as they concentrate and soak. Ordinary dirt and spills don't turn wood black; if a mark is truly black, chemistry happened.
Can black stains be sanded out of hardwood?
Sometimes. A full sanding removes about 1/32 of an inch, so stains shallower than that — gray marks, light rings, young spills — usually disappear. True black stains have typically penetrated several times deeper and survive sanding. We sand first and evaluate what remains, rather than promising an outcome we can't see under the old finish.
Does vinegar, hydrogen peroxide, or baking soda remove black stains?
On genuine black stains, effectively no. Household peroxide can slightly lighten very fresh marks, but iron-tannate and set urine stains are in the wood's structure, beyond what surface treatments reach. The DIY approaches mostly waste weeks — and strong ones can damage the surrounding finish, adding a repair to the bill.
What is oxalic acid and does it work?
Oxalic acid is wood bleach — it chemically reverses the iron-tannin reaction, and on black water rings it can work impressively well. It does much less against pet urine, which has different chemistry. Applied carelessly it lightens a halo around the stain, so we treat it as a precision tool used after sanding, not a first resort.
Is a black stain a sign of mold?
Usually not — iron tannate and urine account for most of what we see. But gray-black discoloration in a damp area with a musty smell can be mold in or under the boards, and that's a moisture investigation before it's a refinishing job. If the boards are also cupped or lifted, have the water source found first; sanding a wet problem locks it in.
Can stained boards just be replaced?
Yes, and for deep, localized damage it's often the best value: we cut out the affected boards, weave in matching stock, and refinish so the repair disappears into the floor. The craft is in the match — species, board width, and grain. Replacement plus refinish beats fighting a hopeless stain with successive sandings that thin the whole floor.
Will a dark stain color really hide black marks?
It folds them into the color rather than erasing them — under a $6.50/sq ft dark stain refinish, residual shadows read as natural variation instead of damage. It's the honest choice when a floor has many stains and replacement would mean rebuilding half of it. The trade is that the whole floor goes darker to hide a few offenders.
How much does fixing a black stain cost?
It rides on a refinish rather than being priced alone: $3.99/sq ft for a natural sand and refinish (500 sq ft minimum), $6.50 with stain, plus board replacement if the damage runs deep — quoted per repair once we see the floor open. Spot treatments like oxalic acid are part of the refinish work, not a separate line.
