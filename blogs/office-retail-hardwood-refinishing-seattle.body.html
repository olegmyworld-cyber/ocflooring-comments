What a Commercial Floor Goes Through That a House Never Does

A desk chair is a grinding wheel with a person on it. Hard casters, eight hours a day, five days a week, orbiting the same three-foot radius — that’s more concentrated abrasion than a family delivers to a whole living room in a year. Multiply by however many chairs are in the room and you get the classic office floor: pristine under the credenzas, matte and gray in a scatter of circles.

Retail wears differently. The abrasive isn’t the shoes, it’s what the shoes carry: sidewalk grit, and between October and May in Seattle, wet sidewalk grit, which is worse because water carries the fines under the mat line and holds them there. An entry that sees a few hundred people a day is being sanded, gently and continuously, by its own customers — which is why the failure is a lane rather than a field, and why spot-fixing a worn traffic path is a question we get from building managers as often as from homeowners. Add point loads from display fixtures, safes, and lateral files, which dent hardwood and also mask wear, and a store that resets its plan every season ends up with a checkerboard of protected squares and abraded aisles.

Then there’s the maintenance program, which cuts both ways. A good janitorial crew extends a commercial floor’s life dramatically. A crew running the wrong chemical — anything oil-soap based, anything that leaves a film — builds a haze no recoat will bond to reliably, and it has to come off before finish goes down. If a floor in your space looks dull and feels faintly tacky underfoot, that’s usually not worn finish at all; it’s residue from cleaning products, and it’s the cheapest problem on this page to fix. Our list of what to hand a cleaning crew and what to throw out is worth forwarding to whoever holds the janitorial contract.

Recoat on a Cycle, or Pay to Sand

Here is the entire commercial hardwood strategy in one sentence: a recoat needs finish left to abrade into. Screening a floor means scuffing the existing coating so a fresh one grips it. If the traffic lane has gone through to raw wood — and on a retail entry it will, faster than anyone expects — there is nothing left to bond to in exactly the spot that matters most, and a recoat over it will read as a patch and fail there first. At that point the honest answer is a full sand, and the price moves from $1.99 to $3.99 per square foot, and the closure from one day to three to five. We walk through the diagnosis in detail in buff and coat vs. full sand, and the strip-versus-screen question in screen and recoat vs. chemical abrasion.

What cadence, then? We won’t pretend there’s a universal number — traffic counts vary too much — but the pattern is consistent enough to plan around. A busy retail entry wants attention every year or two; general office space with chair mats under the desks tends to run three to five years; a showroom or a church hall used two days a week goes longer still. The way to set it is to look at the finish in the worst lane once a year. Sheen gone but wood still sealed means you are exactly on time. Raw wood showing means you are late, and the invoice already changed.

The arithmetic isn’t subtle. Three recoats over a decade at $1.99 cost less than one full sand at $3.99, buy you three one-day closures instead of a week-long one, and — critically — never spend any of the wood. Every full sand takes thickness off a floor that only has so much to give. We laid out the cost of delay in is recoating worth it, or is delaying cheaper; on commercial floors the same math runs harder, because the closure is revenue, not inconvenience.

What Actually Fits in a Closed Weekend

The constraint that decides a commercial schedule isn’t when people can walk on the finish — with the waterborne systems we use, that’s the same evening in socks. It’s when the furniture can come back: 48–72 hours before desks, fixtures, and rolling loads go back down, and about seven days before the finish reaches full cure. A Saturday recoat is walkable Saturday night, ready for the movers Monday morning, and fully hardened by the following weekend. Plan the furniture, not the footsteps.

Phasing: How to Refinish a Floor You Can’t Close

Not every space gets a dark weekend. Clinics run six days, open-plan offices have teams who cannot lose their desks, and a retail lease may simply forbid closing. The answer is zones: we tape a hard boundary, work one area at a time, and hand it back before opening the next. Waterborne chemistry is what makes this tolerable — coats stack in the same day, and the odor is low enough that adjacent occupied space stays usable, which is not remotely true of oil-modified polyurethane at 450–550 g/L with its 30-day cure. That product has no business in an occupied commercial building.

Phasing costs something, and we’d rather say so than surprise you: more mobilizations, more masking, more edge work where two zones meet, and a longer calendar even though no single day is disruptive. What it doesn’t change is the square-foot rate — $1.99 for a recoat is $1.99 whether it happens in one push or five — so we put the number of mobilizations in writing instead of burying it. Where the space shares air handling with other tenants, we price containment properly rather than hope: sealing returns and running negative air is a $250 line item and the reason the suite next door doesn’t call the building manager. Who Signs the Invoice: Landlord, Tenant, and the Lease

We don’t read leases, and we’re not going to pretend to. But we have written the same estimate three different ways often enough to describe the patterns honestly. Between tenants, base-building floor restoration usually falls to the landlord, and the work is easiest — an empty suite, no phasing, a full sand if the floor needs it. In a build-out, the floor comes out of the tenant improvement allowance and gets scheduled against every other trade, which mostly means: last. Mid-lease, a tenant refreshing their own space typically pays, and often needs written landlord sign-off on the scope — including, sometimes, on the finish product itself.

Two pieces of advice that cost nothing. First, get the finish specified in writing, by name. A cheap recoat with an unnamed product is how a floor ends up with an unknown coating that the next contractor can’t bond to — and that’s a full sand nobody budgeted. We spell out what we’re putting down: Bona and Pallmann waterborne systems, two to three coats, chosen by traffic level. Second, whoever is paying should hold the workmanship warranty; ours runs a year, and it’s worth almost nothing to a party that will have vacated the suite by then. Working in Seattle Buildings, Specifically

Seattle’s commercial floors sort into roughly three eras, and each one changes the estimate. In Pioneer Square, along the waterfront, and in the older Ballard and Georgetown blocks, you get brick-and-timber buildings with old-growth fir — beautiful, dense, tight-grained, and often thin, because a century of tenants means a century of sandings. Before we promise anyone a full sand on a floor like that we check remaining thickness at the boards and at the nail line, because fir at 660 on the Janka scale dents easily and there may not be a next refinish left in it. Sometimes the right answer on a historic floor is a recoat and honest expectations about the scars, which we get into in our piece on old-growth fir in Ballard and Queen Anne.

Second era: the Capitol Hill, Fremont, and West Seattle storefronts converted out of houses and small mid-century commercial buildings — oak, usually, sometimes over questionable subfloor patches where a wall used to be. These are the jobs where we spend real time on transitions and repairs before anybody turns on a sander.

Third: South Lake Union, Belltown, and the newer downtown fit-outs, where the “hardwood” is frequently engineered with a thin wear layer and the honest answer may be that it can be recoated but never sanded. We measure before we quote. Downtown also brings its own logistics — freight elevator reservations, certificates of insurance filed with building management, after-hours access badges, loading zones that need permits on some blocks — which we handle as a matter of course, and which is part of why commercial estimates take a walkthrough rather than a phone call. The Call, Compressed

A recoat is enough when…

  • The finish is dull and scratched but the wood underneath is still sealed
  • Wear is concentrated in lanes and chair circles, not across the whole field
  • There are no deep gouges, pet or water stains, or boards to replace
  • Nobody has waxed, oil-soaped, or acrylic-coated the floor since the last refinish
  • You want the space back in a weekend, at $1.99/sq ft

It needs a full sand when…

  • Raw, gray wood shows in the entry lane or under the chairs
  • The finish is peeling or flaking — a coat over failing film fails with it
  • Boards are being replaced or a wall was removed and the floor patched
  • The color is changing: stain, sun fade, or a previous refinish gone uneven
  • You’re changing the color at all — that’s $6.50/sq ft with stain, not a recoat

Office & Retail Refinishing: What Managers Ask Us

Can you work nights and weekends so we don’t close?
Yes — most of our commercial work happens outside business hours or in phased zones. A screen and recoat started Friday evening is walkable that night and ready for furniture Monday morning. For spaces that can never fully close, we work one taped-off zone at a time and hand each back after about 48 hours.
How long before desks, fixtures, and rolling carts can go back?
48 to 72 hours after the final coat. Foot traffic in socks is fine the same evening, and shoes the next day, but rolling loads and heavy point loads need the film to harden first. Full cure is about seven days, which is when you should also reinstall any mats or chair mats.
Will the whole building smell like a floor job?
Not with what we use. Waterborne finishes like Bona Traffic HD run around 65 g/L VOC and are GREENGUARD Gold certified — low enough that adjacent occupied suites usually notice sanding noise long before they notice odor. Oil-modified polyurethane at 450 to 550 g/L is a different story, which is why we don't put it in occupied commercial buildings.
Our floor is engineered. Can it be refinished?
Sometimes. Engineered flooring can be recoated almost always, but it can only be sanded if the wear layer above the tongue is thick enough — and many commercial-grade engineered products are not. We measure at a vent or a threshold before quoting, and we'll tell you plainly if recoating is the only move you get.
How much of the floor do you need cleared?
All of it, for the areas being coated — including under desks and fixtures, because finish has to be continuous. Who does the clearing is a line item in the estimate. Some clients handle it with their own crew over a Friday afternoon; for others we quote the labor. Either way it should be in writing before the schedule locks.
Do you carry the insurance our building management will ask for?
Yes — we're licensed and insured, and we file certificates of insurance with building management as a normal part of commercial scheduling, along with freight elevator reservations and after-hours access arrangements. Send us your building's requirements with the walkthrough request and we'll have it handled before day one.
How often should a retail floor be recoated?
Faster than most owners expect. A busy entry lane often wants a recoat every year or two, while general office space with chair mats tends to run three to five years. The reliable test is annual: look at the worst lane, and if the sheen is gone but the wood is still sealed, book it. Once raw wood shows, the recoat option is gone.
Is there really no price difference between commercial and residential?
Our square-foot rates are the same: $1.99 for a screen and recoat, $3.99 natural, $6.50 with stain. What differs is everything around the rate — mobilizations for phased work, furniture handling, containment, off-hours access. We quote those as visible line items rather than folding them into an inflated per-foot number.
