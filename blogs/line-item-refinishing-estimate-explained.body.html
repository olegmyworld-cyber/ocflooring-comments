Quick answer

A refinishing estimate for a Lynnwood home is really just four things: a rate per square foot for the service level, a measured area, a per-tread number for stairs, and a short list of named add-ons. Ours reads $1.99/sq ft for a screen and recoat, $3.99 for a full sand with natural finish, $6.50 if it’s being stained, $55–$75 per stair tread, $160 to move a washer/dryer pair and $80 for other appliances, and $250 for dust containment. Everything else — the sanding sequence, the flattening pass, two to three coats, masking, the final clean — is inside the square-foot rate, not extra. If a line on your estimate doesn’t fit that shape, ask what it is.

Why We Write It Line by Line

Two reasons, and only one of them is about transparency. The first is that a line-item estimate makes the scope arguable in a good way: if you don’t want dust containment, you can see exactly what removing it saves. If you decide to do the stairs next year, we strike a line and the number moves by an amount you can see. Lump-sum quotes make every change a renegotiation.

The second is that it protects both of us on change orders. When we open a closet and find a black stain from an old washing machine leak, the conversation is “here’s an added line and here’s what it costs,” not “the job is more now.” We’ve written about the specific surprises that generate those calls in our guide to the hidden costs in refinishing quotes — the short version is that almost all of them are foreseeable at the estimate if somebody bothers to look.

The Base Rate, and What Hides Inside It

The per-square-foot number is the single biggest line, and it is where quotes diverge most, because it is the only line where a contractor can cut scope invisibly. Ours covers a complete service, not a machine pass. At $3.99/sq ft for a full sand with natural finish, that includes: the full sanding sequence in graduated grits rather than one aggressive pass; hand and edge work along walls, in corners, around registers and door casings; a Lägler TRIO multi-disc pass to flatten the surface and erase machine tracks before coating; two to three coats of Bona or Pallmann waterborne with abrasion between coats; masking and protection of baseboards and adjacent finishes; and a final clean and walkthrough. None of those is a separate line, because none of them is optional.

That is why a $2 per square foot full-refinish quote should worry you rather than delight you. At that rate somebody is skipping grits, skipping the flattening pass, running one coat instead of three, or using bargain single-component finish — and you won’t know which until year two. We ran the comparison properly in what separates a $2 quote from a $6 quote and in when the cheap refinishing quote ends up costing more. Our $1.99 line does exist — it’s just for a screen and recoat, which is one day of work on a floor whose finish is still intact.

How the Square Footage Gets Measured

This is the line most likely to differ between two honest estimates, and it’s worth asking about directly. We measure the finished floor area we will actually sand and coat. That means closets count if they’re getting done and don’t if they aren’t. It means the floor under a refrigerator or a range counts, because we finish under there. It means the footprint of fixed cabinetry does not count, because we don’t sand under permanent cabinets — a separate question we cover in refinishing under kitchen cabinets. Hallways, doorway thresholds and the landing at the top of the stairs all count as floor.

Two practical notes. First, contractors who measure off a Zillow square footage figure are measuring your whole house, including bathrooms, garage and unfinished space — that number is always bigger than your wood floor area, and a quote built on it is either padded or about to be revised. Second, if your job comes in under 500 square feet, the honest thing is a minimum rather than a per-foot fiction; we explain how we handle those in minimum job sizes and small refinishing jobs.

The Add-Ons, and Why Each One Exists

Stairs, $55–$75 per tread. Stairs cost more per square foot than any floor in the house because almost none of the work is machine work: each tread is hand-sanded, the nosing is shaped by hand, risers and stringers get detailed separately, and the coating schedule has to leave a path for the household to get upstairs. The spread between $55 and $75 is about the stair itself — open risers, returns, curved treads and spindles all push toward the top of the range. We break the whole calculation down in stair refinishing cost per step.

Appliances, $160 for a washer/dryer pair and $80 each for others. These are disconnect-and-move charges, not a surcharge. A stacked or pedestal laundry pair has to be uncoupled, walked out, and reset over freshly coated wood after cure — that’s real labor and real liability. If you move them yourself before we arrive, the line comes off.

Dust containment, $250. Plastic barriers at openings, sealed and filtered returns, negative-pressure management between the work area and the rest of the house. We recommend it whenever you’re staying in the home or when part of the house has to stay usable, and we don’t pretend it’s free. The mechanics are in our piece on HVAC and dust control during refinishing.

Repairs, board replacement and transitions are quoted per situation rather than by a rate, because a water-damaged section under a dishwasher and a single split board are not the same job. What they have in common is that we’d rather find them at the estimate than on day two.

Where These Lines Land in Lynnwood Homes

Lynnwood’s housing stock makes some of these lines much more predictable than they’d be elsewhere. The neighborhoods that filled in between the 1960s and the early 1980s — around Alderwood, Meadowdale, Martha Lake and the older grid west of 44th — are dominated by split-levels, tri-levels, and daylight ramblers, and they share a floor plan logic: red oak strip on the main and upper levels, a stair run of ten to fourteen treads to the lower level, carpet or vinyl below, and a laundry closet somewhere in the middle of the wood.

What that means practically is that the stair line is almost never zero here, and it’s the line most often missing from a competitor’s quote — a 13-tread run at $65 is $845, which is fifteen percent of the job on our sample estimate. It also means the washer/dryer line comes up more often than it would in a rambler with a garage laundry. And because these houses were built with the wood ending at the top of the stairs, the transition detail at the landing gets its own attention; we get into that in our piece on flooring transitions between rooms.

Split-levels also tend to be occupied during the work — there’s a whole lower level to retreat to — which is why the containment line shows up on most of our Lynnwood estimates rather than a few. Lines that should be on any honest estimate

  • A named service level — recoat, natural refinish, or stain refinish
  • The measured square footage, and what was and wasn’t included in it
  • Stairs counted by tread, with the tread count written down
  • Named add-ons: appliances, containment, repairs, transitions
  • Timeline, cure milestones, deposit terms and the warranty

Lines that should prompt a question

  • “Misc.” or “materials” as a lump with no explanation
  • A total with no square footage behind it
  • No stair line on a house with stairs — it will reappear later
  • A full refinish priced near $2/sq ft — ask what’s being skipped
  • A deposit over about a third of the job before anything is on site

Reading a Refinishing Estimate: Common Questions

Why is the estimate a rate per square foot instead of a flat price?
Because sanding and coating scale almost linearly with area, so a rate is the honest unit for the biggest part of the job. What doesn't scale — mobilization, stairs, containment, appliance moves — gets its own line instead of being smeared into the rate. That's why the two parts are separated rather than blended into one number.
Does your square footage include closets and under the appliances?
Closets count if we're finishing them, and we'll ask. The floor under a refrigerator, range or washer counts, because we finish under there rather than stopping at the appliance. Fixed cabinetry doesn't count, because we don't sand under permanent cabinets.
What's actually included in the base rate?
The full sanding sequence in graduated grits, hand and edge work, a Lägler TRIO flattening pass, two to three coats of Bona or Pallmann waterborne with abrasion between coats, baseboard and casing protection, and a final clean and walkthrough. Those are the parts that a very low quote is usually cutting.
Why are stairs priced per tread instead of per square foot?
Because they're hand work. A tread's surface area is small but the labor per tread is high — sanding, shaping the nosing, detailing risers and stringers, and coating on a schedule that still lets the household get upstairs. $55 to $75 per tread reflects the stair's complexity; open risers, returns and spindles land at the top of the range.
Is dust containment optional?
Technically yes, and it's a separate $250 line specifically so you can decide. We recommend it whenever the household is staying in the home or when a room has to stay usable, because sanding dust travels through return ducts to every corner of a house. In an empty home about to be painted, it's often reasonable to skip.
What deposit should I expect?
A deposit in the neighborhood of a third of the job is normal and reasonable; it covers scheduling and materials. A demand for most or all of the money before anything is on site is not, and it's the most common warning sign we hear about. We wrote a whole piece on what a refinishing deposit should cover.
If two estimates are far apart, what should I compare first?
Three things, in order: the service level, the measured area, and whether stairs are included. Most large gaps we see turn out to be a recoat priced against a full refinish, a square footage measured two different ways, or a missing stair line — not a genuine difference in what the work is worth.
Can the total change once you start?
It can, and the honest answer is that it does on a minority of jobs — almost always because something was hidden under furniture, an appliance or a rug. When that happens it comes to you as a named added line with a price before we proceed, never as a bigger number at the end. Finding those at the estimate is exactly why we measure in person.
