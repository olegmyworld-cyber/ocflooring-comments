Quick answer

Refinishing means sanding the floor down to bare wood and building a new finish on it. Resurfacing is a marketing word with no fixed definition — most often it means a screen & recoat or a chemical-bond system that never touches bare wood. Restoration means repair work first — boards, filler, water damage, patched additions — and then refinishing. The confusion costs real money, because a $1.99/sq ft recoat and a $3.99/sq ft full sand are both advertised as “resurfacing” in this market. One question clears the fog: does the sander go down to bare wood?

Refinishing: The One Word That Means Something Specific

Refinishing is sanding the floor to bare wood and building a new finish on it. In practice that’s a coarse cut to get under the old finish and level the boards, two or three finer grits to erase the previous scratch pattern, an edger where the big machine can’t reach, hand scraping in corners, and a Lägler TRIO final pass to blend the perimeter into the field so the room reads flat under low winter light. Then sealer and two to three coats of finish — we run Bona and Pallmann waterborne systems, which recoat in 2–3 hours instead of overnight.

Everything above the wood goes: old finish, old stain, sun fade, surface scratches, and pet stains that never got past the finish layer. That’s why refinishing is the only one of these three processes that can change the color of your floor or take out damage you can feel with a fingernail. It’s also the only one that spends your floor’s finite sanding thickness, which is why nobody should do it more often than needed — the full walkthrough of what those days look like is in our refinishing process timeline. On our price list it’s $3.99/sq ft natural, $6.50/sq ft with stain, 500 sq ft minimum, typically 3–5 days from first cut to last coat.

Resurfacing: A Word Without a Fixed Definition

No trade standard defines “resurfacing.” In the field it gets used three ways. Sometimes it’s a straight synonym for refinishing, chosen by a company that thinks it sounds less alarming than “we’re going to sand your house for three days.” Sometimes it means a screen & recoat: buff the existing finish with an abrasive pad, vacuum, and lay one new coat — $1.99/sq ft, one day, socks that evening. And sometimes it means a chemical-abrasion system, the “sandless” category, where a bonding liquid takes the place of the buffer before the new coat goes down.

All three are legitimate work. The problem is the gap between what the word promises and what the buyer pictures. A homeowner with gouged, sun-faded, twenty-year-old oak who buys “resurfacing” at $2/sq ft usually pictures a floor that looks new and receives a floor that looks glossy with all the same scratches under a fresh coat — because no recoat removes anything below the finish film. We wrote the honest version of both in does sandless refinishing actually work and screen and recoat vs chemical abrasion systems. Short answer: a recoat is a great deal on a tired-but-intact finish and a waste of money on a damaged one, and the deciding factor is whether your damage is in the finish or in the wood — which is exactly the line we drew in deep vs surface scratches.

Restoration: Everything That Has to Happen Before the Sanding Starts

We use “restoration” for floors where sanding is the last third of the job. A 1912 Seattle fir floor with a hundred years of face-nailing, three plumbing patches in mismatched lumber, and a doorway where a wall used to be. A board field that buckled after a dishwasher line let go. A dining room where somebody’s previous refinisher stopped at 36 grit and called it done. The work is board replacement matched for species, width, and grain direction; filler and lacing where a patch meets original material; sometimes flattening a floor that has moved with the house — and only then the sanding and finishing everyone was picturing from the start.

Restoration doesn’t price cleanly per square foot, because the repair hours drive the number and they vary by an order of magnitude between floors. We quote it as repair labor plus materials plus the refinishing rate, itemized, so you can see which dollars bought new wood and which bought finish. The related reading here is what sanding can’t hide and buckled hardwood after a leak. And the honest warning: “restoration” is also the word some contractors reach for to justify a premium on an ordinary refinish. If a quote says restoration, the estimate should name the boards, the square footage of repair, and the reason — not just carry a bigger number.

How to Read a Quote That Uses the Wrong Word

Ignore the noun at the top of the estimate and read for four things. First, the grit sequence. A real sand names grits — something like 36, 60, 80, then the TRIO pass. A recoat quote can’t name them because there aren’t any. Second, the finish product and coat count. “Two coats of commercial-grade polyurethane” is not a product; “Bona Traffic HD, sealer plus two coats” is. Grade matters more than base — that’s the argument we laid out in water-based vs oil-based finish. Third, edges, closets, and stairs. Stairs are priced per tread — ours run $55–$75 — and a quote that folds them into a square-foot number is either guessing or excluding them. Fourth, what happens to the stuff in the room: appliances, base shoe, dust containment. Those are the line items that turn a low bid into a mid bid on the last day.

None of this requires knowing flooring. It requires making all three quotes answer the same questions, at which point the cheap one is either genuinely cheaper for the same work — it happens — or selling a different job. Our breakdowns of the costs that hide in refinishing quotes and what each line of an estimate is actually paying for go item by item.

  • “Does this sand to bare wood, or coat over the existing finish?”
  • “What grit sequence, and does the perimeter get the same treatment as the field?”
  • “Which finish product, how many coats, and what’s the VOC content?”
  • “Are stairs, closets, and the base shoe in this number or not?”
  • “What’s the total, including appliances, containment, and the 500 sq ft minimum?”

Phrases that should slow you down

  • “Resurfacing” with no description of what physically happens to the floor
  • “Restoration” with no boards, square footage, or repair scope named
  • “Commercial-grade finish” without a brand and product name on the estimate
  • “No sanding, no dust, same result” — pick any two, not all three
  • A per-square-foot price with no minimum, no stair line, and no appliance note

How the Three Words Land Around King and Snohomish County

Geography sorts these jobs more neatly than you’d expect. In Seattle’s pre-war stock — Ballard, Wallingford, the Craftsman belt — the word is usually restoration, because old-growth fir has been patched, plumbed through, and covered in carpet tack strips for eighty years, and the sanding is the easy part; that’s the everyday reality of hardwood floor refinishing in Seattle. On the Eastside, in the 1990s and 2000s red oak that fills Sammamish, Redmond, and Bellevue, the honest answer is often the cheap one: the boards are fine, the finish is just scuffed in the traffic lanes, and a $1.99/sq ft screen & recoat buys another five to seven years. In rental-heavy pockets of Everett and Lynnwood, it’s usually a straight refinish between tenants — nobody’s restoring anything, they’re resetting a floor to a durable baseline.

We’ve been doing this since 2013, past a thousand floors, and the pattern holds: the right word is a description of your floor’s condition, not a product tier. That’s also why we won’t sell a full sand to somebody whose floor needs a recoat — it spends sanding life that floor will want in fifteen years. Refinishing, Resurfacing, Restoration: What Homeowners Ask

Is resurfacing the same thing as refinishing?
Not reliably. Refinishing has a fixed meaning — sanding to bare wood and applying new finish. Resurfacing is a marketing term that different companies use for refinishing, for a screen and recoat, or for a chemical-bond sandless system. Always ask whether the process reaches bare wood before comparing prices.
Which one do I need if my floors have deep scratches?
Refinishing. Scratches you can catch with a fingernail are in the wood, not in the finish, so any process that coats over the existing surface will leave them visible under a shinier film. Sanding is the only way to remove them, within the floor's remaining thickness.
Which one do I need if my floors are just dull?
A screen and recoat, at $1.99 per square foot and one day of work. If the finish is worn but unbroken and there are no gray or bare patches, abrading and recoating restores the sheen and adds years of protection for half the price of a full sand.
Can you resurface a floor that has bare or gray spots?
No. Once wear has gone through the finish into the wood, a new coat over the top seals in the discoloration and the boundary between worn and unworn areas stays visible. Bare spots mean full sanding, or spot repair followed by full sanding.
Why do restoration quotes vary so much?
Because repair hours vary enormously and finishing hours don't. Two 900-square-foot floors can need the same three days of sanding and finishing but differ by a week in board replacement, lacing, and flattening work. That's why we itemize repair separately instead of burying it in a square-foot rate.
Does refinishing wear out the floor?
Every full sand removes a small amount of wood, so a solid hardwood floor has a finite number of them — typically several over its lifetime, depending on original thickness and how much has already been taken off. That's the real argument for recoating when a recoat will do, and for hiring someone who takes off the minimum needed.
How long does each option take?
A screen and recoat is one day, walkable in socks that evening. A full refinish is 3 to 5 days on site with furniture back at 48 to 72 hours and rugs at about a week. Restoration adds repair days on the front end, so plan on a week or more depending on scope.
What do you call the job when you write our estimate?
We describe the physical work rather than the category: sanding sequence, finish product and coats, repair scope, stairs per tread, and the extras. The heading matters much less than the line items, and you should be able to hand our estimate and a competitor's to a neighbor who could tell they're the same job or not.
