What a Sanding Actually Removes

Picture the sequence: a coarse cut to take off the old finish and level the surface, one or two intermediate grits to remove the scratch pattern from the cut before it, and a fine final pass. Add it up and you’ve taken off a thin skin of wood — enough to reach fresh material, deliberately not more, because every sanding spends wood you can never put back. That budget is why we care so much about how many times a floor can be refinished.

Which means depth is the whole story. Anything that lives in that top skin — surface scratches, dull finish, gray dirt-stained fiber in the traffic lanes, minor sun fade — genuinely disappears. Anything that goes deeper stays. A crew that sands harder to chase a gouge isn’t being thorough, they’re dishing the floor around the defect and shortening its life for a mark that will still be visible.

The clean test at home: run a fingernail across the mark. If it catches, sanding alone won’t remove it. If it doesn’t, it’s probably gone after we’re done. Our guide to deep versus surface scratches goes further, but that one test settles most of them.

Filler Is a Compromise, Not a Repair

Wood filler is a real tool and we use it on nearly every job. It’s also the single most oversold item in refinishing, so here’s what it actually does.

The standard approach on a full sand is a trowel filler — a paste, sometimes mixed with sanding dust from your own floor so the color lands close — skimmed across the floor to fill nail holes, small checks, and hairline gaps, then sanded flush. Used that way it’s excellent: it makes a hundred small imperfections stop reading as damage, and because it’s taking the color of your wood’s own dust it blends better than anything out of a tube.

The limits are worth knowing. Filler takes stain differently than wood does. It has no grain and no pores, so it either resists color and reads light or drinks it and reads dark — either way, filled spots become visible dots the moment a stain goes down. On a natural finish they’re subtle; under a dark walnut stain they can look like a constellation. Filler shrinks. A wide gap or a deep hole packed with paste will sink slightly as it dries and can crack or pop out later, especially in gaps that open and close with the seasons — which is most of them, and why our piece on gaps in hardwood floors argues against filling the moving ones. And filler is not structural. A chunk missing from a board edge, a split, a hole from a plumbing run: those want wood, not paste.

Fill or Replace

Fill it

  • Nail holes and face-nail lines in old fir or oak
  • Small checks and hairline cracks in the boards
  • Narrow, stable gaps between boards
  • Shallow chips at board edges
  • Perimeter tack-strip holes that baseboards won’t cover

Replace the board instead

  • Gouges deep enough to lose a fingernail in
  • Black urine stains that go through the board
  • Burns, charring, or a hole from an old floor register
  • Split, cracked, or crushed boards
  • Anything wider than a pencil that has to look right, not just be filled

Board Replacement: The Real Fix, and Its Own Compromise

When a board is beyond filling, the answer is lacing in a new one: cut the damaged board out, work in a replacement of the same species and dimension, and weave the end joints so the seam doesn’t line up as an obvious rectangle. Done well, the repair disappears into the floor after the sanding and finishing — and this is why lacing happens before the sand, never after. A board laced into an already-finished floor announces itself forever.

The honest caveats: matching old wood is hard. A board milled last year is a different age, often a different cut, and sometimes a different growth pattern than a board milled in 1948 — you’ll get very close on a stained floor and slightly less close on a natural one. Old-growth fir in particular is nearly impossible to match with new material, which is why we salvage boards from a closet or under an appliance when a Renton house has spare floor hidden somewhere. And a laced board is a different piece of wood, so it will take stain a little differently; the same mechanism that makes a whole floor read differently room to room, which we cover in why a refinished floor looks different room to room.

Pricing: lacing is carpentry, so it’s quoted by the repair rather than by the square foot, and it’s in addition to the $3.99/sq ft sand and finish. Two or three boards is a small line item. Twenty boards is a conversation about whether the floor is worth saving at all — which is the question our guide to refinishing old hardwood floors tries to answer honestly.

What We Find in Renton Floors

Renton gives us two recurring versions of this problem. The first is the Highlands and the wartime and immediate-postwar housing built for Boeing workers — small, sturdy houses from the 1940s and early 50s, many with fir floors that were face-nailed rather than blind-nailed. Face nails mean visible rows of filled nail heads running down every board, and those holes never go away; they get filled, sanded flush, and become part of the floor’s character. Homeowners who expect a seamless surface are surprised by them; homeowners who understand they’re original construction usually end up liking them.

The second is the carpet story, and it’s everywhere in the Boeing-era ramblers south and east of the Highlands. Oak or fir went in, wall-to-wall carpet went over it in the 60s or 70s, and when that carpet comes up you get a perimeter band of tack-strip nail holes plus a scattering of staple holes from the pad. Good news: most tack-strip damage is within an inch or two of the wall and disappears under baseboard and shoe molding. Less good: staples pulled at an angle can tear small chunks out of the wood, and those need filling. That whole assessment is part of how we quote hardwood floor refinishing in Renton — we look at the perimeter before we quote the field.

The third thing we find in these houses, less often but more expensively, is pet damage in one specific room, usually near a door. Black staining from urine goes deep into the wood and rarely sands out — the full diagnosis is in can black stains be sanded out — and the fix is board replacement in that area. It’s worth knowing before the estimate rather than after the sanding.

Your Color Choice Decides How Much of This Shows

Here’s a lever most homeowners don’t know they have. A natural, no-stain waterborne finish is the most forgiving thing you can put on a repaired floor: filler that was mixed with your own sanding dust lands close to the wood color, laced boards blend into the natural variation of the species, and nothing gets emphasized. That’s the $3.99/sq ft option.

A dark stain does the opposite. It exaggerates every difference in how material absorbs color — filled spots, new boards, and old repairs all become more visible, not less. Dark floors also show dust and every future scratch as a light line. If your floor has a lot of history in it, and you’re choosing between a rich stain and a natural finish, that history is a real argument for natural; our guide to choosing a stain color walks through the rest of the tradeoffs. Sheen matters a little too: satin hides surface irregularity better than semi-gloss, which throws raking light across every low spot.

And one plain expectation-setter for anyone comparing quotes: a floor that has been through eighty years of life will look wonderful after a refinish, and it will not look like a factory-finished new floor. If that’s what you want, you want a new floor, and we’ll price that honestly too. Most people, standing in the room on the last day, are glad they kept the old one.

Holes, Gouges, and Filler: What Renton Homeowners Ask Us

Will sanding remove the nail holes in my floor?
No. A full sand removes roughly a thirty-second of an inch of wood, and a nail hole goes all the way through the board. We fill them, sand the filler flush, and they become small, subtle marks rather than open holes. On face-nailed fir floors — common in older Renton houses — those filled rows are original to how the floor was built.
How deep a scratch will sanding take out?
Anything shallower than the material we remove, which in practice means anything your fingernail doesn't catch on. Run a nail across the mark: if it snags, it will still be visible after sanding, though usually shallower and less noticeable. If it glides over, it's almost certainly gone.
Does wood filler take stain the same as the wood?
No, and this is the main thing to know before choosing a dark color. Filler has no grain or pores, so it either resists stain and reads lighter or absorbs it and reads darker. Filled spots that are nearly invisible under a natural finish can look like scattered dots under a dark stain. Mixing filler with your floor's own sanding dust helps, but it doesn't eliminate the difference.
Should gaps between boards be filled?
Narrow, stable gaps, yes. Wide ones that open and close with the seasons, no — filler shrinks, cracks, and eventually pops out, leaving you worse off than before. For genuinely large gaps in an old floor, slivers of matching wood glued in are the durable answer, and sometimes the right call is to accept the gaps as part of an old floor.
When do you replace a board instead of filling it?
When the damage is deeper than filler can honestly handle: gouges you can lose a fingernail in, black urine staining, burns, splits, and holes left by old registers or plumbing. Replacement always happens before the sanding, never after, so the new board gets sanded and finished with everything else and blends in.
Can you match a replacement board to my old floor?
Close, usually. Species and dimension we can match; age and grain pattern are harder, and old-growth fir is the hardest of all — new fir simply doesn't have the same tight rings. When a house has spare original flooring hidden in a closet or under an appliance, that's the best source and we'll use it. Expect very good rather than invisible.
Does repair work cost extra on top of the refinish?
Yes. The $3.99 per square foot natural refinish includes normal filling of nail holes and small checks. Board replacement is carpentry and gets quoted per repair, because two boards and twenty boards are completely different jobs. We'll mark and price them at the estimate rather than discovering them mid-job.
My floor was under carpet for forty years. What should I expect?
A band of tack-strip nail holes around the perimeter, staple holes scattered across the field, and — usually — hardwood in better shape than you feared, because carpet protected it from wear and sun. Most perimeter damage ends up hidden under baseboard and shoe molding. The staple holes get filled. It's one of the better surprises in this trade.
