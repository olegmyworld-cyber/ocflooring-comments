Quick answer

Cupping — boards sitting higher at their edges than their centers, so the floor feels like a washboard — is a moisture problem, not a finish problem: the bottom of each board is wetter than the top, almost always from below. Refinishing can absolutely erase cupping, but only in the right order: find the moisture source, fix it, let the floor dry back to flat or nearly flat, verify with a moisture meter, and then sand. Sand a still-wet cupped floor and you trade cupping for crowning — a worse problem you paid for. What Cupping Is, Mechanically

A wood floor board is a sponge with a finish on top. The finished top face takes on and gives off moisture slowly; the raw bottom face, sitting over your crawlspace or slab, exchanges moisture quickly. When the air (or worse, liquid water) under the floor is wetter than the air above it, the bottom of each board swells while the top doesn’t. The bottom gets wider than the top, and the only shape a board can make with a wide bottom and a narrow top is a shallow trough — edges up, center down. Multiply by every board in the room and you get the washboard. The wider the board, the more dramatic the cup, which is one reason wide-plank floors are less forgiving of moisture swings than old 2¼-inch strip.

The direction of the cup tells you where the water is. Edges up means wet below — the overwhelming majority of cases. The reverse shape (center humped up, called crowning) means either wet from above… or, more often in our experience, someone sanded a cupped floor flat while it was still wet, and then the boards finished drying and pulled the sanded-flat surface into a dome. That second kind of crowning is permanent until the next full refinish, which is exactly why the timing rules below matter.

The Usual Suspects, Everett Edition

In our service area, cupping traces back to a short list. Crawlspace moisture is the big one: bare or poorly covered earth under the house breathing water vapor into the subfloor all winter. Plumbing and appliance leaks — dishwashers, fridge lines, washing machines — soak the subfloor quietly and cup the boards above; if that’s your situation, our guide on what to do after water damage covers the first 48 hours. Seasonal humidity swings cup floors gently every winter and release them every summer — the same mechanism that opens gaps between boards in the dry season, running in reverse. And concrete slabs without a working vapor barrier feed moisture upward year-round. The severity ranges from “texture you only feel in socks” to “boards lifting off the subfloor” — and the response should range accordingly.

Why We Won’t Sand It Right Away (Even If You Ask Nicely)

Here’s the trap. A cupped floor that’s still wet is a floor whose boards are temporarily swollen into troughs. Sand it now and the sander happily flattens the surface — by cutting the high edges down. The floor looks great for a month. Then the moisture source you didn’t fix keeps working, or the drying you didn’t wait for completes, the boards shrink back toward their original shape, and every board pulls into a crown: centers high, edges low, because the edges are where the sander removed the wood. You’ve converted a recoverable moisture symptom into permanent missing material, and thinned the floor’s finite sanding budget doing it — wood a floor can only spend a few times in its life.

So the sequence is fixed, and we don’t reorder it for schedule convenience: source, then dry, then measure, then sand. We check moisture content at the top and bottom of the boards; when the two faces read within a couple points of each other and the readings hold steady across a few weeks, the floor is done moving and it’s safe to make dust. Mild cupping often relaxes most of the way flat on its own once the source is gone — some floors need no refinish at all, just patience. The stubborn residual washboard is what the full sand is for, and it removes far less wood from a dried, mostly-flat floor than it would have from a wet, badly cupped one.

What the Refinish Itself Looks Like

Once the floor is verified dry, a cupping correction is a standard full refinish with extra attention on the first cuts: aggressive grit to level the residual ridges, a Lägler TRIO final pass to get the surface truly flat rather than approximately flat, then 2–3 coats of Bona or Pallmann waterborne. Natural finish runs $3.99/sq ft, stain $6.50, and the work takes the usual 3–5 days. Two honest caveats. First: if boards were badly compressed at their edges during the wet phase (the trade calls it compression set), a floor can hold a slight permanent cup that even a careful sanding only mostly hides. Second: fix the humidity story or the cup returns — on crawlspace houses that means the ground cover stays intact, and inside, some effort to keep winter humidity from cratering; our piece on how humidity moves hardwood covers the ranges worth targeting.

Cupped Floor Questions Everett Homeowners Ask Us

Can refinishing fix cupped hardwood floors?
Yes, if the order is right: fix the moisture source, let the floor dry, confirm with moisture meter readings, then sand. A full refinish flattens the residual ridges and refinishes the wood. What refinishing cannot do is fix cupping while the moisture source is still active — the cup simply comes back through the new finish.
Will my cupped floor flatten on its own?
Often, substantially. If the cause was a one-time event or a fixed leak, boards release much of their cup as the moisture gradient equalizes — over weeks to months, not days. Mild seasonal cupping that appears in winter and vanishes in summer needs no repair at all. Whatever washboard remains once the floor is stable is what sanding is for.
Why can't you just sand it flat now?
Because sanding a wet, cupped floor cuts down the swollen edges, and when the boards later dry and shrink back, those cut edges drop and every board crowns — centers high. That crowning is permanent until another full refinish, and you've spent wood from the floor's limited sanding budget to create it. Drying first isn't caution; it's the difference between fixing the floor once and fixing it twice.
How long does the floor need to dry before sanding?
It varies too much for one number — weeks after a modest leak, months after serious soaking, especially through a Western Washington winter. We go by measurement, not the calendar: when the top and bottom of the boards read within a couple points of each other on a moisture meter and hold steady, the floor is ready.
What causes cupping in Everett homes specifically?
Crawlspace moisture is the leading cause — most older Everett homes sit on vented crawlspaces, and marine air off Port Gardner keeps them damp most of the year, especially where the ground vapor barrier is missing or torn. Plumbing and appliance leaks are next, followed by winter indoor humidity swings and slab moisture in basement-level rooms.
Would a screen and recoat help a cupped floor?
No. A screen and recoat ($1.99/sq ft) abrades and recoats the existing surface, following whatever shape it has — it adds fresh finish to a washboard. Flattening requires the full sand. Recoating is the right tool for dull or lightly scratched flat floors, not for shape problems.
What's the difference between cupping and buckling?
Degree, and it matters a lot. Cupping is boards changing shape while staying attached — recoverable. Buckling is boards lifting off the subfloor entirely, usually after major water. Buckled sections generally need board replacement before any refinishing, and sometimes full replacement. If you can slide anything under a board edge, you're past cupping.
Does homeowners insurance cover a cupped floor?
Sometimes — typically when the cupping came from a sudden covered event like a burst pipe or appliance failure, and typically not for long-term crawlspace moisture or maintenance issues.
