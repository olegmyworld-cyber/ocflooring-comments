What Inspections Actually Flag (It’s Not Dullness)

Let’s separate two audiences that sellers tend to merge. A buyer’s inspector is looking for problems, not polish: water staining and rot, cupped or buckled boards suggesting moisture, soft spots, active leaks near appliances. No inspection report has ever dinged a house for satin finish gone dull in the hallway. The buyer, though, walks in the door and prices the floor emotionally within seconds — gray traffic lanes and pet stains read as “this house wasn’t cared for,” and that impression contaminates everything else they see. So the strategy writes itself: fix anything an inspector would flag (that’s repair work, not cosmetics — do it first and properly), then spend the minimum that makes the buyer’s first impression match the house you actually maintained. That minimum is usually one day and $1.99 a square foot.

The Ladder: Four Rungs, Priced

Floor money before a sale comes in four sizes. Deep clean — nearly free, and worth doing regardless, but it can’t fix finish wear; a scratched dull floor is scratched and dull after mopping. Screen and recoat, $1.99/sq ft — the sweet spot: we abrade the existing finish and apply a fresh commercial waterborne coat, erasing dullness and the shallow scratch haze that makes floors look old. Full refinish, $3.99/sq ft natural — sand to bare wood, 2–3 coats, 3–5 days; this is the rung for wear that went through the finish. Replacement — many times the cost, weeks of disruption, and before a sale it almost never returns its price; the buyer would rather have your solid old-growth oak refinished than builder-grade new material anyway. The trap to avoid is paying for a higher rung than your floor needs — or trying to save with a lower rung that can’t actually fix your symptom, which is where the next section comes in.

Why the $1.99 Recoat Is the Seller’s Best Friend

A screen and recoat works because most “worn out” floors aren’t — the wood is fine, and only the plastic wear layer on top has aged. Abrade that layer, bond a fresh coat over it, and the floor regains the even sheen and depth that reads as “well maintained” in listing photos, for about a quarter of what buyers will assume you spent. The honest limits: a recoat renews the finish, not the wood, so it cannot erase gray traffic lanes, stains, or scratches that have reached bare wood — put fresh finish over gray wood and you get shiny gray wood, sealed in. It also needs a compatible, uncontaminated existing finish to bond to; floors that have been heavily waxed or polished with the wrong products can reject a new coat, which is exactly what we check at the estimate. What Not to Spend On (Sellers’ Edition)

Three pre-sale floor spends we routinely talk people out of. Replacement: tearing out sound hardwood to install new flooring before a sale is the classic negative-return renovation — you pay full price, the buyer discounts it to taste. Big stain projects: a trendy color choice is a coin flip on the buyer’s taste; natural finish is the neutral answer and costs $2.51/sq ft less. Covering hardwood with carpet or laminate: Eastside buyers rip that out mentally (then literally) and mark the house down for the mystery underneath — if anything, sellers make money going the other direction, pulling old carpet off intact oak. The unifying principle: before a sale, your floor spend is marketing, not renovation. Marketing wants clean, light, neutral, and cheap — which is a one-day recoat far more often than it’s anything else.

Pre-Listing Floor Questions Kirkland Sellers Ask Us

Will a home inspector fail worn hardwood floors?
No — worn finish isn't a defect, and inspection reports don't grade cosmetics. Inspectors flag damage: water staining, rot, cupping, buckling, loose or soft boards. If your floor has any of those, fix the cause and the damage first. If it's just dull and scratched, the inspection isn't the problem — the buyer's first impression is.
Is a screen and recoat really enough before listing?
If the wear is still in the finish layer, yes — and that describes most floors we look at. One day, $1.99 per square foot, and the floor photographs like new. If wear has gone through to gray wood, a recoat can't fix it and we'll say so at the estimate rather than take your money for the wrong service.
How do we tell if the wear is through the finish?
Look at the traffic lanes in raking light: if the worn areas are grayish or whitish and feel like raw wood, the finish is gone there. Drip a little water on the worst spot — if it soaks in and darkens the wood instead of beading, you're through the finish and into full-refinish territory.
How fast can this happen before our photo date?
A recoat: one day of work, walkable that evening, photo-ready in about two days. A full refinish: 3 to 5 working days, then 48 to 72 hours before furniture and staging go back on. Booked reasonably ahead, either fits a normal listing-prep window — the recoat fits even a panicked one.
Does refinishing floors before selling actually pay back?
In this market, generally yes — refinished original hardwood is a feature Eastside listings advertise in the first line, and worn floors become a negotiating chip that costs sellers more than the work would have. The strongest returns come from the cheapest appropriate fix: a $1,990 recoat that removes a $10,000 mental discount is the best trade in home prep.
Should we replace the floors instead if they're really bad?
Almost never before a sale. Even a badly worn solid hardwood floor is usually a $3.99/sq ft refinish away from excellent, at a fraction of replacement cost. Replacement only makes pre-sale sense when the floor is structurally gone — major water damage, rot — and at that point it's a repair conversation, not a cosmetic one.
What about pet stains — will buyers notice?
Yes, and so will their inspector if the stains suggest moisture. Dark pet stains are in the wood, not the finish, so they need a full refinish, and the deepest ones may need board replacement before sanding. We weave in matching boards and sand everything together so the repair disappears into the new surface.
Do you work around our moving and staging schedule?
Constantly — pre-listing jobs are half our fall calendar. The cleanest sequence is: house empty or mostly empty, floors done, then stagers in after the 48–72 hour furniture window. Tell us your photo date at the estimate and we'll build the schedule backward from it.
