What Rain Actually Threatens — and What It Doesn’t

Start by separating the scary-sounding from the relevant. Rain never touches your floor; what could matter is water vapor — indoor humidity — and the moisture content of the wood itself. Finish drying is an evaporation race, and evaporation slows in humid air. If your living room in January sat at outdoor conditions, that would be a real problem. It doesn’t: a heated Seattle house in winter is actually on the dry side indoors, because warming cold air drops its relative humidity. The same furnace that makes January survivable makes it a perfectly good finishing environment — often better than a muggy August afternoon.

The wood is the other variable. Hardwood swells and shrinks with the seasons, and boards carry more moisture in the wet months — the mechanics we laid out in how humidity affects hardwood floors. For refinishing, this mostly matters at the extremes: a floor that’s actively taking on water — a damp basement level, a crawlspace venting moisture upward, a recent leak — should be diagnosed and dried before anyone sands it, in any season. That’s not a rainy-season rule; it’s the same logic behind why we won’t sand a cupped floor flat until the moisture source is fixed. Rain just makes the diagnosis more common.

Our Rainy-Season Playbook

Three adjustments cover nearly all of it. First, we measure instead of assuming. A moisture meter reads the boards before the sander runs; wood within normal indoor range gets refinished, and wood reading wet gets investigated — because sealing excess moisture under fresh finish is how you buy cupping and clouding later. Second, the house runs at living temperature. Normal heat through the workdays and the nights between coats gives the finish the same stable, mild, reasonably dry environment it would see in June. This is the whole trick, and it costs you a utility bill increment, not a compromise.

Third, we ventilate on the finish’s schedule, not the weather’s. Waterborne finishes don’t need windows thrown open to cure — air movement and exchange are managed around coats, and a rainy afternoon outside is irrelevant to a controlled interior. What we don’t do in the wet months is fight physics with the wrong chemistry: oil-modified polyurethane dries by solvent evaporation plus oxidation, both of which crawl in cool, damp conditions — a coat-a-day product becoming a coat-every-other-day product is how one-week oil jobs sprawl across two in a Puget Sound winter. It’s one more entry in the long list of reasons waterborne wins our water-based vs oil-based comparison, and in the rainy season the gap becomes a chasm. If a project truly calls for oil poly, we’ll say so — and we’ll also say it schedules better in summer.

The Off-Season Advantages Nobody Mentions

Rainy-season refinishing isn’t just viable — it has genuine upsides. Scheduling is the obvious one: summer is the trade’s crush season, booked out by everyone who believes floors can only be sanded in sunshine, while the winter calendar has room to fit around your dates instead of the other way around. The full booking calculus, including how the seasons compare across Western Washington, is in our guide to the best time of year to refinish floors here — the punchline is that “best” has far more to do with your household’s schedule than the forecast.

There’s a practical household angle, too. The 48–72-hour furniture wait and 7-day rug rule are easier to live with in the months when nobody’s hosting a backyard party, and getting floors done in November or February means they’re cured, furnished, and beautiful before the holidays or the spring listing photos — instead of competing with them. Sellers especially: by the time the market wakes in March, the winter-refinished floor is old news to you and brand-new to every buyer.

Seattle Specifics: Old Houses, Real Weather

Seattle’s housing stock adds its own rainy-season texture. The Craftsman-era homes of Ballard, Wallingford, and Beacon Hill are older envelopes — some drafty, some on unconditioned basements or crawlspaces — and their floors, often original Douglas fir, sit closer to the damp than a 2005 Eastside build ever does. None of that disqualifies winter work; it just makes the moisture-meter step genuinely load-bearing. A hundred-year-old fir floor reading normal refinishes beautifully in January. One reading wet gets us looking under the house before we touch it — and that finding is worth having in any month.

Heat is the other old-house question: a home with radiators or a retrofit furnace can absolutely host a winter refinish, as long as it can hold ordinary living temperature through finish days — something we confirm at the estimate rather than discover mid-job. This is routine work for our crews doing hardwood floor refinishing in Seattle: the rainy season is roughly half the year here, and a flooring company that couldn’t deliver from October to May would be a seasonal hobby, not a business. Rainy-Season Refinishing: What Seattle Homeowners Ask

Can hardwood floors really be refinished in winter in Seattle?
Yes, routinely. A heated home provides stable, mildly dry indoor conditions year-round, and waterborne finishes cure off those conditions, not the weather. Our winter jobs run the same 3-to-5-day schedule and produce the same results as summer ones — the season changes procedure slightly, not outcome.
Doesn't the finish need dry weather to cure?
It needs a dry-enough room, which a furnace supplies regardless of the forecast. Heated winter air indoors is actually low in relative humidity. The finish that genuinely struggles in cool, damp conditions is oil-modified polyurethane, which is one of several reasons we run waterborne systems on nearly every floor.
Will you need to open windows while it's raining?
No. Low-VOC waterborne finishes don't require open-window ventilation to dry — air exchange is managed around the coating schedule, indoors. This is very different from the solvent-finish era, when airing the house out was part of the job and weather genuinely interfered.
Is my wood too damp to sand in the rainy season?
Almost certainly not, if the house is heated and dry — but we verify rather than assume. A moisture meter reads the boards before sanding. Normal readings proceed; elevated readings mean something specific — a leak, a wet crawlspace, a basement issue — that's worth finding in any season, before finish seals it in.
Does refinishing cost more or less in the off-season?
Our pricing is the same year-round — $1.99/sq ft for a screen and recoat, $3.99 for a full natural refinish. What the off-season offers is calendar flexibility: winter books far more easily than the summer crush, so you pick dates instead of taking what's left.
Should I run a dehumidifier during or after the work?
During, no — normal heating does the conditioning. Afterward, a dehumidifier only earns its keep in spaces with a real moisture load, like some basements. What matters more is simply keeping the house at ordinary living temperature through the finish days and the first cure week.
Is there any part of refinishing that should wait for summer?
Two honest cases: projects that specifically require oil-modified polyurethane, which dries much faster in warm, dry weather; and floors with an active moisture problem, which need the source fixed and the wood dried before sanding — repairs that are sometimes easier in the dry months. Everything else is season-proof.
When should I book if I want floors done before the holidays?
Work backward from the calendar: 3 to 5 days of work, walkable that evening, furniture at 48 to 72 hours, rugs at 7 days. A refinish finishing by mid-December has you fully set up for hosting. October and November dates make it comfortable — and those weeks are exactly when the schedule is most open.
