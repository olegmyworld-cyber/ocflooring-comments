Where the Missing Dollars Come From

Sanding and finishing 800 square feet takes roughly the same machine hours no matter who does it. So when one quote is half of another, the difference isn’t magic efficiency — it’s scope. The usual places a low number gets built: fewer finish coats (two, sometimes one, instead of a full system), a bargain finish in the bucket, no inter-coat abrasion, rushed sanding grits with steps skipped, no dust containment, no insurance or license overhead, and no warranty priced in. None of these shortcuts is visible on move-in day. Every one of them is visible later — that’s the defining feature of cheap refinishing: it photographs beautifully in week one and fails on a schedule.

One more version worth naming, because it’s common: the “refinish” that’s actually a recoat. A screen and recoat — abrade the old topcoat, apply a fresh one — is a legitimate service we sell ourselves at $1.99/sq ft, and when a floor qualifies, it’s the honest budget option. But it doesn’t remove scratches that reach wood, gray traffic paths, or stains, and a bid that prices recoat scope while promising refinish results will disappoint you by exactly the difference between those two jobs.

Failure Mode #1: The Finish That Wears Out in Two Years

This is the most common one, because finish grade is invisible at handover. A single-component bargain waterborne and a two-component commercial product pour the same, level the same, and look identical for the first year. The difference is wear: the commercial finishes we use are specified for restaurants and gyms, while the $40–60 gallon starts thinning in a family kitchen’s traffic paths in two to four years. When it wears through, you don’t re-buy a topcoat — by the time wear is visible, dirt has usually worked into and under the finish edge, and the fix that actually looks right is a fresh sand and full system.

Run that math on a typical 800 sq ft Redmond main floor. The bargain refinish at $2.50/sq ft costs $2,000. Three years later, the re-do at our $3.99 rate is $3,192 — total $5,192, plus two rounds of furniture moving and living around a job site, to end up where $3,192 spent once would have put you. Failure Mode #2: Sanding Marks You See Forever

Finish failures at least wait a few years. Sanding failures are on display immediately — though often only after the finish goes down, which is the cruel part. A drum sander parked a half-second too long leaves a stop mark: a shallow dip that reads as a dark band across the board. An edger run too aggressively leaves swirl marks in a halo around the room’s perimeter. Skipped grits leave scratch patterns that stain soaks into twice as dark. All of it hides in raw wood and appears the moment finish — and especially stain, which is why our stained refinishes run $6.50/sq ft and get extra sanding care — locks it in permanently. Then it’s there every time low afternoon sun comes through the slider.

The fix is a complete re-sand, and here’s the part that should genuinely bother you: a botched sanding isn’t just money, it’s wood. Every trip to bare wood removes material from a floor that has a finite number of sandings in it — the arithmetic we lay out in how many times a floor can be sanded. A bad refinish spends one of your floor’s remaining lives and gives you nothing for it. This is also the core reason we tell people rental-sander DIY is a false economy — the machine rental is cheap; the sanding it does is not.

Failure Mode #3: Peeling, and Other Ways Prep Fails

The third failure is adhesion. Finish sticks to floors through preparation — clean wood, compatible products, and abrasion between coats so each layer keys into the last. Rush any of that and the film releases: flakes at the high-traffic edges first, then sheets. We wrote up the full diagnosis in why floor finish peels and flakes, and the punchline belongs here: nearly every peeling floor we see traces back to a shortcut — no buffing between coats, finish over residue, or a new product thrown over an old incompatible one to save a sanding. Peeling can’t be spot-fixed, because the problem is under the finish, not in it. The remedy is the full re-sand, again.

This failure mode is also where the missing overhead in a cheap quote stops being abstract. When a floor fails under warranty, we re-do it — that’s what the warranty is, and it’s priced into our rate. When a floor fails with no warranty behind it, the re-do is a brand-new job at full price, paid by you, and the original “savings” fund the first half of it.

How to Vet a Low Quote

A low quote can be legitimate when…

  • It names the finish brand and line in writing — and it’s a real commercial product
  • It states the number of coats and inter-coat abrasion
  • License, insurance, and a written workmanship warranty all exist
  • It’s honestly scoped as a screen and recoat, priced like one
  • The contractor walks the floor before the number, not after

Walk away when…

  • The finish is “commercial grade” with no brand name attached
  • Coat count is vague, or “as many as it needs”
  • No warranty, or a verbal one
  • The bid arrived sight-unseen and never changes with the floor’s condition
  • The price only makes sense if something on this page is missing

Cheap Refinishing Quotes: What Redmond Homeowners Ask Us

How far below market does a quote have to be before I should worry?
Quotes vary legitimately by 20–30 percent with overhead, scheduling, and scope. When a bid is 40–50 percent under the cluster of other quotes, the difference is no longer efficiency — it’s coats, finish grade, insurance, or warranty. Ask the bidder to name which one, in writing.
What questions expose a hollow quote fastest?
Five: Which finish brand and line, exactly? How many coats, and is there abrasion between them? Are you licensed and insured? What does your workmanship warranty cover and for how long? Is this a full sand or a screen and recoat? A solid contractor answers all five without flinching.
Is a $2/sq ft refinish ever real?
As a full sand with a commercial finish system, essentially never — the materials and machine time don’t fit under that number. As a screen and recoat, absolutely: our recoat rate is $1.99/sq ft, and for a floor with intact finish and surface-level wear, it’s the right service. The problem is only when recoat pricing is attached to refinish promises.
How long should a properly done refinish actually last?
With a commercial two-component waterborne and normal family life, expect ten-plus years before a full refinish is on the table, with an optional recoat around year five to seven extending it further. If a refinish visibly wore out in two or three years, the finish grade or coat count was wrong — that pace of failure isn’t normal wear.
The cheap job already happened. Do we have to sand again, or can you recoat over it?
It depends on what failed. Worn-but-adhered finish can sometimes be recoated if wear hasn’t reached wood. Peeling floors can’t — new finish over a releasing layer fails with it. Sanding marks can’t be recoated away either, since they’re in the wood. We check adhesion and wear depth on site before recommending, because recoating over the wrong failure just adds a layer to remove later.
Does a re-do really shorten my floor’s life?
Yes. Every full sanding removes wood, and a floor — especially one sanded several times over the decades — has a finite number left. A refinish that has to be repeated in three years spends two sandings for one result. On older floors with limited wood above the tongue, that’s not an abstract cost.
Why does dust containment show up as a separate line?
Because it’s real equipment and setup time — we charge a flat +$250 for full containment rather than burying it in the rate for everyone. A cheap quote that never mentions dust at all usually means bag-on-machine sanding and a house you’ll be wiping down for weeks. Ask what happens to the dust; the answer is informative.
Are expensive quotes automatically safe, then?
No — a high number can carry the same hollow scope with better margins. Price is a signal, not a guarantee, which is why the five written commitments matter more than the total: products, coats, license and insurance, warranty, and scope. Judge every quote, cheap or dear, on whether those hold up.
