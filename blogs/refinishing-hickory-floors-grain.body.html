What Makes Hickory Different

Three things, stacked. First, raw hardness: at 1,820 Janka, hickory is what tool-handle makers use when they need wood that shrugs off impact — your floor is made of axe handles. Second, the calico coloring: mills blend hickory’s pale sapwood and dark heartwood deliberately, so a single floor swings from cream to coffee, board to board and sometimes within one board. Third — and this is the one that actually fights you — the density isn’t uniform. Hickory’s grain alternates hard and harder in irregular waves, so a sanding machine that cuts steadily through oak gets pushed around by hickory, wanting to skate over the hardest streaks and dig into the merely-hard ones. None of this makes hickory a bad floor. It makes it a floor where the difference between a good crew and a rushed one shows up in raking light.

What That Does to Sanding

Practically: everything takes longer and dulls faster. Abrasive belts that would finish an oak room keep getting swapped out; skipping grits — a classic corner-cut on cheap jobs — leaves scratch patterns hickory’s density won’t let you buff away later. The uneven hardness invites chatter marks (fine washboard ripples from machine vibration) and dished soft streaks, both nearly invisible on a dusty floor and painfully visible under the first coat of finish in evening sun. Our answer is the same one we use on every floor, but earns its keep here: a disciplined grit sequence with no skipped steps, and a Lägler TRIO final pass, which cuts flat across the density changes instead of following them. The result reads as one continuous plane instead of a topographic map. Timeline-wise, plan the normal 3–5 days, with hickory more likely to use the top of the range.

Staining Hickory: Where Dreams Go to Blotch

Here’s the conversation nobody enjoys: the gray or espresso floor you saw online was probably white oak, and hickory will not impersonate it politely. Dense wood takes pigment shallowly and unevenly — the same streak-to-streak density that fights the sander also decides, streak by streak, how much stain soaks in. On top of that, stain doesn’t erase hickory’s two-tone character; a dark stain mutes the contrast but the calico ghost stays visible underneath, and a medium stain can actually amplify it. Water-popping the grain before staining helps pigment take more evenly and deeper, and it’s standard practice for us on dense species — but it narrows the gap, it doesn’t close it. Our honest guidance, which matches our general advice in choosing a stain color: on hickory, go natural or go light, and make any darker decision only after seeing a large sample patch on your actual floor. If a client wants a uniform dark floor above all else, we’ll say plainly that hickory is the wrong canvas for that painting.

The Finish Choice: Keep the Contrast Honest

Hickory’s look is its color range, and the finish decides what happens to it. The clear waterborne systems we use — Bona Traffic HD and Pallmann, 2–3 coats — keep the pale sapwood pale and the dark heartwood dark, which is the whole point of owning the species; the comparison in our water-based vs oil-based guide applies double here, because an ambering oil-modified poly slowly yellows exactly the creamy boards that give hickory its snap, flattening the contrast into a muddier warm blur. There’s also a practical angle for the households that choose hickory in the first place: its hardness makes it a favorite for homes with big dogs, and a tough two-component waterborne is the right armor on top — claws mark the finish long before they dent the wood, and hickory’s busy grain is genuinely good at hiding the little scratches every real household accumulates. A satin or matte sheen helps further; gloss on hickory turns every density wave into a highlight.

The Species, Compressed

Hickory rewards you when…

  • You like the calico contrast and want it kept, not fought
  • Big dogs, kids, and gear traffic are daily reality — 1,820 Janka absorbs it
  • You go natural or light, under a non-yellowing waterborne finish
  • You choose satin or matte so the grain reads as texture, not glare
  • Your crew flattens with a TRIO pass instead of calling the drum sander done

Brace yourself when…

  • You want a uniform gray or espresso floor — hickory resists impersonation
  • The quote is suspiciously cheap — skipped grits show worst on dense wood
  • You expect stain samples on paper to predict this species — insist on floor patches
  • You’re matching new hickory boards into an old floor — sap/heart mix varies by batch
  • You want oil-modified poly — ambering flattens the very contrast you paid for

Hickory Refinishing Questions Sammamish Homeowners Ask Us

Can hickory floors be refinished like oak?
Yes — hickory is solid hardwood and sands and refinishes fully. The difference is in the doing: its 1,820 Janka hardness and uneven density wear out abrasives faster and punish sloppy technique with chatter marks and visible scratch patterns. The process is the same as oak; the margin for shortcuts is much smaller.
Does refinishing hickory cost more?
Not with us — $3.99 per square foot natural, $6.50 with stain, the same rates as oak. Hickory may use an extra day inside the typical 3–5 day window because sanding goes slower and abrasives get changed more often, but the price per square foot doesn't change.
Why does stain look blotchy on hickory?
Density. Stain colors wood by soaking in, and hickory's hardness varies streak by streak, so absorption varies streak by streak — some areas drink pigment, others barely accept it. Water-popping the grain before staining evens this out considerably, but hickory never stains as uniformly as oak. It's the species, not the applicator.
Can you make our hickory floor dark and uniform?
We can make it darker; uniform is the honest sticking point. Dark stain mutes hickory's two-tone contrast but doesn't erase it — the calico pattern remains visible underneath. If a large sample patch on your floor doesn't convince you, a genuinely uniform dark look is better achieved with a different species.
Is hickory good for homes with dogs?
One of the best. At 1,820 Janka it resists denting better than any domestic species, which is why it shows up so often in active Sammamish households. Claws still scratch the finish — finish is plastic, not wood — but hickory's busy grain hides light wear well, and a recoat refreshes the surface long before the wood itself suffers.
Should we go natural or stained on hickory?
Natural, in most cases — it's what the species is for. The cream-and-brown contrast is hickory's whole personality, it hides everyday wear brilliantly, and it costs $2.51/sq ft less than staining. If you want color, stay light and neutral, approve it from a patch on your own floor, and expect character to show through — which, on hickory, is a feature.
What finish do you use on hickory?
Bona Traffic HD or Pallmann waterborne systems, 2 to 3 coats, usually in satin or matte. Waterborne stays clear, so the pale boards stay pale for the life of the finish — an ambering oil poly slowly yellows the light sapwood and washes out the contrast. Walk on it in socks the same evening; furniture back at 48–72 hours.
How often does hickory need refinishing?
Less often than softer floors, if you maintain the finish. The wood almost never wears — it's the finish that dulls and scratches. A screen and recoat at $1.99/sq ft every handful of years, whenever traffic lanes start looking tired, can postpone the next full sand for a very long time.
