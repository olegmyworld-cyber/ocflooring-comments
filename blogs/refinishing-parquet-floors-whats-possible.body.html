What Parquet Is, and Where Bellevue Hides It

Parquet is flooring assembled from small pieces — usually oak “fingers” grouped into squares, each square rotated 90 degrees from its neighbors, glued (sometimes also nailed) to the subfloor. The version common in this area is 60s–80s mosaic parquet: squares of five to seven narrow slats, laid in entries, kitchens, family rooms, and sometimes whole main levels. Bellevue has a lot of it, for a simple reason — so much of the city’s housing stock went up in exactly the decades parquet was popular. We find it in Lake Hills and Newport Hills ramblers, in Eastgate split-levels, and in the older downtown condo buildings, frequently hiding under carpet that went down in the 90s when parquet fell out of fashion.

Under the surface dirt and 40-year-old ambered finish, what matters is construction: solid wood blocks, typically 5/16" thick or better, hold enough material to sand and refinish more than once. That thickness question — how much wood is actually above the glue line — is the first thing we check, the same way we check wear layers on engineered floors.

Why the Grain Direction Changes Everything About Sanding

On a strip floor, all the grain runs one direction, and the big sander runs with it — wood sanded along the grain scratches invisibly. Parquet takes that rule away: whatever direction the machine travels, half the blocks are being sanded across their grain. Run an aggressive drum sander over parquet the way you’d sand a strip floor and every alternate square picks up cross-grain scratches that fill with stain and finish and telegraph forever. This is the single most common way parquet gets ruined, and it’s usually the work of either a rental machine or a crew that treats every floor the same.

The right approach is to neutralize direction. Coarse cuts go down at a diagonal to the pattern, splitting the difference between the two grain directions, with successively finer grits — and the floor is finished with a multi-disc machine whose heads sand in overlapping circles rather than straight lines. That final pass is exactly what the Lägler TRIO is for: it flattens the whole surface without a directional scratch pattern, which on parquet isn’t a luxury but the difference between a floor that looks professionally restored and one that looks scarred. It’s why “do you sand parquet differently?” is a fair interview question for any contractor bidding your floor — and why the cheapest bid on parquet is the most dangerous one.

The Stain Question: Where We’ll Talk You Out of It

Here’s the honest part of the conversation. Wood absorbs stain differently along the grain than across it — end grain and cross grain drink deeper and darker. On a strip floor this variation is subtle. On parquet, where adjacent blocks alternate 90 degrees, a medium or dark stain amplifies the alternation: each square takes the color differently than its neighbor, and the floor goes checkerboard. Some homeowners like a gentle version of that effect; most who asked for “even dark brown floors” do not.

So our standing advice on parquet is natural or lightly toned finish. A clear waterborne keeps oak’s color honest, lets the geometry of the pattern be the design statement, and avoids the blotch lottery entirely — the reasoning overlaps with our general guide to choosing a stain color, but parquet pushes harder toward natural than any strip floor would. When a client truly wants dark parquet, we sample on their actual floor in a closet or corner first, and we keep expectations written down. That sample step is non-negotiable; anyone who quotes you a dark stain on parquet without sampling is selling you a surprise.

Loose Blocks, Missing Fingers, and Other Fixable Problems

Parquet’s age shows in its glue before its wood. Walk a 50-year-old parquet floor and you’ll usually find a few spots that click or feel hollow — blocks whose adhesive has let go. Sanding a loose block is how blocks get destroyed, so re-adhering comes first: individual slats and squares get glued back down (or replaced outright if they’re split), gaps get filled, and only then does sanding start. A missing finger here and there is routine; oak slat stock is still available, and a patched-in piece disappears under the new finish far better than people expect.

Two repair caveats worth knowing. First, parquet over a concrete slab — common in condo buildings — depends entirely on that old adhesive bond, and a floor that’s hollow across large continuous areas may need those sections re-set before it can take the vibration of sanding. Second, parquet with a history of standing water is riskier than strip flooring with the same history, because moisture attacks the glue as well as the wood. Neither is automatically fatal; both belong in the estimate, not discovered mid-job. Our rundown of when wood floors can’t be refinished covers the true disqualifiers.

What’s Possible and What Isn’t, at a Glance

Refinishable: the green lights

  • Solid oak mosaic or block parquet, even under decades of grime
  • Ambered, scratched, or dull finish — that all sands away
  • Scattered loose or hollow blocks (re-glued before sanding)
  • A few damaged fingers or squares — patchable with stock slats
  • Painted or carpet-glue-covered parquet, with extra prep

Not refinishable: the honest nos

  • Thin-veneer engineered parquet — the pattern is a 1–2 mm skin
  • Large continuous areas debonded from the slab or subfloor
  • Blocks warped or delaminated by standing water
  • Floors already sanded to the glue line or tongue
  • Asbestos-era adhesive exposed and friable — needs abatement first, not sanding

Parquet Refinishing: What Bellevue Homeowners Ask

Can old parquet floors really be refinished, or is replacement smarter?
Most solid parquet refinishes very well — the blocks are real oak with enough thickness for multiple sandings. Refinishing runs $3.99 per square foot for a natural finish, a fraction of tearing out a glued-down floor and installing new material. Replacement only wins when the parquet is thin-veneer engineered, extensively debonded, or water-damaged.
How is sanding parquet different from sanding regular hardwood?
Parquet blocks alternate grain direction, so any straight-line machine is sanding half the floor across the grain, which leaves permanent scratches. The correct method uses diagonal passes at finer grits and a multi-disc machine like the Lägler TRIO for the final cut, which sands without a directional pattern.
Can parquet be stained a dark color?
It can, but we usually advise against it. Alternating grain absorbs stain unevenly, so adjacent squares take the color differently and the floor reads checkerboard. If you want dark parquet, insist on a sample on your actual floor first. Natural or lightly toned finishes are where parquet looks best.
Some blocks feel loose or sound hollow. Is that a dealbreaker?
No — scattered loose blocks are normal on 40- to 60-year-old parquet and get re-glued before sanding starts. The concern is large continuous hollow areas, especially over concrete, which may need sections re-set before the floor can be sanded safely.
Can you replace missing or broken pieces of parquet?
Yes. Individual fingers and whole squares can be patched with matching oak slat stock, and after sanding and finishing the repairs blend far better than most homeowners expect. Salvaging spare material from a closet in the same house gives the best match of all.
Is my parquet solid wood or veneer, and how can I tell?
Check a heat register opening, a removed threshold, or a closet corner where you can see edge thickness. Solid parquet shows the same wood through its thickness, typically 5/16 inch or more above the glue. A veneer product shows a thin face layer over plywood or composite. If you can't tell, we check it as part of a free estimate.
How long does refinishing a parquet floor take?
The same 3 to 5 days as comparable strip flooring, with repairs and re-gluing adding time up front on floors that need it. With the waterborne finishes we use, you're walking on it in socks the evening of the final coat, furniture returns at 48 to 72 hours, and rugs wait a week.
Does refinished parquet look dated?
Not finished naturally. Ambered 1970s finish is most of what reads as dated; sanded flat and coated with a clear matte or satin waterborne, oak parquet reads closer to the European pattern floors that are back in style. It has become a feature in Eastside listings more than once.
