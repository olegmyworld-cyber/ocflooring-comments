What’s Actually Under Your Feet

Bungalow builders used a two-tier system: the good wood went where guests would see it, and the affordable wood went everywhere else. In practice that usually means oak — often quarter-sawn, sometimes with a decorative border — in the living and dining rooms, and Douglas fir in bedrooms, hallways, and kitchens. Fir was the Northwest’s local lumber, milled a streetcar ride away, and it went down in wide, tight, vertical-grain boards that would cost a fortune to replicate now. Plenty of smaller bungalows are fir throughout; a few grander ones are all oak.

Telling them apart takes ten seconds once you know the trick: oak has visible pores and flecks in the grain, fir has smooth, straight, closely spaced grain lines like the pages of a closed book. Why it matters: the two woods sand differently, take finish differently, and — since fir is roughly half as hard as oak — live differently underfoot. We covered the fir side in depth in our guide to refinishing fir floors in old Seattle homes.

The Measurement That Decides Everything: Thickness

Every sanding removes wood — roughly a sixteenth of an inch with a full cut — and a bungalow floor may have been sanded two, three, or four times since Calvin Coolidge. Original tongue-and-groove flooring has only so much wood above the tongue, and when that’s gone, the floor is done: sand further and you expose the tongue, and boards start cracking and splintering. So the first thing we do in a century home isn’t aesthetic at all. We find a floor vent, a closet, or a radiator pipe cutout and look at the board in cross-section to see how much meat is left above the tongue.

Most bungalow floors pass this check with room to spare — people over-fear it. But not all, and it’s far better to know before the sander shows up. The full arithmetic of sandings-per-floor is in our post on how many times a wood floor can be sanded, and the honest list of disqualifiers is in when floors can’t be refinished. If your floor is on its last sanding, that’s not a reason to panic — it’s a reason to make this refinish count, with a commercial finish and a recoat-not-resand maintenance plan afterward.

What a Century Leaves Behind

Old floors carry their history, and a refinish has to deal with all of it. The usual inventory in a 1920s Seattle bungalow: face nails working up through the boards (set and filled), black water stains around radiator locations and old plant spots (sanded if shallow, chemically treated or patched if deep), pet stains from generations of pets, gaps between boards that open every winter, patches where walls moved or floor furnaces came out, and paint — always, somehow, paint — around the edges of every room that was ever carpeted.

Two honest expectations. First, gaps: we fill what filler can hold, but a century-old floor moves seasonally, and filler in wide gaps eventually cracks out — some gap is part of the house’s character, and pretending otherwise leads to disappointment. Second, stains: deep black staining goes through the wood, not just the finish, so the fix is board replacement, and matching hundred-year-old fir means salvaged material, not the lumberyard. We keep expectations and options clear at the estimate rather than discovering them together on day two.

Choosing a Finish That Respects Old Wood

Here’s where century homes punish the default choice. Oil-modified poly — the amber finish most of these floors wore for decades — keeps yellowing for years, and on fir, which is already warm-toned, it lands somewhere between pumpkin and traffic cone. Most of the “can you fix what the last refinisher did” calls we get from bungalow owners are exactly this. We finish these floors with clear waterborne systems — Bona and Pallmann, 2–3 coats — which hold the wood’s real color: honey-and-rose fir, sandy quarter-sawn oak. The full argument lives in our water-based vs oil-based comparison.

On stain: oak takes it well, so if you want a darker floor in the front rooms, that’s open. Fir mostly doesn’t — it absorbs stain unevenly and blotches, and its beauty is the grain itself. Our standing advice in a bungalow is natural on the fir, natural or a considered stain on the oak, satin or matte sheen, and let a hundred years of tight grain do the decorating.

Is Your Floor a Candidate?

Great refinish candidates…

  • Boards solid underfoot, wear mostly in the finish and color
  • Thickness check shows wood left above the tongue
  • Stains and damage are localized, not wall-to-wall
  • Original oak or vertical-grain fir — wood you can’t buy new
  • You want the house’s character kept, not covered

Time for a harder conversation…

  • Tongue already exposed from past sandings, boards cracking
  • Spongy or springy spots — that’s structure, not surface
  • Deep pet or water staining across whole rooms
  • Previous refinishes gouged or rippled the floor badly
  • You want a perfectly uniform, new-build look — old floors won’t fake it

Refinishing Century-Home Floors: What Seattle Owners Ask

Can 100-year-old floors really be refinished?
Usually, yes — age itself isn't the problem. What matters is thickness: how much wood remains above the tongue after previous sandings. We check that in a vent or closet before quoting. Old-growth fir and oak that pass the check sand and finish beautifully, often better than modern flooring.
How do I know if my bungalow floors are fir or oak?
Look at the grain. Oak shows visible pores and flecks, often with a cathedral pattern; Douglas fir has smooth, straight, tightly spaced parallel grain lines. Bungalows commonly have oak in the living and dining rooms and fir everywhere else, so you may well have both — and they get treated differently.
What does it cost to refinish floors in a 1920s Seattle home?
The same per-square-foot rates as any refinish: $3.99/sq ft for a full sand with natural finish, $6.50/sq ft with stain. Board repairs and replacements are itemized separately at the estimate, since every century home carries different damage. Most bungalow main floors land between 600 and 1,000 sq ft.
Can you get the black stains out?
Shallow ones, often — they sand out or lighten substantially. Deep black staining from water or pets penetrates the wood itself, and the honest fix is replacing those boards, ideally with salvaged period material so the grain matches. We'll tell you which kind you have before work starts, not after.
Should we fill the gaps between boards?
Selectively. We fill stable gaps during finishing, but a century-old floor moves with the seasons, and filler in wide, active gaps cracks out within a few winters. Some seasonal gapping is simply how a 1920s floor lives. We'd rather set that expectation honestly than sell you a perfection the house can't hold.
Can fir floors be stained darker like oak?
We usually advise against it. Fir absorbs stain unevenly and tends to blotch, and its tight vertical grain is the best thing about it. If you want a darker floor, the oak rooms can take stain well; on the fir, a clear waterborne finish that keeps its natural honey tone is almost always the better-looking, better-aging choice.
How long does the work take, and can we stay in the house?
Typically 3 to 5 days for a bungalow main floor — sanding and repairs first, then 2 to 3 coats of waterborne finish. With the low-VOC systems we use, most families stay in the house, walking on the floors in socks the same evening a coat goes down and moving furniture back at 48 to 72 hours.
Is it worth refinishing instead of replacing with new hardwood?
In a bungalow, almost always. The old-growth wood in these houses is tighter-grained and more stable than modern stock, it matches the house's trim and era, and refinishing at $3.99/sq ft costs a fraction of installing new. Replacement makes sense only when the wood is genuinely used up — and we'll tell you if it is.
