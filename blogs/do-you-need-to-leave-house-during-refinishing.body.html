“Do we need to move out?” is the first question at most estimates — usually asked with a hotel budget already half-calculated. It’s the right question, because the answer used to be yes. In the oil-modified polyurethane era, a refinish meant solvent fumes at 450–550 g/L filling the house for the better part of a week, and staying meant headaches at best. That era is over for anyone who chooses it to be: the commercial waterborne systems we run carry a tiny fraction of the fumes, and the honest inconveniences that remain — noise, blocked floors, a day or two of tiptoeing — are the kind you plan around, not flee from.

The Honest Answer Depends on One Choice: the Finish

Everything downstream follows from the finish chemistry. Waterborne polyurethane carries its solids in water, dries in hours, and smells faintly of, roughly, school glue — noticeable in the room, gone from the house quickly, and low enough in VOCs that we coat occupied homes daily without complaint. Oil-modified poly carries its solids in mineral spirits, and mineral spirits do not negotiate: the odor saturates the house during coating days and lingers as the film cures, which takes about thirty days to complete. We put the full comparison in our water-based vs oil-based guide, but for the stay-or-go question the summary is blunt: choose waterborne and staying home is normal; choose oil and you should plan to be elsewhere for the coating days at minimum — and longer if anyone in the house is pregnant, asthmatic, very young, or a bird. Households in that category should read our low-VOC refinishing guide regardless of finish choice.

What Living Through It Actually Feels Like, Day by Day

Assume a typical 3–5 day waterborne refinish of a main floor. Day one is prep and the start of sanding — the loud part. Floor sanders are industrial machines; from inside the house they’re a sustained vacuum-cleaner-times-three roar through working hours, which matters mostly for home offices and nap schedules. Day two finishes sanding and often starts the finish system; the sound drops to almost nothing. Coating days are quiet — the work is careful and low-drama, and each coat is dry to the touch in hours. Final coat down, you’re walking the floor in socks the same evening, moving furniture back at 48–72 hours, and laying rugs at a week. The Three Real Problems: Noise, Access, and Air

With a low-VOC waterborne system, air is the smallest of the three — so plan around the other two. Noise is concentrated in the sanding day or two, and it’s incompatible with working from home in the next room; most clients schedule those days at the office, and remote workers borrow a coffee shop. Access is the sneaky one: fresh finish is a wall you cannot cross, so the question isn’t “can we be in the house” but “can we reach the kitchen, a bathroom, and our beds without crossing wet floor.” In a two-story home with bedrooms upstairs and hardwood down, the answer is usually yes with planning. In a single-level home where the hallway to the only bathroom is being coated, the answer can be no for a night or two, no matter how clean the air is. Air becomes the deciding factor only with oil-based products — at which point it decides everything.

The Middle Path: Sequencing the Job Room by Room

When a whole main floor needs work but moving out isn’t an option, the answer is usually sequencing: refinish the house in phases so there’s always a livable zone and an open path to kitchen, bathroom, and beds. It stretches the calendar — two phases take longer than one continuous job — but it converts an impossible week into a mildly annoying one, and for families, that trade is almost always worth it. We wrote a full playbook on room-by-room refinishing in an occupied home, including where the phase boundaries should fall so the color and sheen match where the phases meet. It’s the approach behind most of the occupied-home refinishes we run from Seattle to Everett, and it’s the first thing we’ll sketch at an estimate if you tell us moving out is off the table.

You can comfortably stay when…

  • The finish is a low-VOC waterborne system (ours are)
  • Bedrooms and a bathroom stay reachable without crossing wet finish
  • The kitchen — or a decent substitute plan — stays accessible
  • Sanding-day noise can be scheduled around work and naps
  • It’s a one-day screen and recoat — barely a disruption at all

Plan to be out when…

  • The finish is oil-modified poly — at minimum for coating days
  • The job blocks your only bathroom or the route to the bedrooms
  • The whole single-level house is being done in one continuous phase
  • Someone in the house is pregnant, asthmatic, a newborn, or a bird
  • You simply don’t want to hear a sander — a fair reason we respect

Staying Home During Refinishing: What Homeowners Ask Us

Is it safe to sleep in the house the night a coat goes down?
With the waterborne systems we use, yes — most of our clients do exactly that, the same evening. VOC content around 65 g/L means the smell is mild near the work and fades within hours. With oil-based polyurethane we recommend sleeping elsewhere on coating days, and longer for sensitive households.
Can my kids and pets stay during the job?
With waterborne finishes, yes, with two caveats: keep everyone off fresh finish until it's walkable, and keep pets' paws off it a day longer than people — claws and enthusiasm are hard on a young film. For birds, which have unusually sensitive respiratory systems, we recommend boarding them for the coating days even with low-VOC products.
How loud is floor sanding, really?
Loud — think several vacuum cleaners running at once, sustained through working hours on the sanding day or two. It's not a problem for an empty house and it's genuinely disruptive for a home office or a napping toddler in the next room. Most clients simply plan those specific days away and come home to quiet coating days.
Can we use the kitchen during a main-floor refinish?
Depends on the sequencing. If the kitchen floor itself is being done, you'll lose it for the coating-and-cure window — a couple of days of takeout or the garage microwave. If the kitchen is reachable without crossing fresh finish, it stays open the whole time. This is exactly the kind of thing we map out room by room at the estimate.
How long before we can walk on the floors?
Socks the same evening with waterborne — the film is dry to the touch in hours. Shoes and furniture at 48–72 hours, rugs and full normal life at about a week. Oil-based stretches all of it: a day-plus before careful sock traffic and a month of curing before rugs.
Does a screen and recoat require the same disruption?
No — it's the easy version. A screen and recoat is one day, light abrasion instead of full sanding, so the noise is a fraction of a refinish, and one coat of waterborne finish that's walkable the same evening. At $1.99/sq ft it's also the cheapest professional service we offer. If your finish is dull but intact, it's likely all you need.
What if we'd rather just leave for the whole job?
Also completely reasonable, and it simplifies scheduling — we can run the phases continuously instead of sequencing around your household. Some clients pair a refinish with a vacation and come home to done floors; just build in the 48–72 hour furniture window at the end. We'll happily coordinate key handoff and daily photo updates.
Do you charge differently for occupied-home jobs?
The rates are the same — $3.99/sq ft natural refinish, $1.99 screen and recoat. What changes is the calendar: sequencing an occupied home into phases can add a day or two versus running the job straight through. We'll quote both schedules at the estimate so you can price the convenience honestly.
