Quick answer

Over the first year, factory-finished flooring wins — it installs faster, skips the dust and the finish schedule, and its aluminum-oxide coating is genuinely harder than anything we can apply in your house. Over twenty years, site-finished wins, and it isn’t close: a floor sanded and sealed in place is one flat, continuous surface that can be recoated for $1.99/sq ft whenever it dulls and fully refinished for $3.99/sq ft when it’s worn — while a factory finish that wears through usually can’t be economically renewed at all. Which trade-off is right depends on how long you’re staying. Here’s the long-term reality as we see it on Renton floors every week.

What the Two Actually Are

A factory-finished (prefinished) floor arrives with its finish already baked on: each board is coated on a production line, usually with multiple UV-cured layers loaded with aluminum-oxide particles — essentially the same mineral as sandpaper grit — then micro-beveled along the edges so slight height differences between boards don’t catch your eye or your sock. Install it, and you’re walking on it the same afternoon.

A site-finished floor goes down as raw wood. We sand the whole installation flat as one plane — our Lägler TRIO does the final pass — then seal it in place with two to three coats of commercial waterborne finish, Bona or Pallmann in our case. The finish flows over boards and the seams between them, curing into one continuous film across the entire room. That single difference — coated boards versus a coated floor — drives almost everything below.

Day One Through Year One: Factory Finish Wins the Sprint

Honesty first, because prefinished flooring’s advantages are real. There is no sanding in your house, which means no dust management, no finish smell, and no days of workers and drying schedules — the job is done when the last board clicks home. The coating itself is applied in a climate-controlled factory at thicknesses and cure conditions we genuinely cannot reproduce on site, and aluminum oxide is harder than any site-applied polyurethane; in straight abrasion resistance, the factory coat wins against anything in our van. For a rental turning over in two weeks or a family that can’t absorb any disruption, those are legitimate deciding factors — the same math we walk through in refinishing vs installing new prefinished hardwood.

Site finishing costs you the schedule instead: after installation the floor still needs sanding and finishing — typically three to five days all in — and you live around that process once. In exchange you get a floor with no bevels, a perfectly flat surface, any stain color you want rather than the manufacturer’s catalog, and seams that are sealed shut. On day one, you mostly notice the look. The seams you notice later.

Year Ten: Bevels, Seams, and the Recoat Problem

Those micro-bevels between prefinished boards are V-shaped grooves running the length of every board, and they work like gutters: dirt, mop water, and pet accidents collect in them, and because the finish was applied per-board at the factory, the seam at the bottom of each groove is not sealed. Spills reach end-grain and board edges in a way they physically can’t on a site-finished floor, where the finish film bridges every joint. Ten years of kitchen life shows up as darkened bevel lines that no cleaner reaches — you’ve seen this floor, even if you didn’t know what you were looking at.

The deeper problem is maintenance. A site-finished floor has a scheduled, cheap renewal path: when the finish dulls and scratches — years before wood is exposed — a screen and recoat abrades the topcoat and lays a fresh one for $1.99/sq ft in a day. Do that every five to seven years and the sand-everything day may never come. Aluminum-oxide finishes mostly close that path: the coating is too hard to abrade uniformly, so new finish struggles to bond, and most manufacturers void the warranty if you try. Prefinished floors are also disproportionately engineered rather than solid — and refinishing an engineered board depends on wear-layer thickness, a lottery we cover in can prefinished floors be refinished and in our solid vs engineered guide. So the tough factory coat protects you right up until it wears through or scratches white — and then the answer is often not “renew it” but “replace it,” at full new-floor prices, assuming the color and profile are even still made.

The Renton Long Game

Renton is a natural lab for this comparison because its housing stock brackets both eras. The Boeing-era ramblers from the 1940s through the ’60s — thick around the Highlands, Kennydale, and Talbot Hill — were built on site-finished solid red oak, and a remarkable share of those floors are still in service on their third or fourth finish. Sixty-plus years from one material purchase is the site-finished long game working exactly as designed, and it’s most of our hardwood floor refinishing work in Renton. Meanwhile, the townhomes and infill built from the 2000s on came with prefinished — often engineered — floors, and the first wave of those is now hitting year fifteen to twenty. When we get called to look at them, the bevels are dark, the traffic paths are scratched white, and the conversation is about replacement budgets, not refinishing quotes.

The rental market sharpens the same point. Renton landlords who inherited old site-finished oak can reset a unit between tenants — a recoat in a day, or a full refinish inside a week, per our guide to refinishing rental hardwood between tenants — and the floor genuinely comes back. A prefinished floor in the same unit absorbs tenant after tenant with no reset button, looking a little worse each turnover until the replacement year arrives all at once.

The Decision, Compressed

Site-finished earns it when…

  • You plan to own the home well past year ten
  • You want a specific stain color or a true flat, seamless look
  • The house already has solid wood elsewhere to match
  • You value a cheap, repeatable renewal path over peak day-one hardness
  • Kitchens and entries are involved — sealed seams matter where water lands

Factory-finished makes sense when…

  • You need the space back the same day — no dust, smell, or schedule
  • It’s a rental, flip, or shorter-horizon home where year 15 is someone else’s problem
  • The install is somewhere sanding equipment fights you — condo rules, tight timelines
  • A catalog color you like exists and matching future repairs doesn’t worry you
  • Maximum scratch resistance in the first decade is the priority

Site-Finished vs Factory-Finished: What Renton Homeowners Ask

Is factory finish really harder than site-applied finish?
In straight abrasion resistance, yes — UV-cured aluminum-oxide coatings are harder than any polyurethane applied in a home, including the commercial waterborne systems we use. The catch is what happens when it does wear or scratch: an aluminum-oxide finish generally can’t be renewed in place, while a site-applied finish is designed to be.
Can a factory-finished floor ever be refinished?
Sometimes. Solid prefinished boards with enough thickness can be sanded — the aluminum oxide comes off with aggressive early grits, and sanding also removes the bevels, effectively converting it to a site-finished floor. Engineered prefinished boards depend entirely on wear-layer thickness; many have too little wood above the plywood core to sand safely. It has to be assessed board-in-hand.
Why can’t you just recoat a prefinished floor when it dulls?
Recoating requires abrading the old finish so the new coat can bond, and aluminum oxide resists exactly that abrasion. Adhesion over an under-abraded factory coat is unreliable, peeling is the failure mode, and most manufacturers exclude recoated floors from warranty. Some newer prefinished lines advertise recoatability — check the specific product’s documentation, not the category’s reputation.
Do site-finished and factory-finished floors cost the same to install?
Close to it. Materials run $4–$10+ per square foot for both, and install labor is $3–$4.25/sq ft in our pricing. Site finishing adds the sanding and finishing scope and a few days of schedule; prefinished adds a small premium per board for the factory coating. The real cost difference is the maintenance path over the following decades.
What are the grooves between my prefinished boards, and why do they get dark?
Micro-bevels — small chamfers milled into each board edge so the assembled floor forgives tiny height differences. They form V-grooves that collect dirt and mop water, and since the factory finish stops at each board, the seam at the bottom isn’t sealed. Dark bevel lines are trapped grime and moisture staining, and no cleaner fully reaches them.
Our Renton rambler has original oak — is that site-finished?
Almost certainly. Homes built here from the 1940s through the 1970s got unfinished solid red oak, sanded and sealed in place. That’s good news: those floors are usually 3/4-inch stock with plenty of life left, and they respond beautifully to refinishing even after decades under carpet.
Which one is better for resale?
Buyers respond to condition more than construction. A freshly refinished site-finished floor photographs and shows better than a mid-life prefinished floor with darkened bevels — and the refinish costs $3.99/sq ft against replacement money. If you’re selling a home with tired prefinished floors, your options are narrower, which is itself the long-term argument.
If factory finish can’t be recoated, what maintenance does it have?
Cleaning, felt pads, rugs at entries — prevention only. That’s the honest limitation: there is no intermediate renewal step between ‘clean it carefully’ and ‘replace it.’ A site-finished floor adds the middle options — recoat at $1.99/sq ft, refinish at $3.99/sq ft — which is exactly what a floor needs in decades two and three.
