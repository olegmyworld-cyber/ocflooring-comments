Why Stairs Can’t Be Priced Like Floors

A stair tread is a small piece of wood with a complicated life. It has a rounded bullnose at the front, two ends that die into stringers or skirt boards, sometimes balusters mounted right on the tread, and a riser meeting it at the back — all of which has to be sanded to bare, clean wood without rounding over edges or chewing into the parts that stay painted. There’s no machine that does this. It’s an edger where the edger fits, an orbital sander where it doesn’t, and a carbide scraper and sandpaper in the corners where nothing else reaches.

That’s why per-square-foot pricing breaks on stairs. If we charged stairs at floor rates, we’d be paying you for the privilege of doing the slowest work in the house; if we charged floors at stair rates, our floor prices would be absurd. Per-tread pricing keeps both honest: you can count your steps from the bottom of the staircase and know the number before we ever show up.

What $55–$75 a Tread Covers — and What Moves the Number

The base price covers sanding the tread and nosing down to bare wood, hand-detailing the edges and corners, and building the same 2–3 coat Bona or Pallmann waterborne system we use on floors. Where you land in the range comes down to a few things we can see at the estimate. Clean, open treads between painted skirt boards with a natural (no stain) finish sit at the bottom. Treads with balusters mounted on them — every spindle is an obstacle to hand-sand around — move up. So does staining, because stain on stairs is applied and wiped by hand, step by step, and has to match the floors exactly. And so does a staircase coming out from under carpet, for reasons that get their own section below.

One boundary worth stating plainly: the per-tread price is for the tread — the part you walk on. Risers and stringers in most Bellevue homes are painted white, and they stay painted; we mask and protect them. If your risers are natural wood and you want them sanded and refinished too, that’s additional hand work we price separately at the estimate, not something quietly folded into the tread number.

The Carpet Question

A lot of Bellevue staircases were carpeted the day the house was built, and what’s underneath is a genuine unknown until the carpet comes up. Sometimes it’s solid oak treads that were always meant to be an option — those refinish beautifully, and pulling the carpet is one of the best value moves in the house. Sometimes it’s builder-grade material that was never supposed to see daylight: finger-jointed stock, paint drips, or treads that are structurally fine but cosmetically rough.

Either way, carpet leaves a mess behind — hundreds of staples, tack strips nailed into the tread ends, and rows of small holes where they lived. We pull the hardware and sand out what sanding can fix, and the tack-strip holes at the edges get filled; they end up unobtrusive, not invisible. That cleanup work is exactly what pushes a tread from $55 toward $75. And when the material under the carpet genuinely isn’t worth finishing, we’ll say so and talk about new treads instead — our stair installation services cover that path, and it’s a better outcome than paying us to polish wood that was never meant to show.

Bellevue Staircases: The Two-Story Entry Problem

Bellevue’s housing stock is dominated by the 1980s through 2000s — two-story homes with red oak floors on the main level and an oak-capped staircase rising straight out of the entry. That staircase is the most visible fifty square feet in the house: it’s what guests see from the front door, and it’s what betrays the floors’ age first, because stair finish takes concentrated traffic on a narrow path. We refinish a lot of Bellevue main floors where the floors themselves could have waited another year — it was the staircase that made the phone ring.

The practical advice that follows: do the stairs and the floors in the same job. Same sanding sequence, same stain batch, same finish system, same sheen — that’s how the staircase reads as continuous with the floor instead of almost-matching it. Sequenced separately, even the same products applied months apart can read slightly different, because floors amber and wear from day one. Living in the House While the Stairs Are Out

Stairs raise a logistics question floors don’t: they’re usually the only route to the bedrooms. Two things make this workable. First, waterborne finish takes sock traffic in hours, not days — so coats that go down in the morning are walkable by evening, and nobody sleeps on the couch. Second, when timing is tight we can coat alternating treads in two passes, so there’s always a (slightly silly-looking) path upstairs. The full walk-on schedule is the same as for floors — we covered it in how long before you can walk on refinished floors — and the rule that surprises people is the runner rule: carpet runners and tread pads wait 7 days, the same as rugs, while the finish fully cures.

On schedule: a staircase adds roughly a day of hand work inside a typical 3–5 day whole-home refinish, and a stairs-plus-landing project on its own runs about two days including coats. One honest caveat — our minimum job size is 500 sq ft, and a staircase alone doesn’t get there, which is why nearly all our stair work happens alongside at least one floor. Refinish or Replace the Treads?

Refinish the existing treads when…

  • There’s solid oak under the carpet or the existing finish
  • The damage is surface-level: wear, dullness, staple and tack-strip holes
  • You’re refinishing the adjoining floors and want a true match
  • The treads are full thickness — no previous aggressive sandings
  • The budget question is $715–$975, not thousands

Talk about new treads when…

  • The carpet comes up on builder-grade or finger-jointed material
  • Nosings are cracked, split, or broken — that’s structural, not cosmetic
  • There’s water damage or deep gouges past what sanding removes
  • You want a different species or plank look than the existing stairs
  • A previous refinish already sanded the treads thin

Stair Refinishing Costs: What Bellevue Homeowners Ask

How much does it cost to refinish a typical staircase?
Count your treads and multiply by $55 to $75. A standard 13-tread Bellevue staircase runs $715 to $975 — the low end for clean treads with a natural finish, the high end for stairs coming out from under carpet or being stained to match floors. Landings are priced as floor area on top of that.
Why are stairs priced per tread instead of per square foot?
Because none of the machines that make floor work efficient fit on a stair. Each tread is sanded with an edger, an orbital, and hand tools, working around nosings, stringers, and balusters. A four-square-foot tread can take as long as a hundred square feet of open floor, so per-square-foot pricing would misprice one job or the other.
Do risers and stringers count as treads?
No. The per-tread price covers the tread and its nosing — the horizontal parts you walk on. Painted risers and stringers stay painted and get masked and protected. If you have natural-wood risers you want refinished, that is extra hand work we quote separately at the estimate.
Can you refinish just the stairs without doing any floors?
Usually not on their own — our minimum job size is 500 sq ft, and a staircase alone doesn't reach it, so nearly all our stair work rides along with a floor refinish. If stairs are genuinely all you need, call us anyway and we'll tell you honestly whether it pencils out.
Can we still get upstairs during the work?
Yes, with planning. Waterborne finish takes sock traffic within hours, so morning coats are walkable by evening. When the schedule is tight we can coat alternating treads in two passes so a path upstairs always exists. The days to plan around are the sanding days, when the staircase is genuinely a workspace.
Will refinished stairs match our refinished floors?
That's exactly why we do them in the same job: same sanding, same stain batch, same finish, same sheen, applied the same week. Done together, the match is as close as wood allows. Stairs refinished months apart from the floors can read slightly different, because finish ambers and wears from day one.
When can we put a carpet runner back on the stairs?
Seven days after the final coat — the same waiting period as rugs on floors. The finish is walkable in hours but needs about a week to fully cure, and a runner laid early traps solvents and can imprint the finish. Cure first, then dress the stairs however you like.
What if the wood under our stair carpet turns out to be bad?
It happens — some builders capped stairs with material that was never meant to show. We'll tell you at the estimate or the moment the carpet comes up, and the honest fix is new treads rather than refinishing wood that can't look good. That's an installation conversation, and we do that work too.
