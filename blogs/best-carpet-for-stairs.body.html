Stairs Are the Hardest Job a Carpet Ever Gets

A bedroom carpet spreads its traffic across a whole room, and most of the room never gets walked on at all. A staircase gets every trip — every school morning, every laundry basket, every dog launch — landed on the same few inches of every tread, in the same spot, thousands of times a year. And unlike flat floor, stair carpet is bent over a hard 90-degree nose edge its entire life, which means the pile at the nose is stretched open and taking impact at the same time.

That is why the same carpet that looks fine in a bedroom after fifteen years can look exhausted on the stairs in five. It is not a defect and it is not bad cleaning — it is physics, and it is exactly why we tell people to shop for the staircase first and the bedrooms second. In Everett this matters more than most places: the split-levels around Silver Lake and the two-story homes off Broadway all run their whole daily life up and down one staircase. It is the main artery of the house, and it deserves the best fiber in the budget.

Density Beats Softness — Do the Bend Test

Showrooms sell softness, because softness is what your palm notices in the aisle. Stairs do not care what your palm thinks. What survives on a staircase is density — how much fiber is packed into each square inch — because dense pile supports itself and springs back, while soft, tall, sparse pile crushes flat at the nose and stays flat.

You do not need a spec sheet to check this. Take the sample and bend it backwards over your knuckles, the way it will bend over a stair nose. If the pile yawns open and you can see the backing grinning through, that carpet will show its backing on your stair noses within a few years. If the pile stays tight and you see only fiber, it is a stair candidate. We do this test with clients on their own staircase during the estimate — it takes ten seconds per sample and it eliminates half the board immediately, including some expensive ones.

Fiber: Nylon Earns Its Premium Here

On flat bedroom floor there is a real argument for saving money with polyester. On stairs there mostly is not. Nylon's defining trait is resilience — it springs back after being crushed — and the stair nose is the most crushed spot in the house. This is the one place in the home where we tell almost everyone to pay the fiber premium, even if the bedrooms upstairs get something softer and cheaper. Mixed-fiber jobs like that are normal for us, and they put the money exactly where the wear is.

Waterfall vs Wrapped: The Install Detail That Shows

There are two ways to carpet a tread, and you can spot the difference from across the room. Waterfall lets the carpet flow over the nose and drop straight down to the next tread in one continuous piece — faster to install, slightly softer look, and the standard on most modern staircases. Wrapped (you will also hear cap-and-band or Hollywood style) tucks the carpet tight under the nose so the profile of each step shows crisply — more tailored, more labor per tread, and worth it on a staircase that is a visual centerpiece.

Neither is wrong. What is wrong is carpet that is not fastened firmly at the nose — that is where loose installation shows up first as a soft, shifting edge underfoot, and a shifting edge on a staircase is a safety problem, not a cosmetic one. It is also why stairs are priced per tread: each one is cut, stretched, wrapped and fastened individually. A quote that buries a staircase inside a square-foot total is worth a follow-up question before you sign it.

Safety: the Part We Bring Up Even When You Do Not

Stairs are where household falls happen, and carpet choice moves the odds. Short, dense pile over a firm, thinner pad gives your foot a stable, predictable surface. Deep plush over a soft, thick pad feels luxurious in the hallway and mushy at the stair nose — the edge compresses underfoot and your footing moves. Of the three pad grades we carry, the right stair answer is usually firmer and thinner than what we would put in the bedroom the stairs lead to, and we will say so at the estimate even though it is the cheaper option.

Carpet also simply out-grips hard flooring on stairs. If your stairs are bare wood and you love them that way, a runner gets you traction while the wood still shows — our stair services page covers the options, and if the treads themselves are worn, stair refinishing is priced per step for the same reason stair carpet is priced per tread: each one is its own small job.

Stair Carpet: What Homeowners Ask Us

What is the best carpet for stairs?
A dense, low-pile nylon cut pile over a firm pad. Density is what survives the concentrated traffic and the bend over the stair nose — verify it by bending the sample backwards over your hand; if the backing shows through the pile, it will show on your stairs within a few years. Softness is the wrong thing to shop for on a staircase.
Is nylon really worth the premium over polyester on stairs?
On stairs, yes — this is the one place we say it almost without exception. Nylon springs back after being crushed, and the stair nose is the most crushed spot in the house. Polyester mats under that kind of concentrated traffic. Many of our jobs run nylon on the stairs and hallway and a softer, cheaper fiber in the bedrooms, which puts the money where the wear is.
Should stair carpet be low pile or high pile?
Low. Short, dense pile keeps its shape at the nose, gives your foot a stable surface, and hides wear longest. Deep plush looks generous on day one, then crushes flat exactly where every foot lands — and over a soft pad it makes the stair edge feel mushy underfoot, which is a footing problem, not just a looks problem.
What is the difference between waterfall and wrapped stair carpet?
Waterfall flows the carpet over the nose and straight down to the next tread — faster, softer-looking, the modern default. Wrapped (cap-and-band, Hollywood) tucks the carpet tight under the nose so each step’s profile shows — more tailored and a bit more labor per tread. Both are correct when fastened firmly at the nose; we price both in writing and you pick.
How are stairs priced for carpet?
Per tread, not by square footage. Every tread is cut, stretched, wrapped and fastened individually, so a staircase carries far more labor per square foot than an open room. A quote that hides the staircase inside a square-foot total is the one to ask questions about — you cannot tell what the stairs are costing you or how they will be done.
What pad should go under stair carpet?
A firm, thinner grade — usually firmer than what we would recommend in the bedrooms. Thick, soft pad lets the carpet flex and shift at the nose, which accelerates wear and makes footing less predictable. We bring all three pad grades to the estimate so you can feel the difference, and on stairs we will usually point you at the cheaper, firmer one.
Is carpet on stairs safer than bare wood?
Generally yes — carpet grips socks and paws far better than any finished wood, and it is the surface we recommend for households with young kids, older adults, or dogs that treat the staircase as a runway. If you want the wood to show, a properly fastened runner is the middle path: traction in the walking line, wood at the edges.
How long does carpet on stairs last?
Honestly: less time than the same carpet anywhere else in the house, because stairs concentrate the whole household’s traffic onto a few square feet. A dense nylon over a firm pad holds up the longest. When stair carpet does give out at the noses, you can often re-carpet just the staircase — it is priced per tread anyway, and it does not require redoing the bedrooms.
